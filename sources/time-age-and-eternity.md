# Time, age, eternity, and the presence of the past

**Status:** focused research addition, 2026-10-12. Supports a compact addition to v2 rather than a separate philosophy-of-time essay. Primary passages and specified scholarly excerpts were checked; no single ancient or modern conception of time is assumed. Hebrew and Syriac vocabulary is coordinated with [the Semitic witnesses dossier](semitic-gospel-witnesses.md).

## TA-01 — Aiōn and English eon

**Merriam-Webster, “eon,” Word History.** [Entry](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/eon). **Status:** etymology checked.

English *eon/aeon* comes through Late Latin *aeōn* from Greek *aiōn*. The dictionary’s Greek senses include “lifetime, long period of time, age.” Later geological usage does not define a New Testament sentence. Nor does the word’s history prove that an age-expression must be finite. Greek *aiōn* and Semitic *ʿolam/ʿālam* are not one etymological root; their association is a matter of translation and overlapping usage.

For Mark’s actual idiom, retain [L-01](greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-01) and [L-06–L-07](greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-06): εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, *eis ton aiōna*, is singular, not “for all eons.” With the negative it ordinarily conveys never. Matthew 12:32 excludes both this age and the coming one. Neither duration’s emotional resonance nor a contemplative philosophy supplies an omitted endpoint.

## TA-02 — Different horizons of Hebrew ʿolam

**1 Samuel 1:22,28; Psalm 90:2–4.** Hebrew text and JPS 1917 English at Mechon Mamre: [1 Samuel 1](https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt08a01.htm), [Psalm 90](https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt2690.htm). **Status:** passages checked.

In Samuel, Hannah says that her son will remain there עַד־עוֹלָם, *ʿad-ʿolam*, “forever”; verse 28 describes the commitment through the length of his life. This is a contextual lifetime horizon, not a universal dictionary rule. The expression is *ʿad-ʿolam*, not exactly the reconstructed *le-ʿolam*.

Psalm 90 gives a contrasting use: מֵעוֹלָם עַד־עוֹלָם, *me-ʿolam ʿad-ʿolam*, “from everlasting to everlasting,” predicated of God. Its thousand-years/day comparison also questions the adequacy of a single human temporal measure, without saying time is unreal or every long duration eventually ends.

**Use:** ask whose life, what covenant, which horizon, and what kind of assertion a temporal expression concerns. **Limit:** finite contextual uses do not cancel the strongest unbounded uses; enduring divine life and lasting judgment cannot be assumed to mean different things merely because we prefer different outcomes.

## TA-03 — Passover: the past enters present practice

**Exodus 13:8 and Mishnah Pesachim 10:5.**

- [Exodus 13, Hebrew and JPS 1917](https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt0213.htm)
- [Mishnah Pesachim, Hebrew; chapter 10](https://mechon-mamre.org/b/h/h23.htm)
- [Alternate Pesachim 10:5 display](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Pesachim.10.5)
- **Status:** Mechon Mamre passages checked; the Sefaria page was not consistently readable in this pass.

Exodus’s festival instruction places deliverance in the first person: what God did for me in coming out of Egypt. The Mishnah requires each generation to regard itself as having come out of Egypt. The draft’s short rendering is supplied from the Hebrew, not copied from an unconsulted English edition.

This is stronger evidence for a lived relation among times than a deduction from the root of *ʿolam*: a remembered deliverance shapes present identity, gratitude, and practice. The later rabbinic formulation is not evidence that every first-century Jewish teacher held one philosophy of time. It also remains Jewish covenantal memory, not merely an interchangeable example of Buddhist metaphysics.

## TA-04 — Augustine’s three presents

**Augustine of Hippo, Confessions XI.20.26; compare XI.11.13, XI.28.37–38.** [NPNF English translation, New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/110111.htm). **Status:** named passages and surrounding argument checked in English; Latin not collated. The quoted three-presents phrase appears in the draft rather than being duplicated here.

Augustine distinguishes present memory of the past, present awareness, and present expectation of the future. His discussion of reciting a psalm makes the relation concrete: expectation becomes voiced attention and then memory. He also distinguishes divine eternity from passing created time. The discussion is late antique, not a report of Jesus’s personal temporal experience.

**Contribution:** a Christian interpreter can hold a complex account of lived time without abandoning chronology or final judgment. The same Augustine’s final-impenitence theology in [R-03](interpretation-history.md#r-03) is therefore an important counterweight to any inference that temporal subtlety entails universal restoration.

## TA-05 — Eternal life as present and future

**John 5:24–29; compare Mark 10:30 and Matthew 12:32.** [John 5:24–29, NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+5%3A24-29&version=NIV), [John 5, NABRE and notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/john/5). **Status:** John’s passage checked in both named displays; Mark and Matthew are established comparisons in the project’s biblical dossier.

John speaks of already having eternal life and having crossed from death to life, yet also speaks of future resurrection and judgment. The draft quotes the short NIV present-tense expression. Eternal life therefore has participatory and relational significance, not merely the length of an interval. This does not mean its duration is unimportant, or that one may replace every eschatological statement with an inward experience.

**Question:** how do an age to come and its present participation belong together? This is a question for Johannine and Synoptic interpretation, not a proof that Mark’s denial of forgiveness is temporary.

## TA-06 — Method: chronology and lived time are not mutually exclusive

**Henri Blocher, “Yesterday, Today, Forever: Time, Times, Eternity in Biblical Perspective,” Tyndale Bulletin 52.2 (2001), 183–202; DOI 10.53751/001c.30273.** [Journal PDF](https://www.tyndalebulletin.org/article/30273-yesterday-today-forever-time-times-eternity-in-biblical-perspective.pdf).

**Checked:** selected opening, lexical, and methodological pages, especially 185–190; printed pp.186–187 inspected visually. Not every theological conclusion or cited work independently reviewed.

Blocher challenges a neat Hebrew/Greek temporal opposition and notes biblical concern with chronology alongside varied literary presentations of time. He distinguishes lexical senses from larger claims about eternity. This is a methodological control, not a source whose whole theology the project adopts. His treatment also notes perpetuity-language outside the *aiōn* family: “forever” does not always represent one ancient word.

**James Barr, Biblical Words for Time** (1962; revised edition 1969). [Reprint publisher](https://wipfandstock.com/9781608990238/biblical-words-for-time/); [1969 catalog/access lead](https://archive.org/details/biblicalwordsfor0000barr_v3f5). **Status:** bibliography and contents lead, not full text read. Obtain the sections on *aiōn*, *chronos/kairos*, and Hebrew temporal vocabulary before proposing a comprehensive semantic history.

**Research distinction:** (1) lexical duration, (2) the force and scope of a particular assertion, (3) a speaker’s larger model of time, and (4) the present practice of memory and anticipation are related questions, not interchangeable ones. A word cannot by itself establish a culture’s philosophy. Neither antiquity nor modernity has one uniform temporal consciousness.

## TA-07 — Relation to the Plum Village comparison

Use the already checked [PV-04](plum-village/time-and-transformation.md#pv-04), Sister Annabel Laity’s account of the three times, and [ND-02–ND-03](nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-02--historical-and-ultimate-neither-monism-nor-separation). No new recording has been reviewed in this pass.

Passover’s enacted memory, Augustine’s three presents, John’s present eternal life, and the Plum Village interbeing of the three times can be studied together. Their differences remain substantial. The comparison concerns how a life carries its past and opens toward a future, not historical dependence or a claim that Jesus taught a Buddhist philosophy of time.

The contribution to Mark is therefore not a hidden translation of “forever.” It is an expanded question about how consequences persist, how a person participates in them now, and how transformation may change that relation. A new beginning does not make another person’s suffering unreal or cancel the responsibility to repair what can be repaired.
