## HT-01

**Mark 1–3: narrative setting and the reversal of blasphemy language.**

- [Mark 1, NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+1&version=NIV)
- [Mark 2, NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+2&version=NIV)
- [Mark 3, NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+3&version=NIV)
- [Mark 3, NABRE and notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/mark/3)
- **Status:** relevant chapter texts checked in the initial pass. This is a literary reading, not a reconstruction of an independently established historical sequence.
- **Key excerpts used in the draft:** the announcement of the kingdom in 1:15, the accusation of blasphemy in 2:7, and the summons and accusation in 3:22–30. See the draft for the brief quotations rather than duplicating them here.

**Narrative sequence:** Spirit, proclamation, exorcism, healing, and solitary prayer in chapter 1; forgiveness and disputed authority, meals with sinners, fasting, and Sabbath in chapter 2; Sabbath healing, anger and grief, crowds, the Twelve, family intervention, and the accusation from Jerusalem in chapter 3. The source-of-power dispute does not open chapter 3; the Sabbath-healing episode does.

**Interpretive use:** blasphemy is first attributed to Jesus for forgiving; the later warning brings blasphemy language to an accusation against his work. Different scenes and speakers must not be silently merged. The opening breadth of forgiveness may intensify the exception rhetorically without becoming empty language whose sole function is contrast.

**Limits:** Mark records no reply between 3:23–27 and 3:28–29. Rising urgency is a reading of the sequence, not a demonstrated failed reasoning attempt followed by a separately recorded escalation. Mark 3:23 introduces parabolic speech; 3:29 is a severe saying within the address, not necessarily itself a parable. No cited verse says the scribes previously pronounced someone eternally unforgivable. Do not invent their words or treat the author’s imaginative paraphrase as a recovered exchange.

**Human descriptors:** the essay now prefers scribes, teachers of the law, those questioning Jesus, or those making the accusation to a standing label of opponents. This does not deny explicit conflict in the narrative, including Mark 3:6; it avoids treating every person’s identity or motive as settled by the conflict. “Encounter,” “exchange,” and “dispute” can replace the technical genre term “controversy” in ordinary exposition.

## HT-02

**Jesus’s human life and the compatibility of humanity with divinity.**

- [Luke 2:52, NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+2%3A52&version=NIV)
- [John 4:6, NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+4%3A6&version=NIV)
- [Catechism of the Catholic Church, §§456–483, official Vatican edition](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1J.HTM)
- **Status:** biblical wording and the relevant Catechism paragraphs checked in the initial pass. The Catechism is a primary witness to Catholic teaching, not proof of every possible Christology.
- **Locator:** §§464, 467, 470–474. The human-mind and human-heart sentence in §470 quotes *Gaudium et spes* 22; identify that nested attribution.
- **Brief excerpt, §464:** “true God and true man”. The draft also uses short portions of §470.

Luke depicts growth; John depicts fatigue. Catholic teaching affirms genuine human nature while retaining divine personhood, uniqueness, and sinlessness. Paragraph 472 addresses knowledge exercised under historical conditions; §§473–474 immediately qualify that discussion through divine knowledge. Do not isolate §472 as an endorsement of arbitrary doctrinal error or collapse Catholic incarnation into the description “an inspired person.”

**Use:** invite closer attention to human speech and encounter without making denial of divinity a condition of that attention. The essay need not settle every Christian account of inspiration and incarnation.

## HT-03

**John’s unique Son, children of God, and the limits of the proposed reframe.**

- [John 3:16, Greek text](https://www.greekbible.com/john/3/16)
- [John 3:16, NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+3%3A16&version=NIV)
- [John 1:12–14, NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+1%3A12-14&version=NIV)
- [William D. Mounce, *monogenēs* entry and concordance](https://www.billmounce.com/greek-dictionary/monogenes)
- **Status:** Greek clause, named English excerpts, and dictionary entry checked in the initial pass. No full history of the only-begotten controversy or original-language patristic collation completed.
- **Dictionary excerpt:** “one and only, unique”. The entry also records older only-begotten/only-born glosses; a debate about generation should not be resolved from a root alone.

**Greek:** τὸν υἱὸν τὸν μονογενῆ.

**Romanization:** *ton huion ton monogenē*.

**Literal:** τὸν υἱὸν, the son; τὸν μονογενῆ, the only/unique one (the second article marks the attributive adjective).

**Closest English:** “the one and only Son” / “the only Son”; traditional “the only begotten Son.” These alternatives belong to translation and interpretation history; their theological uses are not necessarily identical.

**Contextual limit:** the referent in John 3:16 is Jesus. John 1:12 describes those who receive him and believe in his name as becoming children of God. It does not simply assert that everyone already has Jesus’s same unique status. Changing the referent to every person is not a literal translation of *monogenēs*.

**Constructive use:** “each person loved as if an only child” is an image of wholehearted, non-expendable care. John 3:16’s reference to love for the world gives a theological point of contact. The image is not demonstrated as the historical source, likely original intention, or literal meaning of John’s expression. This clarification preserves the author’s contemplative direction without making an unsupported origin claim.

## HT-04

**The mother’s only child and care beyond the favored circle.**

- [*Discourse on Love*, Plum Village; Mettā Sutta, Sutta Nipāta 1.8](https://plumvillage.org/library/sutras/discourse-on-love)
- **Translation:** the page explicitly attributes the English to Thích Nhất Hạnh. Its commentary pointer is *Teachings on Love* (Parallax Press, 1998).
- [Matthew 5:44–45, NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+5%3A44-45&version=NIV)
- **Status:** written translation rechecked on 2026-10-11; Gospel passage checked in the initial pass. No Pali collation or full-commentary review claimed.
- **Quoted image:** the short mother/only-child phrase appears in the draft; the following instruction extends care to all beings. The draft follows the translation’s wording.

**Important distinction:** the sutta instructs the practitioner to cultivate boundless care. It is not a statement about a creator God loving every being as an only child. Matthew joins enemy-love with the Father’s provision for evil and good. Bringing these teachings together does not establish a shared historical source or erase their religious differences.

**Use:** move from incomparable care for one child toward refusing to treat other lives as expendable. This does not excuse harm, remove boundaries, or guarantee restoration of relationships. Keep the comparison compact rather than opening a separate theology of sonship.

The publisher presents the book as TNH’s commentary on Linji and describes disruptive teaching methods alongside direct realization. Aaron’s comparison with a Zen teacher remains a useful framing question. Before attributing a particular saying, intention, or successful effect to Linji, obtain the text and its locator. The comparison does not require a claim about Buddhist influence on Jesus.

## HT-06

**Editorial analysis: words for the condition and limits of provocative teaching.**

This record is project analysis, not ancient terminology or a scholarly diagnosis.

- **Clinging to certainty** emphasizes how someone holds a view; this is the main descriptive phrase in the revised draft.
- **Closed-heartedness** can describe a relational effect, but must not become an accusation against doubt, trauma, or a protective boundary.
- **Dogmatism** names the doctrinal form of attachment. It is not a synonym for every doctrine or firm conviction.
- **Hardening of heart** is a biblical comparison whose application to disciples prevents an outsider-only reading. It is not lexically identical to blasphemy.
- **Fanaticism** is stronger and more accusatory. Do not use it merely because a person is zealous, disagrees, or holds a traditional interpretation.

A broad promise followed by an exception can be rhetorically forceful without being a formal contradiction. Koan-like attention does not prove that the exception is temporary or that a future condition will change. Distinguish what a saying asserts, the response it may seek, and the response actually recorded.

**After `8dd280b`:** distinguish authentic warning about real danger from coercive use of a claimed danger. Urgency or discomfort does not establish coercion. Claimed benevolence does not establish truth or benefit. The Third Training includes compassionate dialogue as well as freedom of thought; [ND-09](nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-09--truthful-beneficial-and-timely-speech) supplies a specific early Buddhist comparison concerning true, beneficial, timely but unwelcome speech.

## HT-07

**The Greek Gospel and the limits of “transcript.”**

- [SBL Greek New Testament, official edition information](https://sblgnt.com/)
- [Bart D. Ehrman, “Mark as Peter’s Scribe,” author response dated 3 June 2013, 11:09 pm](https://ehrmanblog.org/mark-as-peters-scribe/)
- [USCCB introduction to Mark](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/mark/0)
- **Status:** official SBL edition information, the scholar’s own reply, and the introduction checked on 2026-10-11. The reply, not the surrounding reader speculation, is the source for Greek composition.
- **Brief excerpt from Ehrman’s reply:** “The Gospel of Mark was definitely composed originally in Greek”.

The essay uses the more measured formulation that the Gospel is generally understood to have been composed in Greek. This does not identify its author conclusively or establish the original language of each remembered saying. Greek composition and the earlier languages of Jesus and his hearers are different questions. A modern critical edition is not an original manuscript or a verbatim transcript of the encounter.

**Full-verse control:** the complete Greek of Mark 3:28–30 was compared with the existing [B-02](biblical-texts.md#b-02) record and the Online Greek Bible’s [3:28](https://www.greekbible.com/mark/3/28), [3:29](https://www.greekbible.com/mark/3/29), and [3:30](https://www.greekbible.com/mark/3/30) displays. The Online Greek Bible’s English at 3:29 does not reliably match its displayed noun, so the draft does not copy that English.

The SBL site identifies Michael W. Holmes as editor and states the CC BY 4.0 license. Complete verse text is now visible in ordinary Markdown, followed by romanization, literal English, and contextual English explanation. “All will be forgiven” is an excerpt within verse 28, not the full verse. Literal “into the age” does not translate the idiom’s force better than ordinary “never.”

## HT-08

**John Chrysostom’s time and place.**

- [Benedict XVI, General Audience, “Saint John Chrysostom (1),” 19 September 2007](https://www.vatican.va/content/benedict-xvi/en/audiences/2007/documents/hf_ben-xvi_aud_20070919.html)
- **Locator:** paragraphs on birth in Antioch, Syria; priestly ministry there; appointment to Constantinople; and the 386–397 preaching period.
- **Status:** biographical paragraphs checked on 2026-10-11. This supplies the concise identification in the draft, not an independent dating of every homily.

The Vatican account places his birth around 349 and priestly ministry in Antioch from 386 until his appointment to Constantinople. “A late-fourth-century preacher in Antioch, in Roman Syria” identifies the setting without requiring unfamiliar modern or ecclesiastical titles. The interpretation itself remains cited to [R-02](interpretation-history.md#r-02), *Homily 41 on Matthew*, §§1 and 5. The biography’s appreciative assessment is not adopted as a substitute for examining the homily’s polemic.
