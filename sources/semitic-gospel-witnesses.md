# Aramaic and Hebrew: witnesses, language settings, and study reconstructions

**Status:** research deposit, 2026-10-12, following the Aramaic discussion and Aaron’s Hebrew/time questions. This collects the preceding conversation’s sources and distinguishes newly checked texts from retained leads. No original Aramaic or Hebrew text of the Mark 3 encounter has been recovered. Syriac translations, reconstructed speech, and the critical Greek text are different kinds of evidence.

**Main-text use:** a compact Syriac paragraph in §3. Hebrew experiments remain here rather than adding another full language apparatus to the essay. For time, see [Time, age, and eternity](time-age-and-eternity.md).

## AS-01 — Aramaic in its different settings

**Comprehensive Aramaic Lexicon, Hebrew Union College, “The Aramaic Language.”**

- [Institutional overview](https://cal.huc.edu/aramaic_language.html)
- **Checked:** written overview, especially Palestinian Aramaic, the Dead Sea Scrolls, Christian Palestinian Aramaic, and Syriac.
- **Brief excerpt:** “fragments of Aramaic speech are imbedded in the Greek of the New Testament”.

The overview distinguishes varieties and periods. Qumran Aramaic is important comparative evidence for the language of first-century Palestinian Jews. Syriac developed as a literary language associated with Edessa; it is not simply Jesus’s Galilean dialect under another name. Christian Palestinian Aramaic translations are later witnesses in a different variety. The overview’s broad account of Hebrew’s retreat should be read alongside the more detailed debate in AS-02.

**Primary Gospel control:** [Mark 5:41](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/mark/5) preserves *talitha koum* within Greek narration. A preserved Aramaic expression is not the same evidence as a complete later Gospel translation.

## AS-02 — How much Hebrew or Greek did Jesus speak?

**Steven E. Fassberg, “Which Semitic Language Did Jesus and Other Contemporary Jews Speak?” Catholic Biblical Quarterly 74.2 (2012), 263–280.**

- [Hebrew University publication record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/which-semitic-language-did-jesus-and-other-contemporary-jews-spea-2/)
- [Article, university-hosted PDF](https://elearning.unito.it/scienzeumanistiche/mod/resource/view.php?id=80339)
- [JSTOR record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/43727847)
- **Checked:** introduction, principal Hebrew-evidence discussion, and conclusion; conclusion visually checked on printed p.280. Not every cited work independently examined.
- **Brief excerpt, conclusion:** “converse naturally in Hebrew”.

Fassberg argues that Jesus’s Aramaic is secure and that the evidence for living Hebrew makes his knowledge of Hebrew highly likely. This is a specialist’s argued conclusion, not evidence identifying the language of Mark 3. Written documents, oral-language inference, regional differences, and Jesus’s individual competence must remain distinguishable.

**Stanley E. Porter, “Jesus and the Use of Greek: A Response to Maurice Casey,” Bulletin for Biblical Research 10.1 (2000).** [Article access point](https://www.galaxie.com/article/bbr10-1-05). **Status:** opening discussion checked; full subscription text not read. Porter argues for multilingualism and a greater role for Greek. This complements, rather than settles, the Semitic-language debate.

**Randall Buth, “Language Use in the First Century: Spoken Hebrew in a Trilingual Society in the Time of Jesus,” Journal of Translation and Textlinguistics 5.4 (1992), 298–312.** [Author’s article, republished](https://www.jerusalemperspective.com/31004/). **Status:** relevant discussion checked. The webpage’s recent posting is not the article’s original publication date. Buth argues more strongly for spoken Hebrew in Jewish teaching; do not call this the unanimous position.

**Israel Antiquities Authority, “Languages and Scripts.”** [Dead Sea Scrolls overview](https://www.deadseascrolls.org.il/learn-about-the-scrolls/languages-and-scripts). **Status:** institutional description checked. Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek manuscripts document multilingual literary activity, not the language of every individual conversation.

## AS-03 — The Old Syriac Gospels and Peshitta

**Andreas Juckel, “Old Syriac Version,” Gorgias Encyclopedic Dictionary of the Syriac Heritage, hosted by Beth Mardutho.** [Entry](https://gedsh.bethmardutho.org/Old-Syriac-Version). **Status:** entry checked.

The origins of the translation are disputed, with early attempts commonly placed in the third century. The Sinaitic manuscript is dated to the fourth/fifth century; the Curetonian to the fifth. Translation dates are not manuscript dates. Importantly, Curetonian Mark is almost entirely lost, apart from 16:17–20; it is not an available witness to Mark 3:29. Sinaitic Mark includes the relevant passage. The versions relate to Greek textual transmission, not a demonstrated original Galilean Gospel.

**“Peshitta,” Gorgias Encyclopedic Dictionary of the Syriac Heritage / Beth Mardutho.** [Entry](https://gedsh.bethmardutho.org/Peshitta). **Status:** opening and New Testament discussion checked. **Brief excerpt:** “became the standard version around 400”. The Old Testament and New Testament Peshitta have different translation histories; an account of the OT’s Hebrew basis must not be transferred to the Gospels.

## AS-04 — Peshitta Mark 3:28–30: checked wording

**Dukhrana Biblical Research, Peshitta text from the British and Foreign Bible Society edition; morphological data from George A. Kiraz’s SEDRA.**

- [Mark 3:28](https://www.dukhrana.com/peshitta/analyze_verse.php?font=Serto+Batnan&size=125%25&source=&verse=Mark+3%3A28)
- [Mark 3:29](https://www.dukhrana.com/peshitta/analyze_verse.php?font=Serto+Batnan&size=125%25&source=&verse=Mark+3%3A29)
- [Mark 3:30](https://www.dukhrana.com/peshitta/analyze_verse.php?font=Serto+Batnan&size=125%25&source=&verse=Mark+3%3A30)
- **Checked:** displayed Syriac, selected morphology, and the accompanying named English translations. This is not manuscript-image collation. Romanizations below are approximate conventional Syriac, not reconstructed first-century pronunciation.
- **2026-10-12 review and revision:** Mark 3:29 was rechecked in the linked Dukhrana display. This edit aligns the main-text ending with the project rendering below, “judgment that is forever”; it adds no new claim about the Old Syriac reading or the history of the variant.

| Attested expression | Romanization | Project literal English |
|---|---|---|
| ܪܘܚܐ ܕܩܘܕܫܐ | *rūḥā d-qudšā* | Spirit of holiness |
| ܠܝܬ ܠܗ ܫܘܒܩܢܐ ܠܥܠܡ | *layt leh šubqānā l-ʿālam* | There is no forgiveness for him forever |
| ܠܕܝܢܐ ܕܠܥܠܡ | *l-dīnā da-l-ʿālam* | to judgment that is forever |
| ܪܘܚܐ ܛܢܦܬܐ | *rūḥā ṭanpṯā* | an impure spirit |

The Peshitta preserves the emphatic temporal denial and ends with **judgment that is forever**, whereas the essay’s critical Greek reads **eternal sin**. This English wording keeps the noun difference visible without softening the temporal force. The translation does not independently establish whether the difference reflects its Greek exemplar, a translation choice, or revision. *Rūḥā* means spirit here; holiness and impurity are the contrasting descriptions. Holiness is not merely another word for ritual purity, and neither every impure state nor physical illness is thereby a moral fault.

Verse 28 uses the sin vocabulary *ḥṭāhē* and a form of the forgiveness root *š-b-q*. Verse 30 expresses the accusation as an impure spirit being in Jesus. These remain religious claims in the narrative, not merely descriptions of mental attitudes.

## AS-05 — Lexical records from the earlier discussion

**Comprehensive Aramaic Lexicon, Hebrew Union College.** Relevant entries reopened; lexical ranges are contextual possibilities, not cumulative meanings of every occurrence.

- [šwbqn: forgiveness/remission](https://cal.huc.edu/oneentry.php?cits=all&lemma=%24wbqn+N). The wider release vocabulary does not prove that Mark concerns self-release rather than divine forgiveness.
- [ʿlm: time, world, and temporal expressions](https://cal.huc.edu/oneentry.php?cits=all&lemma=%28lm+N). The expression *l-ʿālam* has an established forever-use. Choosing “age” mechanically does not establish an endpoint.
- [ḥwb: debt, obligation, guilt/sin](https://cal.huc.edu/oneentry.php?cits=all&lemma=xwb+N). This noun is not the Peshitta’s noun for the sins in 3:28. Choosing it in a retroversion would foreground a metaphor; that choice would itself require justification.

## AS-07 — Temple setting: ritual language is not every conversation

**Mishnah Sotah 7:1–2.** [Hebrew text, Mechon Mamre](https://mechon-mamre.org/b/h/h35.htm). **Status:** relevant Hebrew read. **Brief excerpt:** בלשון הקודש, *bi-leshon ha-qodesh*, “in the sacred language” (project rendering).

The text distinguishes recitations allowed in any language from those assigned to Hebrew, including priestly and high-priestly blessings. The Mishnah’s compilation is later than Jesus, conventionally around 200 CE; it preserves traditions, not a transcript of Temple conversation in the 30s. Ritual prescriptions do not establish that every teacher or visitor always conversed in Hebrew.

**Luke 2:41–52.** [Text](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/luke/2). **Status:** passage checked. Luke depicts the twelve-year-old Jesus listening and asking questions among Temple teachers. The story does not identify their language or document a subsequent course of formal Temple education. Mark 3 concerns teachers arriving from Jerusalem; it is not itself a Temple dialogue.

## AS-08 — Hebrew vocabulary already present in Jewish scripture

**Primary Hebrew texts, Mechon Mamre:** [Psalm 130:4](https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt26d0.htm), [Isaiah 63:10–11](https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt1063.htm), [Zechariah 13:2](https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt2313.htm). **Status:** named passages checked.

- Psalm 130 uses הַסְּלִיחָה, *ha-seliḥah*, forgiveness. Hebrew *s-l-ḥ* and Syriac *š-b-q* are not the same root, although they can convey corresponding meanings here.
- Isaiah speaks of רוּחַ קָדְשׁוֹ, *ruaḥ qodsho*, “his holy spirit,” in an account of rebellion, divine grief, and severe response. It offers an older scriptural vocabulary, not proof of a verbatim source for Mark.
- Zechariah names רוּחַ הַטֻּמְאָה, *ruaḥ ha-ṭumʾah*, “the spirit of impurity.” Its larger account includes severe treatment of false prophecy. The phrase must not be detached into an exclusively gentle psychology.

Hebrew *ruaḥ* and Syriac *rūḥā* belong to the same Semitic word-family. Their contextual sense here is spirit; their broader range includes breath and wind. A shared root does not establish identical theology or recover the original language of Mark 3.

## AS-09 — A Hebrew study back-translation, not recovered speech

**Status:** assistant-composed comparative exercise. No first-century manuscript is being quoted. This is readable Hebrew employing older vocabulary; it is not certified as a specific first-century dialect, register, or pronunciation. Only selected clauses are rendered, not all three verses.

**Mark 3:28, compressed sense**

כל החטאים והגידופים של בני האדם ייסלחו להם

*Kol ha-ḥaṭaʾim ve-ha-giddufim shel bene ha-ʾadam yissalḥu lahem.*

“All the sins and revilings of human beings will be forgiven them.”

**Mark 3:29, central clause only**

אבל המגדף את רוח הקודש אין לו סליחה לעולם

*Aval ha-megaddef ʾet ruaḥ ha-qodesh, ʾen lo seliḥah le-ʿolam.*

“But the one who reviles the Spirit of holiness has no forgiveness forever.” Literally, *ʾen lo*: “there is not for him”; *le-ʿolam*: a conventional forever-expression. The clause does not include the Greek sentence’s final phrase about an eternal sin.

**Mark 3:30**

כי היו אומרים: רוח טמאה בו

*Ki hayu ʾomrim: ruaḥ ṭemeʾah bo.*

“Because they were saying: an impure spirit is in him.”

No definite claim about Jesus’s exact words follows from how easily the meaning can be expressed in Hebrew. The root choices, word order, tense/aspect, and formal register need expert review. Modern Hebrew New Testament translations, including Delitzsch’s nineteenth-century version displayed at [Mark 3:29](https://biblehub.com/text/mark/3-29.htm), are comparisons, not ancient witnesses.

**Previous Syriac experiment:** the chat replaced the attested Peshitta ending about judgment with a constructed sin-phrase to match the critical Greek. That hybrid was explicitly conjectural. It must never be relabeled a Peshitta quotation or a recovered Galilean saying. Preserve the distinction between an attested translation and a proposed back-translation rather than circulating the hybrid without its warning.
