## L-01

**Robert G. Bratcher and Eugene A. Nida, Mark translation handbook; online extract, “Translation commentary on Mark 3:29–3:30,” UBS material hosted by TIPs.**

- [Relevant extract](https://tips.translation.bible/story/translation-commentary-on-mark-329-330/)
- **Status:** relevant extract checked, not the complete handbook or manuscript apparatus. Confirm the exact print edition when a formal bibliography is prepared.
- **Brief quotation:** “In this passage the phrase means ‘eternally,’ ‘forever’.”

**Use:** the handbook treats the negative age-idiom as strong non-forgiveness; discusses aphesis, liability, the explanatory imperfect in verse 30, and the preferred “sin” reading over “judgment.” It explains that liability language here names what one is guilty of, not a specified punishment.

**Limit:** This does not mean the passage has no judicial dimension. “The noun is sin rather than punishment” and “no judgment is implied” are different assertions. The handbook also does not supply an explicit “while unrepentant” condition.

**Question:** What further assumptions must be added to derive final impenitence, irreversible hardening, or ultimate restoration?

## L-02

**William D. Mounce, online Greek dictionary, hamartēma.**

- [Entry](https://www.billmounce.com/greek-dictionary/hamartema)
- **Status:** entry checked; a convenient lexical aid, not a historical corpus by itself.
- **Short excerpt:** “sin, wrongdoing”.

**Greek:** ἁμάρτημα — *hamartēma*.

**Lexical gloss:** a sin, fault, wrongdoing; the form in Mark 3:29 is ἁμαρτήματος, *hamartēmatos*, “of a sin.”

**English choices:** “sin” is natural in Mark; “wrongdoing” is possible; “offense” can be a gloss but may add unwanted modern criminal associations. The word is not restricted by its etymological relationship to missing or failing. It does not itself establish that every sin is only an innocent perceptual error.

**To study:** distinguish hamartēma from hamartia and hamartanō without exaggerating a rigid separation. Check classical, Septuagint, and early Christian examples in LSJ, BDAG, and a Septuagint lexicon.

## L-03

**William D. Mounce, online Greek dictionary, aphesis.**

- [Entry](https://www.billmounce.com/greek-dictionary/aphesis)
- **Status:** entry and displayed biblical usages checked.
- **Short excerpt:** “forgiveness, pardon, release, cancellation of a debt”.

**Greek:** ἄφεσις — *aphesis*; Mark uses accusative ἄφεσιν, *aphesin*.

**Lexical gloss:** forgiveness/remission; release. **English choices:** “forgiveness” or “remission” in a sin context, “release” as an illuminating broader lexical comparison.

**Limits:** Luke 4:18 illustrates release language, but does not prove that every occurrence means liberation from a psychological condition. Mark’s lack of an explicitly named divine subject in “does not have forgiveness” cannot establish that God has no active role. Economic, ritual, relational, and judicial associations need context-specific study.

## L-04

**William D. Mounce, online Greek dictionary, enochos.**

- [Entry](https://www.billmounce.com/greek-dictionary/enochos)
- **Status:** entry and displayed usages checked.
- **Short excerpt:** “subject to; guilty, liable for”.

**Greek:** ἔνοχος — *enochos*.

**Lexical gloss:** subject to, liable, answerable, guilty. **English in Mark:** “guilty of an eternal sin” or “answerable for an eternal sin.”

**Important control:** Mark 14:64 uses this vocabulary in relation to death; Matthew 5:21 relates it to judgment. Legal resonance is not a late English invention. The noun or complement matters. The phrase does not denote merely a feeling of guilt, and etymological “held in” should not replace established contextual usage.

## L-05

**William D. Mounce, online Greek dictionary, blasphēmeō.**

- [Entry](https://www.billmounce.com/greek-dictionary/blasphemeo)
- **Status:** entry checked.
- **Short excerpt:** “to defame, revile, slander”.

**Greek:** βλασφημέω — *blasphēmeō*; Mark 3:29 has βλασφημήσῃ, *blasphēmēsē*.

**Lexical gloss:** malign/revile/blaspheme. **English alternatives:** “blasphemes against,” “maligns,” “slanders.” Not confined to accidental profanity.

**Grammar:** the Markan form is aorist subjunctive in an indefinite relative construction. It does not encode “keeps continually blaspheming,” nor prove a single momentary act is intended. Narrative and theological interpretation are still required. The imperfect elegon in 3:30 depicts their saying, but does not insert a condition of future pardon.

## L-06

**William D. Mounce, online Greek dictionary, aiōn.**

- [Entry and biblical examples](https://www.billmounce.com/greek-dictionary/aion)
- **Status:** entry and displayed examples checked.

**Greek:** αἰών — *aiōn*; εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα — *eis ton aiōna*.

**Segmented gloss of the phrase:** eis, into/unto; ton aiōna, the age. **Ordinary English in a negative idiom:** “ever/forever,” hence “never” with the negative.

**Controls:** Mark 11:14 uses the phrase about fruit from the fig tree; Mark 10:30 speaks of the age to come. Compare the distinct explicit “until the completion of the age” wording in Matthew 28:20. No philosophy of time follows solely from choosing the dictionary gloss “age.”

**To test:** claims about exhaustive versus rhetorically emphatic age-language against actual Jewish and Greek usage. Read Barr before adopting a Hebrew-time/Greek-time or chronos/kairos opposition.

## L-07

**William D. Mounce, online Greek dictionary, aiōnios.**

- [Entry](https://www.billmounce.com/greek-dictionary/aionios)
- **Status:** entry consulted; detailed corpus comparison remains open.

**Greek:** αἰώνιος — *aiōnios*; αἰωνίου ἁμαρτήματος — *aiōniou hamartēmatos*.

**Segmented gloss:** aiōniou, eternal/everlasting (genitive); hamartēmatos, sin (genitive). **Natural English:** “of an eternal sin.” “Age-enduring” is a marked study alternative, not a demonstrated finite duration here.

**Question:** How does an adjective modifying sin relate to guilt, continuing action, or consequences? Do not automatically replace the noun “sin” with “punishment,” but do not infer absence of judgment from the distinction.

## L-08

**Biblical comparisons: debt, sin, and release. Added in the flow-and-evidence pass following author revision `d6e1bf6`.**

**Responsible texts and editions:** Matthew 6:12 and Luke 11:4; Luke 4:18 and 24:47. NIV (2011), Biblica, as displayed by BibleGateway; Greek clauses checked in the Online Greek Bible. These are neighboring textual uses, not a completed history of Greek or Jewish concepts.

- [Luke 4:18, Greek](https://www.greekbible.com/luke/4/18)
- [Luke 24:47, Greek](https://www.greekbible.com/luke/24/47)

**Status:** the three NIV passages and Luke 11:4’s footnote were read directly; the two Greek Luke verses were checked. No critical apparatus, Hebrew source collation, or dependence model was examined in this pass. The web displays’ adjacent English is not used as the source of the NIV quotations.

**Brief excerpts:** Matthew asks to “forgive us our debts”; Luke begins “Forgive us our sins.” Luke 4:18 includes “set the oppressed free.” These excerpts anchor the discussion in v2 §3; they should not become interchangeable definitions.

**Luke 11:4 detail:** the NIV main text translates the second clause using sin-language; its footnote identifies the underlying Greek as indebtedness. The draft’s reference to forgiving those indebted to us is a paraphrase of that Greek-text note, not an unmarked quotation of the NIV main text. Retain this distinction in later revisions.

**Aphesis comparison:** Luke 4:18 contains both ἄφεσιν (*aphesin*, release) for captives and ἐν ἀφέσει (*en aphesei*, in release) with the oppressed. Luke 24:47 uses ἄφεσιν ἁμαρτιῶν (*aphesin hamartiōn*, forgiveness/remission of sins). In ordinary English these contexts support release from oppression and forgiveness of sins respectively. The shared noun provides a comparison; it does not establish that the two situations are identical or that all its meanings operate in Mark 3:29.

**Use and limits:** debt and sin imagery coexist within the Gospel tradition. This gives a concrete reason not to import one modern model into every occurrence. It does not prove a chronology from innocent error to crime, date either prayer’s origin, or determine Mark’s account of guilt and punishment. The larger Hebrew/Septuagint and historical-semantic inquiry remains open.

## Methodological reading priorities

[Gary Anderson](modern-scholarship.md#s-02) offers a history of sin’s metaphors; [James Barr](modern-scholarship.md#s-03) is important for resisting etymological and time-language shortcuts. Add Hebrew/Aramaic and Septuagint controls, early Greek usage, Latin reception, and modern English conceptual comparisons. These layers may interact; they do not form a demonstrated one-way progression from nonlegal to legal thought.

Modern psychological forgiveness is a separate comparator, not the original definition of aphesis. Likewise, forgiveness, reconciliation, absolution, restored trust, and removal of consequences must not be treated as synonyms.
