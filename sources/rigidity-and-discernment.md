# Rigidity, discernment, and the responsibility of interpretation

**Status:** first research dossier, 2026-10-10. Supports the revised introduction and new section 4 of the working draft. Textual parallels, a constructive hypothesis, and a historical application are distinct kinds of evidence. This is not a completed scholarly treatment of hardening in Mark.

## Working distinction

Rigidity here means not strong conviction or commitment to a tradition, but an unwillingness to let a judgment be questioned by evidence, another person's experience, or the suffering associated with one's actions. This is the essay's definition, not a translation of blasphemy. The proposed connection is that a settled judgment can turn the means of correction into an enemy. Mark's language about hardening gives this proposal a narrative foothold; it does not prove that every kind of hardening is the unforgivable sin.

## RD-01 — Hardness before the blasphemy controversy

**Mark 3:1–6, especially 3:4–5.**

- [USCCB chapter: text and notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/mark/3)
- [Greek of Mark 3:5](https://www.greekbible.com/mark/3/5)
- **Status:** narrative and Greek phrase checked.

**Greek:** συλλυπούμενος ἐπὶ τῇ πωρώσει τῆς καρδίας αὐτῶν.

**Romanization:** syllypoumenos epi tē pōrōsei tēs kardias autōn.

**Segmented gloss:** συλλυπούμενος, grieving; ἐπὶ τῇ πωρώσει, at the hardness/hardening; τῆς καρδίας, of the heart; αὐτῶν, of them/their.

**Study rendering:** “grieving at the hardness of their heart.”

**Natural alternatives:** “grieved at their hardness of heart”; “grieving over their hardened hearts.” The Greek has singular “heart” with a plural possessive; natural English may use a plural.

Jesus asks about doing good and saving life on the Sabbath. The opponents' silence is followed by the narrator's description of anger and grief at their hardness. Healing proceeds. This supplies a nearby narrative association between hardening and the response to restoration.

**Limit:** 3:5 and 3:29 are different sayings. Neither verse explicitly equates every instance of hardening with blasphemy against the Spirit. Do not reconstruct the historical opponents' private motives from the narrator's evaluation. The emotional language also matters: Jesus is portrayed as both angry and grieved, not as detached from human conflict.

## RD-02 — The disciples are not exempt

**Mark 6:52; 8:14–21.**

- [Mark 6:45–52 in a USCCB reading](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/010726.cfm)
- [Mark 8, text and notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/mark/8)
- [Greek of 8:17](https://www.greekbible.com/mark/8/17)
- **Status:** 6:52 checked in the reading; 8:14–21 and the Greek of 8:17 checked.

Mark connects the disciples' failure to understand the loaves with hardened hearts. Jesus later questions their hardened hearts, failed perception, and memory. The story continues to address and teach them.

**Use:** hardening is an insider danger, not a trait assigned to Jewish opponents and absent from Christians. Continued teaching raises a question about responsiveness, but does not prove that every hardened person ultimately changes. Do not identify all these disciples as having committed Mark 3:29's precise sin.

## RD-03 — Claimed sight and remaining sin

**John 9:1–3, 39–41.**

- [Greek of 9:41](https://www.greekbible.com/john/9/41)
- [USCCB chapter](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/john/9)
- **Status:** Greek of 9:41 and the indexed English ending checked; direct chapter access was intermittent. See the previously checked [B-12](biblical-texts.md#b-12) for the chapter's opening and context.

John connects an assertion of sight with sin's remaining. This is a particularly close canonical parallel for asking whether certainty can obstruct recognition. It is not independent proof of Mark's intended meaning.

**Limits:** retain John's own strong judgment language. The chapter's opening rejects a simple inference from physical blindness to the person's or parents' sin. Do not turn disability, trauma, lack of knowledge, or honest doubt into culpable refusal. Jewish people who do not accept Christian claims are not thereby an instance of the essay's rigidity hypothesis.

## RD-04 — Profession, claimed power, and conduct

**Matthew 7:1–5, 15–27.**

- [USCCB chapter and notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/matthew/7)
- **Status:** text and notes checked.

Jesus distinguishes invocation of his name from doing the Father's will. Those rejected claim prophecy, exorcism, and powerful deeds: the warning is not only against people who speak but do absolutely nothing. The surrounding passages concern fruits, self-examination, and putting teaching into practice.

**Important complication:** this passage prevents “an impressive healing claim proves divine authorization” from becoming a sufficient test. The actions and life of the claimant still require examination. The text does not oppose belief to conduct; it opposes professed allegiance and religious performance to a life that fails to do God's will.

## RD-05 — Hunger, imprisonment, recognition, and judgment

**Matthew 25:31–46.**

- [USCCB chapter and interpretive notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/matthew/25)
- **Status:** text and notes checked.

The scene joins response to hunger, thirst, estrangement, nakedness, sickness, and imprisonment with response to the king. Both groups are surprised. This supports an inquiry into recognition enacted through care, not a claim that every omission is deliberate ideological rigidity.

**Two required qualifications:** (1) the identity of the least brothers is disputed: all vulnerable people, disciples, or particularly vulnerable emissaries. The USCCB note favors the missionary reading while recording disagreement. A broader ethical application should be identified as such. (2) The scene ends with eternal punishment and eternal life. It cannot be recruited to make judgment disappear. Widows are not named in this list; use RD-06 for that distinct strand.

## RD-06 — Widows: exploitation and care

**Mark 12:38–40; James 1:22–27.**

- [Mark 12:38–44, USCCB reading](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/readings/060626.cfm)
- [Greek and English display of Mark 12:40](https://www.greekbible.com/mark/12/40)
- [Greek and English display of James 1:27](https://www.greekbible.com/james/1/27)
- [James 1, published reading](https://www.dioceseoflansing.org/james-1)
- **Status:** key verses and James 1:22–27 checked in the linked displays.

Mark criticizes piety associated with taking widows' property. James explicitly connects religion with care for widows and orphans and with conduct, not hearing alone.

**Attribution:** James is an epistle, not a Gospel saying attributed to Jesus. These verses, Matthew 7, and Matthew 25 form a thematic comparison; they are not one combined speech. Use Mark 12:40 rather than assuming Matthew 23:14 belongs unproblematically to the critical text.

## RD-07 — The ethical criticism is already Jewish

**Isaiah 58:6–10.**

- [Text, KJV display](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+58%3A6-10&version=KJV)
- **Status:** English passage checked; no Hebrew or Septuagint lexical argument has been completed in this pass.

The passage describes acceptable fasting through liberation from oppression, provision of food and shelter, and attention to afflicted people. It prevents a contrast between supposedly rigid Judaism and compassionate Christianity. Jesus's challenge is intelligible within Jewish scriptural traditions of justice and mercy, not only against them.

## RD-08 — Practices that may interrupt rigidity

**Luke 18:9–14; Matthew 7:1–5, 15–27; John 8:31–36; Luke 10:25–37.**

- [Luke 18:9–14, KJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+18%3A9-14&version=KJV)
- [Luke 10, text and notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/luke/10)
- Matthew: RD-04. John: [B-09–B-11](biblical-texts.md#b-09).
- **Status:** Luke passages and Matthew passages checked; John passages previously checked in this repository.

The proposed antidotes are a synthesis: self-examination instead of contempt; readiness to receive rather than only assert; concrete care across an inherited boundary; testing conduct and consequences; practice that can correct one's understanding. These are not a list Jesus explicitly gives as a cure for Mark 3:29.

**Self-application:** the Pharisee/tax-collector story must not become a reason to congratulate ourselves for being unlike Pharisees. Protecting someone from abuse is not rigidity. No harmed person is obliged to resume contact or supply the offender's moral education. Grace and encounter may interrupt resistance; openness is not a guaranteed self-administered technique.

## RD-09 — Christian interpretation and the Holocaust

**United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “The German Churches and the Nazi State.”**

- [Article](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/the-german-churches-and-the-nazi-state)
- **Locators:** “Background”; “Protestant Churches in Nazi Germany”; “Summary.”
- **Status:** text checked. This is an institutional historical synthesis, not a primary document of the 1930s.
- **Short excerpt:** “traditional interpretations of religious scriptures seemed to support these prejudices.”

The museum describes the convergence of racial Nazi antisemitism with older antisemitism rooted partly in Christian history. Nationalism and other political conditions also shaped conduct. It records church support and accommodation, failures to oppose persecution, and exceptions involving resistance and rescue. These findings support a discussion of Christian responsibility without making theology the sole cause of genocide.

**Historical limits:** do not equate doctrinal rigidity with Nazism, identify all churches or Christians as identical, or claim Mark 3:29 caused the Holocaust. Do not turn Jewish lives into illustrations whose value depends on Christian moral learning. A Christian examination of complicity must not suggest that Jewish refusal of Christian claims caused persecution.

**Further reading listed by the museum, not yet read here:** Doris L. Bergen, *Twisted Cross: The German Christian Movement in the Third Reich* (1996); Robert P. Ericksen, *Complicity in the Holocaust: Churches and Universities in Nazi Germany* (2012); Victoria Barnett, *For the Soul of the People* (1992). Consult these and Jewish accounts before enlarging the historical section.

## RD-10 — Authoritative teaching can also correct harmful interpretation

**Second Vatican Council, Nostra aetate, 28 October 1965, sections 4–5.**

- [Vatican text](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_decl_19651028_nostra-aetate_en.html)
- **Status:** sections 4–5 checked in the official English text; a primary source for the council's teaching.
- **Short excerpt:** “the Jews should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God”.

The declaration rejects collective attribution of responsibility for the Passion, condemns antisemitism, and requires care in teaching and preaching. It retains Catholic theological commitments: do not redescribe it as a wholesale abandonment of doctrine. It demonstrates that doctrinal authority can constrain harmful readings as well as protect them. Its issuance does not establish that antisemitism or institutional failures were thereby ended.

## RD-11 — Hard evidence the proposal must retain

- Mark 3:29's temporal denial and Matthew 12:32's reference to both ages: [B-02–B-03](biblical-texts.md#b-02).
- [Mark 4:10–12](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/mark/4): text checked. Its relation among parables, perception, turning, and forgiveness is harder than a simple model of voluntary openness. Research judicial hardening and Isaiah 6; do not imply that people always choose their inability to understand.
- Matthew 25:46: RD-05. The care passage itself retains severe judgment.
- Paul: [B-05–B-08](biblical-texts.md#b-05). He is a canonical test of transformation, not proven to have committed Mark's exact act.
- Aquinas: [R-04](interpretation-history.md#r-04). Resistance to a remedy is not automatically a limit on divine power; his larger theology still includes final impenitence.

## Next questions for this section

1. Study the whole hardening vocabulary in Mark, rather than treating rigidity as an established synonym. Compare 3:5, 6:52, 8:17, and 10:5 with Hebrew scripture and the Septuagint.
2. Distinguish ordinary incomprehension, defended refusal, moral hypocrisy, and judicial hardening. Which distinctions does Mark actually make?
3. Test the hypothesis against Matthew 7's rejected miracle workers: how are genuine liberation and an authority's claim to produce it distinguished?
4. Develop the historical application through specific claims, institutions, and actions, with specialist and Jewish sources, not a generic assertion that narrowness caused the Holocaust.
5. Ask the question of the essay itself: what evidence would require revision of this preferred interpretation?
