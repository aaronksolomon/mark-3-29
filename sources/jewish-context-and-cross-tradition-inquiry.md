# Jewish contexts and learning across traditions

**Status:** first bounded research pass, 2026-10-12, following Aaron’s annotations at `fe35e8aed443284b33b5cb4b6bedb2632b5d0816`. This is not a completed study of Jewish theology from antiquity to the present. The records distinguish biblical and Second Temple material from later rabbinic, medieval, and contemporary interpretation. Later sources illuminate continuing Jewish inquiry; they do not supply a transcript of what every first-century teacher believed.

Jewish sources have two roles here: the religious and linguistic inheritance of Jesus and the evangelists, and an independently continuing tradition of interpretation. Buddhist sources offer a further comparative perspective. These roles should not be flattened into interchangeable illustrations for an argument already decided.

## JC-01

**Sabbath, covenant, and healing: Exodus 20:8–11; Deuteronomy 5:12–15; Mark 3:1–6; Mishnah Yoma 8:6.**

- [Exodus 20, NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus+20&version=NIV)
- [Deuteronomy 5, NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy+5&version=NIV)
- [Mark 3, NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+3&version=NIV)
- [Mishnah Yoma, Hebrew, Mechon Mamre](https://mechon-mamre.org/b/h/h25.htm)
- **Status:** selected biblical texts and Hebrew Mishnah passage checked. Mechon Mamre displays common Yoma 8:6 at its paragraph 8:4 [6]. No comprehensive first-century halakhic reconstruction completed.

Torah commands Sabbath rest, extending it to household workers, resident foreigners, and animals. Deuteronomy relates it to liberation from slavery. Mark depicts scrutiny of a particular healing. Do not turn that scene into a claim that Jewish law universally prohibited all healing. The later Mishnah permits treatment where life may be in danger; its formulation cannot simply be projected backward into the scene or made to decide the status of this non-emergency healing.

**Research needed:** first-century diversity, emergency and non-emergency treatment, and the different activities counted as work. Torah includes instruction, worship, covenantal obligation, and communal practice; it is not merely a modern criminal code, but neither is its normative force unreal.

## JC-02

**Exodus 34:6–7: mercy and accountability in one proclamation.**

- [Hebrew and JPS 1917 English, Mechon Mamre](https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt0234.htm)
- [NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus+34&version=NIV)
- **Status:** Hebrew/English presentation and NIV passage checked.

The draft quotes two short NIV phrases concerning forgiveness and the guilty. They belong to the same proclamation of the divine name. The passage also speaks of consequences across generations: do not suppress that difficulty or make it interchangeable with a modern therapeutic account of responsibility. Mercy is not introduced into a previously merciless tradition by Jesus or by the Buddhist comparison.

**Use:** an inherited conjunction of pardon and seriousness, not a direct solution to Mark’s exception or a complete account of Jewish theology.

## JC-03

**Ezekiel 33:7–20, especially 11 and 14–16: warning, turning, and changed conduct.**

- [Hebrew and JPS 1917 English](https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt1233.htm)
- [NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+33&version=NIV)
- **Status:** relevant chapter text checked.

The watchman must warn; the purpose of the warning is related to turning and living. Verses 14–16 describe restitution and just conduct after a threat of death. The draft uses the brief call to turn and the denial of pleasure in death. Neither the danger nor responsibility disappears. This supplies a Jewish scriptural context in which severe speech and concern for life coexist, not proof that Mark directly depends on this passage or that all eschatological judgment is reversible.

Verses 30–33, retained for later study, also distinguish enjoying a prophet’s words from doing them. Do not add another main-text comparison unless it replaces repetition.

## JC-04

**Ben Sira: Sirach 28:1–7 and the Greek translator’s prologue, lines 15–26.**

- [Sirach 28, NRSVUE](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Sirach+28&version=NRSVUE)
- [Prologue, NRSVUE, displayed before Sirach 1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Sirach+1&version=NRSVUE)
- **Status:** relevant English passages checked. The Hebrew textual history and Greek edition have not been collated in this pass.

This Jewish wisdom work belongs to the Second Temple setting before the Gospels. Chapter 28 relates forgiving another person to seeking divine pardon, while retaining anger, judgment, and accountability. The draft quotes the opening instruction of 28:2, not the entire passage. Its Christian canonical status differs across churches; it is used here first as Jewish evidence.

The prologue’s translator identifies his grandfather’s Hebrew work and discusses what translation changes. Its caution concerns the work of rendering sacred teaching, not a claim that meaning is inaccessible. Its short excerpt appears in the draft. The NIV does not contain Sirach; the NRSVUE attribution must remain explicit.

## JC-05

**Mishnah Yoma 8:9: repentance and wrongs against another person.**

- [Hebrew text, Mechon Mamre](https://mechon-mamre.org/b/h/h25.htm)
- **Locator:** common numbering 8:9, displayed on this page as 8:7 [9].
- **Status:** relevant Hebrew paragraph checked; the brief English below is a project study rendering, not a quotation from a named published English edition.

**Hebrew phrase:** עד שירצה את חברו. **Study rendering:** “until one has appeased one’s fellow.” The context concerns wrongs between people for which the Day of Atonement does not atone without addressing the person wronged. The same paragraph rejects planning to sin on the assumption that repentance or the holy day will automatically clear the matter.

This is later rabbinic evidence, not a recovered rule spoken in Mark’s encounter. It distinguishes divine atonement from interpersonal obligation; it does not by itself articulate every modern distinction among forgiveness, safety, restored trust, and reconciliation. Read with JC-06 rather than treating repentance as a technique that compels an outcome.

## JC-06

**Moses Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Laws of Repentance, 2:9 and 6:3.**

- [Chapter 2, Hebrew](https://mechon-mamre.org/i/1502.htm)
- [Chapter 6, Hebrew](https://mechon-mamre.org/i/1506.htm)
- **Locators:** conventional 2:9 appears at 2:11 [9]; conventional 6:3 begins at 6:4 [3] and continues over the following numbered paragraphs.
- **Status:** selected Hebrew passages checked; short English renderings are project translations. A complete bilingual critical study remains outstanding.

The medieval codification joins repentance for interpersonal wrongdoing to restitution and seeking the injured person’s pardon. It also allows grave, deliberate wrongdoing to be punished through withholding repentance. In 6:3, **שמונעין ממנו התשובה** is rendered here as “repentance is withheld from him.” Isaiah 6 and Pharaoh belong to that argument.

These passages prevent a one-sided picture of Jewish reflection as uniformly restorative or free of hardening theology. They are not evidence that Mark assumed Maimonides’s later system. Chapter 2 also contains duties of forgiveness and polemical contrasts between communities; do not present it as identical to a modern trauma-informed framework.

## JC-08

**Crossing a boundary: Matthew 8:5–13 and Mark 7:24–30.**

- [Matthew 8, NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+8&version=NIV)
- [Mark 7, NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+7&version=NIV)
- **Status:** relevant passages checked.

Matthew presents Jesus as recognizing extraordinary trust in a centurion. Its surrounding saying also contains exclusionary judgment; the encounter is not a declaration that every religious claim is equivalent. Mark explicitly identifies the woman as Greek and Syrophoenician by birth. Her answer matters within an exchange that begins with a difficult image of children and dogs. Retain both the boundary and the response; do not invent an unrecorded motive of testing her or a proven change in Jesus’s entire theology.

The main text uses these encounters to question the ownership of insight by one’s group. That is a constructive reason for comparative inquiry, not a historical claim that Jesus taught modern interreligious pluralism or Buddhist nonattachment. For the different case of Samaritans and shared Israelite inheritance, retain [ND-06](nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-06--the-neighbor-and-worship-beyond-rival-locations).

## JC-09

**Composition, language, and surviving witnesses.**

- [Bart D. Ehrman, “Mark as Peter’s Scribe,” author’s reply, 3 June 2013, 11:09 pm](https://ehrmanblog.org/mark-as-peters-scribe/)
- [SBL Greek New Testament](https://sblgnt.com/)
- **Status:** the scholar’s relevant reply checked; existing [HT-07](humanity-and-provocative-teaching.md#ht-07) remains the edition/transmission record.

Ehrman gives 65–70 CE as the majority dating in this discussion and identifies the work as Greek composition, not translation Greek. The draft uses the broader formulation “commonly dated around 70 CE.” This does not establish exact authorship, location, or an exact date. Earlier oral or written sources and surviving manuscript copies are separate questions. No extant manuscript is an autograph of Jesus’s speech, and no demonstrated earlier Aramaic Gospel of Mark is supplied here.

**Thomas/oldest-record question:** keep composition date separate from date, language, and extent of a surviving manuscript. The [Gospel of Thomas translation](https://www.gospels.net/thomas) was located, but this pass has not resolved Thomas’s composition history or surveyed the earliest Gospel papyri. Do not call Thomas the earliest Gospel or infer a lost Aramaic Mark from later Coptic witnesses. A concise, specialist-checked chronology is still needed before expanding this point.

## JC-10

**Mark 4:10–25 in relation to Isaiah 6:9–13 and Matthew 13:10–17.**

- [Mark 4, NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+4&version=NIV)
- [Isaiah 6, Hebrew and JPS 1917](https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt1006.htm)
- [Matthew 13, NIV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+13&version=NIV)
- **Status:** selected texts checked; no full source-critical, Hebrew/Greek, or reception study completed.
- **2026-10-12 review and revision:** the targeted review reread Mark 4:10–12 and 21–24 in the linked NIV text. This explanation now names the insider/outsider distinction and the teaching’s role in concealment and disclosure. The wider research limits above remain.

In Mark 4:11–12, Jesus distinguishes those given the kingdom’s secret from those outside, then connects parabolic speech through purpose language with seeing without perceiving, hearing without understanding, and failure to turn and receive forgiveness. The difficulty concerns the role assigned to the teaching in concealment or hardening, not only a hearer’s failure to pay attention. Mark also depicts those around Jesus asking questions (4:10), says that what is hidden is meant to be disclosed (4:21–22), and calls for attentive hearing (4:24). These counterpoints do not simply cancel what is said about outsiders. Mark 4 does not independently state an eternal duration of exclusion. It challenges a claim that every parable simply communicates reassurance or invariably succeeds in opening understanding.

Matthew’s version emphasizes closed eyes differently. Do not make either version dissolve the other or assert that the purpose language is unambiguously benevolent. Study Isaiah’s commission and its judgment context. A compassionate interpretation must engage that tension rather than assume that a troublesome phrase can only mean what compassion already requires.

## Next research, without enlarging the essay into a survey

Build a small chronological corpus: Hebrew scripture and Greek Jewish translations; a few Second Temple witnesses; later Mishnah and Talmud; medieval interpreters; contemporary Jewish scholarship and practice. For each claim, record which period it can illuminate. Do not treat a later formulation as a general account of Jesus’s contemporaries.

The next priorities are Sabbath interpretation; differentiated wrongdoing and repentance; atonement and interpersonal repair; hardening and the possibility of turning; and the distinction between composition and manuscript dates. Bring the existing Barr/Anderson reading queue into this study. A qualified Jewish reader and a scholar of early Judaism would be especially valuable for testing both the positive account and its omissions.

**Editorial decision:** retain v2. Introduce Buddhism earlier as a source of perspective; ground Jesus’s own teaching positively in Jewish sources; keep larger comparisons in notes. Compassion remains the author’s organizing commitment, not proof of every historical intention or a reason to call every contrary reading cruel.

## JC-12 — Retaining rejected opinions and learning from a rival

**Checked October 14, 2026.** Mishnah Eduyot 1:4, [Hebrew text](https://mechon-mamre.org/b/h/h47.htm), explains preserving rejected opinions as teaching a person not to insist upon their own position. The surrounding unit concerns legal deliberation and records concrete disagreements; it does not abandon law or render all claims equally valid. The essay paraphrases the Hebrew rather than attributing an unconsulted English translation.

Babylonian Talmud Eruvin 13b, [Hebrew/Aramaic text](https://mechon-mamre.org/b/l/l2201.htm), passage beginning with Rabbi Abba’s report in Samuel’s name, commends Hillel’s school for teaching the rival account and putting it before its own. The essay supplies a brief marked study rendering. A practical ruling remains. These later formulations do not establish that Mark’s scribes held these maxims or that Jesus used them in this encounter. They offer independent Jewish resources for the practice of interpretation, not a generic theory of relativism.

## JC-14 — Rabbi Akiva completes the Yoma passage

Mishnah Yoma 8:9, [Hebrew text](https://mechon-mamre.org/b/h/h25.htm), displayed as 8:7 [9], ends with Rabbi Akiva’s address to Israel concerning purification by their heavenly Father. **Text checked October 14, 2026.** The essay adds a marked study rendering of his question and answer. This is the same unit that rejects planned reliance on later atonement and requires addressing the person harmed. Akiva draws on Ezekiel 36:25 and Jeremiah 17:13. Divine purification and interpersonal responsibility coexist in the unit; they are not interchangeable concepts, and its address to Israel must not silently become a claim that the Mishnah teaches universal salvation. This later rabbinic voice is not a transcript of Mark’s encounter.
