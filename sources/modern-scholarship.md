## S-01

**Janusz Kręcidło, “What Is the Sin of Blasphemy against the Holy Spirit, and Why Is It Unforgivable (Mark 3:28–30 and Parallels)?”**

- [Journal landing page, abstract, bibliography, and PDF link](https://czasopisma.kul.pl/index.php/ba/article/view/18561)
- [DOI: 10.31743/ba.18561](https://doi.org/10.31743/ba.18561)
- **Published:** 29 January 2026. Landing-page citation metadata identifies *The Biblical Annals* 16(1), 97–113; verify printed pagination against the PDF before final citation.
- **Status:** abstract and bibliography checked. Full-text retrieval did not succeed during setup; do not describe this as a completed article review.
- **Brief quotation from abstract:** “the rejection of the revelation offered by God in Jesus Christ”.

The abstract proposes a rejection-of-revelation/apostasy reading and distinct emphases in the three Synoptic presentations. It proposes that Luke universalizes the saying, probably with Q behind it. These are the author’s arguments, not facts established by the abstract or a scholarly consensus.

**Why read:** recent focused study, access to a substantial earlier bibliography, and a direct challenge to overly simple answers. Follow its references to Athanasius, Callaham, Collins, France, and other interpretive traditions.

## S-02

**Gary A. Anderson, Sin: A History. Yale University Press, 2009; paperback 2010.**

- [Publisher page](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300168099/sin/)
- **Selected edition:** paperback ISBN 9780300168099. The same page lists the 2009 e-book and 2010 paperback separately.
- **Status:** publisher description and metadata checked; book not read in this setup.
- **Brief phrase from publisher description:** “eclipsed by economic metaphors”.

The publisher describes Anderson’s argument as tracing changing imagery of sin, including burden and debt, across Jewish and Christian traditions. This is particularly relevant to Aaron’s question about changes in meaning. It should prompt corpus-based study, not be used to declare that all biblical sin has one metaphor or that punitive concepts appeared only late.

**Reading questions:** Which texts, periods, and languages support the proposed changes? What overlaps remain? How do forgiveness and consequences coexist? How does the account resist a Judaism-versus-Christianity caricature?
