## Earlier assistant governing thesis — preserved, not endorsed unchanged

> Mark 3:28–30 preserves a paradoxical prophetic warning rather than a finished doctrine of eternal punishment. All forms of human sin and slander are announced as forgivable, yet the person who identifies the Spirit’s liberating work as evil is said not to possess release “into the age.” As the saying passes through the evangelists, manuscript traditions, theological systems, and modern languages, its relational vocabulary increasingly acquires the associations of crime, guilt, judgment, and punishment. Paul’s transformation places a crucial question within the Christian canon itself: can profound religious blindness be transformed by the same Spirit it once opposed? The passage may warn that blindness has no remedy while the remedy is called evil, without requiring us to conclude that blindness is an eternal essence from which even the Spirit cannot free a human being.

### Why the active thesis is more cautious

“Relational vocabulary increasingly acquires” a legal frame was not supported by a completed semantic history and understates ancient liability language. “Prophetic warning” also needs to be treated as a characterization of the situated saying, not a settled verdict on historical genre. The broad promise must remain qualified by Mark’s actual exception.

The active formulation therefore asks how overlapping meanings and interpretive additions operate, rather than asserting a linear change from relational innocence to punitive law. It preserves Paul as a canonical question, but does not claim that Paul demonstrably committed the precise act in Mark or that his transformation settles every possible reading.

## Conditional-resistance proposal — assistant elaboration

> So long as healing is perceived and named as evil, it cannot be received as healing. So long as release is understood as corruption, one does not have release. The blindness has no cure while the cure itself is named the enemy. But when perception changes—when what was called evil is recognized as life—the person no longer stands in precisely the same relation described by the blasphemy.

**Status:** constructive interpretation. The condition “so long as” or “while” is not explicit in Mark 3:29. Its value must be tested against the Greek idiom, Matthew, Paul, reception history, and a coherent account of transformation. Inability, lack of access, injury, honest disagreement, and culpable refusal should not be conflated.

## The time comparison — editorial summary

The proposed Buddhist comparison is not that aiōn secretly means nonlinear time. It asks whether a person’s continuation necessarily means a permanently fixed relation to a past act. Past harm continues through effects; present recognition can alter subsequent conduct without undoing those effects or obliging another person to reconcile. Future possibility prevents the past from automatically becoming a complete description of the person.

A precise Plum Village source is now available: Sister Annabel Laity’s discussion of the interbeing of the three times, including regret and changed action in the present. Its authorship and scope are recorded in [PV-04](../../sources/plum-village/time-and-transformation.md#pv-04). TNH recordings are separate sources awaiting audio-level quotation checks.

## The project’s central open question

Could the warning concern a relation in which release itself is repudiated, rather than a past act that makes every future transformation impossible? If the person comes to see differently, what happens to the relation named in the warning? The interpretation remains accountable to the text precisely by acknowledging what it adds and what resists it.
