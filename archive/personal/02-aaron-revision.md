## Title and opening

**The Open Door**  
**Finding Forgiveness in the Humanity and Divinity of Jesus**

> In 2021, I caused harm in a community that I cared deeply about.
>
> In the years since, I have had to look deeply into both forgiveness and reconciliation.
>
> Before this experience, forgiveness was less complex, perhaps more of a notion. I could speak about compassion, reconciliation, understanding, and healing. I believed in these things. I had the sense of practicing them.
>
> Having caused deep unintentional suffering for myself and others has brought me into deeper contact with what forgiveness and reconciliation may mean.
>
> I have come to recognize that my intentions do not determine the consequences of my actions. I failed to see something important about myself, about the power and responsibility that I held, and the situation in which I found myself.
>
> The consequences of my actions cannot simply be taken back.
>
> Forgiveness does not make them disappear.
>
> Understanding why I acted as I did does not undo what happened. Remorse does not automatically lead to reconciliation. Trust may take a very long time to return, if it can return at all.
>
> And yet another question remains.
>
> When I have caused harm, is the harm I caused the final truth about who I am?
>
> Can understanding deepen? Can responsibility become transformation? Can a person learn to see what they could not see before? Can something broken become—not what it was before, perhaps—but something honest and alive again?

**Editorial summary of the next passage:** the draft turns to a fresh encounter with Mark 3:28–30. It asks for complete Greek, romanization, literal English, and a canonical/textual source. The placeholders were not completed in Aaron’s submitted version; the active [biblical notes](../../sources/biblical-texts.md) now provide a checked study apparatus.

## Learning to See

> They encounter what the Gospel understands as healing, and they call it evil.
>
> Remarkably, human beings can look directly at goodness and fail to recognize it. We can be sincere and still be mistaken.
>
> We can inherit ways of seeing that make some things visible and others invisible. We can become attached to an idea, a community, an identity, or a way of understanding God. When something appears that does not fit the world as we know it, we may experience it not as an invitation but as a threat.
>
> Perhaps this is one way to understand sin. Not simply wickedness. Not simply breaking a rule.
>
> I have often thought of sin as being rooted in wrong perception. We do not see clearly, and because we do not see clearly, we act in ways that bring suffering to ourselves and to others.
>
> However, leaving it at wrong perception makes this sound harmless.
>
> We may act out of confusion, fear, certainty, loyalty, or deeply inherited beliefs and still cause deep harm. This is my lived and personal experience.

> Still, we do not need to condemn the people who came before us in order to tell the truth about this historical suffering, or to grapple with what it means to reconcile, to heal, to transform suffering, and to forgive.
>
> We can understand that people live within the perceptions available to them. We can also acknowledge the suffering those perceptions caused.

> “All is forgiven” can be used to avoid listening, learning, transforming and healing. That is not the forgiveness I am coming to understand.
>
> Forgiveness does not mean that consequences disappear nor that reconciliation must occur on our timescale in the way we want it to, or that trust must return.
>
> I think forgiveness is something more demanding:
>
> Nothing is hidden: the harm must be recognized, and no one needs to be reduced forever to the worst that they have done.

**Interpretive proposal in this version, not a literal translation:**

> My hearing of the second phrase of the passage, “This will not be forgiven forever,” lands closer in me as “the consequences of seeing good as evil are extremely severe.”
>
> My paraphrase is:
>
> All can be forgiven.
>
> And:
>
> How we see matters. Holding on to our views can keep us from touching the Kingdom of God that is right here.

**Editorial caution:** the subsequent scholarly reset explicitly returns to the harder temporal language rather than replacing it with severity of consequences.

## The Human Stream

> Perhaps revelation has always traveled through human beings rather than around them. Churches have continued the living stream of tradition and teaching, transmitting not only the written word, but the living word.
>
> This does not diminish the power of scripture. It shows us how to see into scripture more deeply.
>
> A living tradition is not the same thing as an infallible transcript. It is a stream of human beings trying to remain faithful to something they have encountered as true. There is wisdom in that stream. There is also fear and misunderstanding.
>
> There is courage and blindness, insight and confusion, love and sometimes hatred.

> And if we can allow the Gospel writers, Paul, the religious teachers, and ourselves to be fully human, perhaps we also allow Jesus to be fully human, to speak with anger, or even imperfectly.

> What would it mean to trust Jesus enough to allow his humanity to be real?

> We see something when he meets the person others have rejected, when he touches someone others will not touch. We see a radical love when a person is no longer reduced to a category, when service becomes greater than status, when forgiveness becomes more important than condemnation, when fear gives way to love.
>
> Perhaps the divine is not what remains after humanity has been removed, but what shines most brightly through a human life.

**Editorial caution:** the complete draft also speculated that Jesus likely did things he regretted. This has not been established historically and is not retained in the active draft. Human limitation, anger, moral error, and divinity require distinct questions.

## Remaining in the Teaching

> Doing the truth.
>
> Some truths have to be lived before they can be fully known.
>
> We can study forgiveness. We can construct a theology of forgiveness. We can read everything Jesus says about forgiveness.
>
> Then we must forgive. And sometimes we have to ask to be forgiven.
>
> We can study reconciliation, but at some point we have to sit in the presence of another human being whose experience may be very different from our own.
>
> We can study repentance, but at some point we have to let the truth change us.
>
> The Gospel looks like something we understand by walking.

> This does not mean that experience is infallible.
>
> Our experience can deceive us. Our preferences can deceive us. Our communities can become closed around shared misunderstandings.
>
> This is why we need so many ways of looking, to be curious about the meanings and lives of the people who lived and wrote. It is why we need traditions and the challenging of traditions across all the religions and human wisdom. It's why we need each other, and to study, not only teachings, but our own lives.

> But faith is not the same thing as certainty. Living faith asks us to inherit deeply, to look deeply into our own experience and the lives of others, to trust what we can see is good, to continue, and to see farther.

**Editorial note:** the submitted draft requested John 7:17 in full and better textual sources for John 8:31–32 and 3:21. These are now in [B-09–B-11](../../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-09). The earlier “be a disciple” sequence should not be changed into “become” as a translation.

## The Door

> In my own journey toward healing and reconciliation, I cannot ask forgiveness to erase the past. I must remain open to the uncertain process of inner transformation, longing for healing and reconciliation, and continually opening to new insight, new pathways, deeper understanding of pain and suffering.
>
> I cannot make another person’s experience conform to my understanding or longing.
>
> I cannot decide what reconciliation looks like.
>
> I can practice looking, listening, acknowledging, forgiving.
>
> I can learn.
>
> I can take responsibility where responsibility is mine to take.
>
> I can allow the suffering that occurred to become part of the conditions for greater understanding.

> If wrong perception becomes very deep, can it still be transformed? If a person closes the door, can the door open again?

> The person who caused harm is not necessarily finished. And, the consequences of our actions are lasting.
>
> Holding these together is perhaps one of the difficult forms that love takes.

**Editorial caution:** learning from consequences must not be presented as making harm necessary or valuable for the people affected. The active draft avoids assigning that meaning to others’ suffering.

## Walking

> The answer is not to declare ourselves innocent. Neither is the answer be to declare ourselves forever condemned.
>
> There is another possibility. We can learn the art of forgiveness and the art of opening our hearts. We can walk toward making repair. We can accept consequences.
>
> We can allow the others to have their own experience and understanding, while still saying ‘no’ to harm.
>
> We can loosen our certainty. We can begin again.

> We do not have to choose between reverence and inquiry.
>
> Perhaps deep reverence requires deep inquiry.

> Does the full frail and complex humanity of Jesus lead us toward his true divinity—not divinity understood as distance, but divinity becoming visible through a human life given to love?

> How do we see clearly enough that we do not close off ourselves, or someone else, from the possibility of healing?
>
> Perhaps we are not asked to know the answer.
>
> We are asked to take the next step.
>
> To listen, share, look, learn, forgive, be open.
>
> To remain with what is difficult.
>
> To look again at someone we thought we understood.
>
> To remain in the teachings on love, and to see what becomes true in our lives, to let the Gospel live in us and our communities.
