# Personal-draft development — full-draft and tightening stages

**Provenance:** editorial summary with selected assistant excerpts from the supplied conversation. This file records what the long drafts attempted; it is not a replacement transcript. The drafts are superseded and are not the current stylistic target.

## The full personal essay

The assistant’s first complete personal draft was titled *The Open Door: Finding the Humanity and Divinity of Jesus*. It began with the 2021 harm statement, then moved through six major sections. It was longer than the requested roughly four-page essay and repeated several claims in slightly different language.

### Opening: forgiveness after harm

The opening distinguished intention from effects, explanation from undoing, remorse from entitlement to reconciliation, and trust from forgiveness. It asked whether harm is the final truth about the person who caused it. The questions led into Mark, rather than using autobiography as proof that a particular translation must be right.

The original community name is omitted from this public archive. No incident details are added. Assistant-composed first-person prose remains proposed language, not an additional autobiographical record.

### Learning to See

The draft read Mark 3:30 as the interpretive key: liberation is called demonic. It proposed wrong perception as one root of sin, immediately qualified by the reality of harm. It connected this to harmful Christian anti-Jewish teaching, insisting that compassion for earlier interpreters does not require denying consequences.

A central sentence was:

> Nothing is hidden, nothing harmful needs to be called harmless, and no human being needs to be reduced forever to the worst thing they have done.

**Current caution:** wrong perception should not become a total lexical definition of sin. Nor should “all can be forgiven” be treated as equivalent to everything Mark explicitly says.

### The Human Stream

The draft traced teaching through speaking, remembering, communities, Gospel composition, copying, translation, and interpretation. It moved from the humanity of the evangelists and Paul toward that of Jesus. The proposed theological image was divinity disclosed through humanity, rather than divinity remaining after humanity has been subtracted.

**Current caution:** the actual historical transmission is more complex than a straight chain. Genuine humanity does not establish any specific error by Jesus. A constructive Christology should not be presented as merely the conclusion of historical criticism.

### Remaining in the Teaching

John 8:31–32, 7:17, and 3:21 supplied the themes of abiding, willing to do, and doing truth. The draft paired study with practice and emphasized that experience too can mislead. Scripture, tradition, community, and inquiry were intended as mutually correcting resources.

**Current correction:** “you are truly my disciples” is not a future stage of becoming a disciple. John’s specific theological claims cannot be reduced to an experiment in subjective verification.

### The Door

The draft returned to the proposed pattern: resistance can reject the very thing that could heal it. The personal voice acknowledged that it cannot decide how someone else should reconcile or what their experience must mean. The draft suggested that consequences can become teachers.

**Current caution:** no one is entitled to assign a redemptive value to another person’s suffering. Present growth does not make past harm necessary, justified, or erased.

### Walking

The conclusion placed the writer inside the same human history as disciples, religious teachers, Paul, evangelists, and generations of believers. It ended in practice rather than claiming to have solved Mark 3:29.

Selected closing lines:

> We do not have to choose between reverence and inquiry.
>
> Perhaps deep reverence requires deep inquiry.

> How can we learn to see clearly enough that we do not close ourselves, or one another, outside the possibility of healing?

## Aaron’s revision

Aaron made the first-person voice more specific about power and responsibility and more explicit about openness across religious traditions. He kept the section structure but requested better Greek and canonical source references. He also proposed hearing Mark’s severe clause as a warning about the consequences of calling good evil.

The subsequent scholarly direction preserves that interpretation as a proposal, while refusing to substitute it for the Greek. See [Aaron excerpts](02-aaron-revision.md).

## The attempted tightening

The assistant’s revision retained the title *The Open Door: Finding Forgiveness in the Humanity and Divinity of Jesus*. It reduced some repetition and removed some speculative language, but kept the same progression and many short-line rhetorical patterns.

Two formulations became central:

> The person who has caused harm is not necessarily finished becoming.
>
> And the harm is still real.

The assistant also explicitly advised retaining uncertainty rather than translating “never forgiven” into “the consequences are severe.”

## Why this strand was set aside

Aaron found that the version still did not land. He asked to start again with a scholarly presentation of sources, changing meanings, and questions. He rejected attempted imitation of TNH’s voice. This is the governing editorial decision.

What remains useful is not the whole style but the underlying ethical constraints: acknowledge harm; do not demand reconciliation; do not freeze persons at a past moment; include oneself in the fallibility of interpretation; and allow personal experience to enter later without making it carry the entire exegetical argument.
