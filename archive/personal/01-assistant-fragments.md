# Early personal opening, middle, and closing — assistant excerpts

**Provenance:** selected assistant-composed passages from the supplied conversation; line breaks and punctuation normalized. Not a complete draft or independent record of Aaron’s life. The community name included in some earlier formulations is not reproduced. These passages belong to the abandoned personal/contemplative drafting stage, not the current required voice.

## Opening offered after Aaron proposed personal grounding

> In 2021 I caused harm in a community I cared deeply about. In the years since, I have had to think differently about forgiveness.
>
> Before this experience, forgiveness could still be, at least partly, an idea. Afterward it became something much more difficult. I had to live with the fact that our intentions and our understanding of ourselves do not determine the effects of our actions. We can believe that we are acting rightly and still cause suffering. We can fail to see something important about ourselves, about another person, or about the situation before us.
>
> Some consequences cannot simply be taken back. Forgiveness does not make them disappear.
>
> And yet I have also had to ask another question. When we have caused harm, is the harm we caused the final truth about who we are? Can understanding deepen? Can responsibility become transformation? Can trust ever be rebuilt? Can something that has been broken become, perhaps not what it was before, but something honest and alive again?
>
> These questions have changed the way I hear a difficult passage in the Gospel of Mark.

**Editorial note:** this opener was offered as possible language, not as a claim that every sentence had been independently confirmed by Aaron. His subsequent revision is a separate authorial source.

## Accountability and mercy

> Forgiveness does not mean that nothing happened. Forgiveness does not erase consequences. Forgiveness does not make reconciliation automatic, nor does it remove the need for truth, accountability, restitution, repentance, and change. Perhaps forgiveness means that even after real harm, we refuse to say that the harm is the final truth about a human being.

> Compassion does not require us to pretend that every interpretation is harmless. We can refrain from condemning one another while still looking honestly at the suffering our views and actions have produced.

**Editorial note:** the discussion sought to avoid two evasions: irredeemable condemnation and a spiritual softness that moves past harm too quickly. Forgiveness, reconciliation, restored trust, and consequences are distinct questions.

## Practice as part of understanding

> We can study forgiveness. We can develop a theology of forgiveness. We can read every passage in scripture concerning forgiveness. All of this can help us.
>
> But we also have to forgive.
>
> Only then do we begin to discover what forgiveness is.

> We can study love of enemies. But there is something about this teaching that cannot become clear until we encounter someone toward whom our heart has closed and try, patiently and honestly, to find another way of seeing.

**Editorial note:** these paragraphs are exploratory spiritual writing, not a demand that injured people forgive on someone else’s schedule. The current draft avoids turning participation into a universal guarantee of knowledge.

## Return to the personal journey

> I cannot approach forgiveness as an abstraction anymore. I know that harm can continue in the lives of other people long after the person who caused it has begun to change. I know that remorse does not entitle us to reconciliation. I know that understanding why we acted as we did does not undo what happened.
>
> But I have also come to know how important it is that a human being not be frozen forever at one moment in his or her life.
>
> If I am capable of seeing differently today than I did then, perhaps this capacity for new seeing is itself part of grace.
>
> This does not absolve me of responsibility. It makes responsibility deeper. I cannot change what happened. I can allow what happened to change how I live.

**Editorial note:** this remains assistant-proposed testimony. Aaron alone can decide whether and how it expresses his experience.

## Earlier closing passage

> Perhaps this is what it means to receive the Gospel as a living inheritance. We do not stand outside the long human story of misunderstanding and awakening. We belong to it. We walk with the disciples who did not understand, with the religious teachers who feared they were being deceived, with Paul who changed his mind and his life, with the evangelists who struggled to preserve what they had received, and with generations who have tried to understand what Jesus was pointing toward.
>
> We too see only partially. Yet we can continue walking.
>
> We can remain in the teaching, put it into practice, watch carefully what bears the fruits of love and freedom, acknowledge when our understanding has caused harm, and become willing to see again.

**Editorial note:** the personal/communal invitation remains available. Historical claims about motives and the relation of practice to truth need more care in a scholarly version.

## What this stage contributed

The beginning moves from harm and responsibility to a textual question. The middle refuses both excusing and permanent identity reduction. The ending includes the writer within the human interpretive stream rather than standing above the Gospel authors or earlier theologians. Those functions survive even though the style and much of the repetitious wording have been set aside.
