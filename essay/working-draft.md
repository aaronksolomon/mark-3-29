# All Will Be Forgiven?
## Words, Time, and the Humanity of Interpretation

> *“Truly I tell you, people can be forgiven all their sins and every slander they utter, but whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit will never be forgiven; they are guilty of an eternal sin.”*
>
> — Mark 3:28–29, New International Version (2011). [RD-00](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-00--niv-opening)

## 1. A tension before an answer

In one movement, this saying opens forgiveness to the whole range of human wrongdoing and then names an exception that appears permanent. What distinguishes the second case from the first? Is Jesus, as Mark presents him, identifying a particular act whose consequences cannot be overcome, or a condition in which the very possibility of forgiveness is refused? The words do not permit an easy answer. But neither does their severity relieve us of the need to interpret them. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

Mark gives the warning a specific setting. The scribes have attributed Jesus's liberating work to an unclean power. One possibility explored here is that the accusation reveals a rigidity of perception: a judgment so defended that what might challenge or heal it is itself named evil. This is a proposal about the condition underlying the accusation, not a substitute translation for blasphemy. It raises a question that concerns interpreters as well as the people in the story. Can a religious understanding become so fixed that defending it takes precedence over recognizing the good before us?

We bring histories of our own to these words. Sin may suggest moral failure, inherited corruption, broken relationship, or violation of divine law. Forgiveness may suggest pardon, release from debt, relinquished resentment, or reconciliation. Guilt can mean being answerable for an act or feeling guilty about it. Forever may suggest an endless succession of moments. These associations are not interchangeable. Which are appropriate here, and which do we supply without noticing?

The inquiry therefore concerns both the saying and the human work of receiving it. What happens when words attributed to Jesus in a particular conflict are remembered, arranged within a Gospel, translated, preached, and incorporated into doctrine? Respect for Jesus does not require us to decide beforehand that he could never have spoken severely. Respect for scripture need not require that every reported sentence be treated as a complete theological account detached from its occasion. Jesus, the evangelists, and their later readers belong to this inquiry as human participants, not as abstractions whose words arrive without circumstances.

### The two clauses

**Greek:** πάντα ἀφεθήσεται — *panta aphethēsetai*.

**Segmented gloss:** πάντα, all things; ἀφεθήσεται, will be forgiven.

**Natural English:** “All will be forgiven,” with the following words specifying human sins and blasphemies. This is not, by itself, an assertion that every individual will eventually be reconciled. The NIV lead-in uses “can be forgiven”; the study gloss here makes the Greek future passive visible without silently altering the named translation.

**Greek:** οὐκ ἔχει ἄφεσιν εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα — *ouk echei aphesin eis ton aiōna*.

**Segmented gloss:** οὐκ, not; ἔχει, has; ἄφεσιν, forgiveness or release; εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, into/unto the age.

**Natural English:** “Never has forgiveness” or “has no forgiveness forever.” “Into the age” is a mechanical gloss, not better idiomatic English. It does not say “until the end of the age.” [B-01–B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-01)

Both clauses deserve to remain audible. The breadth of the first should not make the second disappear. The severity of the second should not prevent us from asking what its words, setting, and subsequent interpretation actually establish.

## 2. Words moving through worlds — section to develop

The most useful starting point is not a contrast between an innocent ancient meaning and a punitive modern one. Ancient religious language could already be juridical. The word translated “guilty,” enochos, can express liability; “offense” is a possible English gloss for hamartēma, although it is not a separate noun in Mark’s sentence. Conversely, Mark’s preferred critical reading says “eternal sin,” not explicitly “eternal punishment.” The distinction matters without abolishing judgment. [L-02](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-02), [L-04](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-04)

**Develop with a small historical corpus:** Greek error and wrongdoing vocabulary; Jewish scriptural uses of burden, debt, covenant, pardon, and judgment; the Septuagint; later reception; contemporary English. Consult Anderson and Barr before proposing a trajectory. The present notes do not yet establish a comprehensive semantic history.

The wider range of aphesis includes release and remission. That makes “release” a useful question to bring to Mark, but not permission to replace forgiveness with a purely psychological process. Nor does the present tense “does not have” cancel the accompanying “forever.” A sound reading must hold the syntax together. [L-03](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-03), [claim audit](../notes/claim-audit.md)

## 3. What the story names

Mark supplies a reason for the warning: the scribes were saying that Jesus had an unclean spirit. Within the story, what Jesus is doing overcomes destructive power; the accusation attributes that work to the ruler of demons. The issue is not simply an irreverent word. It is a reversal in the recognition of what is taking place. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

**Greek:** ὅτι ἔλεγον· Πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον ἔχει — *hoti elegon: pneuma akatharton echei*.

**Segmented gloss:** ὅτι, because; ἔλεγον, they were saying; πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον, an unclean spirit; ἔχει, he has.

**Natural English:** “Because they were saying, ‘He has an unclean spirit.’”

This narrative explanation brings the warning closer to a recognizable human danger: we may reject something good because receiving it would unsettle our understanding. But Mark’s perspective must not be confused with a transcript of the historical opponents’ minds. The story does not explicitly tell us that they privately recognized divine action and knowingly lied about it. Neither should we assume that sincerity renders harmful judgment harmless.

**Still to write:** the divided house and strong man; the place of forgiveness within God’s reign; the family and disciples in Mark’s larger scene. The following section develops the hardening comparison without equating narrative opponents with Jewish people or religious teachers as a class.

## 4. Rigidity: when conviction prevents recognition

### A condition, not a class of people

By rigidity I do not mean having convictions, belonging to a tradition, or holding firmly to a moral obligation. I mean protecting a judgment from correction: refusing to let what happens, what another person says, or what our actions produce call that judgment into question. The problem is not simply that we have an interpretation. It is that our interpretation has become exempt from examination.

Mark offers a nearby point of contact. Earlier in the same chapter, Jesus asks whether the Sabbath permits doing good and saving life. The response is silence. Mark describes Jesus as angry and grieving at the opponents' hardness of heart before he restores the man's hand. [RD-01](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-01--hardness-before-the-blasphemy-controversy)

**Greek:** συλλυπούμενος ἐπὶ τῇ πωρώσει τῆς καρδίας αὐτῶν.

**Romanization:** *syllypoumenos epi tē pōrōsei tēs kardias autōn*.

**Segmented gloss:** συλλυπούμενος, grieving; ἐπὶ τῇ πωρώσει, at the hardness/hardening; τῆς καρδίας, of the heart; αὐτῶν, of them/their.

**Natural English:** “grieved at their hardness of heart,” or “grieving over their hardened hearts” (Mark 3:5).

This does not establish that hardness of heart and blasphemy are synonyms. It does suggest a question about the chapter's recurring concern: what happens when an established judgment prevents a person from responding to restoration? In the later controversy, the accusation can protect itself against correction. What the story presents as evidence of liberation is made into evidence of demonic power. Could this be the underlying condition that gives the warning its force?

Crucially, Mark also attributes hardened hearts to the disciples (6:52; 8:17). Jesus questions their understanding and memory and continues to teach them. Hardening is therefore not a quality that divides Jewish opponents from Christian insiders. It can describe those who follow Jesus. Nor does the use of this vocabulary automatically mean that a person is past all possibility of response. [RD-02](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-02--the-disciples-are-not-exempt)

John offers a related, though distinct, comparison. At the end of the healing story in John 9, the assertion of sight is connected with sin's remaining. The problem is not simply a lack of information; it concerns a claim to understanding in the face of what the story discloses. But these images must not turn physical disability, uncertainty, or lack of capacity into moral failure. John's own opening rejects a simple attribution of the man's blindness to sin. [RD-03](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-03--claimed-sight-and-remaining-sin)

### When religious profession displaces care

This possibility connects Mark's warning to a wider tension between professed allegiance and enacted faithfulness. In Matthew 7:21–23, calling Jesus Lord is not enough. Those rejected even appeal to prophecy, exorcisms, and powerful deeds performed in his name. Religious language and impressive claims do not exempt a life from examination. The surrounding teaching asks readers to consider fruits, examine themselves, and act on what they hear. This also complicates our argument: a claimed healing is not, simply because it is claimed, proof of a teacher's goodness or authority. [RD-04](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-04--profession-claimed-power-and-conduct)

Matthew 25:31–46 makes care for hungry, displaced, sick, and imprisoned people decisive within a scene of judgment. Its identification of the least brothers is disputed, and its ending retains severe punishment language. Nevertheless, recognition and response to concrete need belong at the center of the scene. Mark's criticism of piety that exploits widows and James's injunction to care for widows and orphans belong to related, but separate, passages. We should not combine these into a single saying Jesus never delivered. [RD-05](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-05--hunger-imprisonment-recognition-and-judgment), [RD-06](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-06--widows-exploitation-and-care)

These comparisons do not prove that every failure of care arises from rigidity. They ask whether a religious identity can become a substitute for the life it claims to express. The question is already present within Jewish scripture: Isaiah 58 joins acceptable fasting to the release of the oppressed and provision for people in need. It would distort this inheritance to tell a story in which Christianity supplies compassion to a Judaism that lacked it. [RD-07](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-07--the-ethical-criticism-is-already-jewish)

### A warning to the interpreters

The most difficult application is inward. If we read Mark only as a warning about other people's resistance, we may reproduce the very pattern we are proposing to examine. A Christian interpreter can defend an account of goodness while refusing to hear the people harmed by its application. Even forgiveness can be used this way when it requires an injured person to cease speaking before responsibility has been acknowledged.

The history of Christian anti-Judaism makes this responsibility concrete. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum documents how inherited Christian prejudices and scriptural interpretations converged with Nazi racial antisemitism. It also identifies other political causes and records differences among complicity, accommodation, resistance, and rescue. Christian theology did not singly cause the Holocaust. But an account that excludes Christian teaching and conduct from its conditions is also inadequate. [RD-09](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-09--christian-interpretation-and-the-holocaust)

This history is not proof that a particular theological opponent has committed the unforgivable sin. Nor should Jewish suffering become merely an illustration in a Christian account of self-improvement. The question it presses upon Christian interpreters is more demanding: when an inherited reading helps us disregard another person's dignity or suffering, what would allow that reading to be questioned? What practices and institutions enable correction before harm is repeated?

The alternative need not be abandonment of doctrine. In Nostra aetate, the Second Vatican Council rejected collective blame for Jesus's death and the presentation of Jews as divinely rejected or accursed. Authoritative teaching can therefore also restrict harmful interpretation. That does not erase earlier injury or establish that the work is finished; it shows that fidelity and correction need not be opposites. [RD-10](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-10--authoritative-teaching-can-also-correct-harmful-interpretation)

### What might interrupt rigidity?

The texts suggest practices rather than a formula. Self-examination comes before correcting another person in Matthew 7:1–5. Luke introduces the Pharisee and tax collector's prayer as a challenge to confidence in one's righteousness joined to contempt for others. These passages can ask the reader to relinquish an exemption from correction, not to exchange one supposedly inferior group for another. [RD-04](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-04--profession-claimed-power-and-conduct), [RD-08](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-08--practices-that-may-interrupt-rigidity)

Care is a second possible interruption. Feeding, visiting, protecting, and listening place our interpretation beside a life that is not reducible to it. The Samaritan story in Luke 10 asks about becoming a neighbor through action rather than merely settling the boundaries of obligation. In the essay's application, listening to someone harmed by an institution would have to include willingness to change the institution's practices, not simply to invite testimony and then leave everything unchanged. This is an ethical extension of the comparison, not a claim that Luke addresses modern institutional procedure.

A third is sustained learning. In Mark 8, Jesus asks the disciples to remember and reconsider. In John, abiding in the teaching and doing truth are joined to knowing. Study, prayer, practice, and attention to their consequences can correct one another. Neither a claim of spiritual insight nor a claim of textual authority should make a teacher's conduct unanswerable. [RD-02](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-02--the-disciples-are-not-exempt), [B-09–B-11](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-09)

These are possible antidotes, not conditions we may impose on people who have been harmed. A boundary against further abuse is not evidence of rigidity. Nor can we assume that everyone can become receptive by an act of will. Paul's story, considered below, allows encounter and the Spirit to interrupt an understanding that did not initiate its own correction.

The proposal is therefore limited but consequential. While healing is treated as an enemy, it cannot be welcomed as healing. If that relation changes, what happens to the condition named by the warning? Mark does not explicitly supply this conditional reading, and neither his temporal language nor Matthew's judgment sayings can be set aside. Mark 4:10–12 also complicates a simple account of voluntary openness. These are questions the proposal must face, not obstacles to hide. [RD-11](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-11--hard-evidence-the-proposal-must-retain)

The antidote to rigidity may not be a lack of conviction. It may be a form of fidelity that remains answerable to truth, to those affected by its claims, and to the possibility of correction. That requirement must apply to this essay's interpretation as well.

## 5. The humanity of transmission — section to develop

Matthew retains the controversy but explicitly excludes forgiveness in both this age and the coming age. Luke places the saying among instructions about acknowledgment, denial, and the Spirit’s help before authorities. These differences show interpretation within the Gospel tradition itself. They do not, without further argument, establish which words Jesus originally spoke or which Gospel invented an element. [B-03–B-04](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-03)

A teaching comes through a human teacher, hearers, rememberers, communities, writers, and readers. This need not be described as a story of degeneration. Human participation can preserve insight as well as change it. Even Catholic teaching that affirms Gospel truth recognizes the evangelists’ selection, synthesis, and explanation of inherited material. [R-09](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-09)

**Question to develop:** What would it mean to take Jesus’s humanity seriously without making him either a flawless modern projection or a person whom we confidently diagnose across two millennia? His historical situatedness and his divinity are not resolved merely by defining a Greek word. The essay may propose that humanity is a medium of revelation rather than its opposite, while acknowledging that stronger claims about fallibility are theological judgments.

## 6. The tradition’s differing answers — researched scaffolding

The earlier interpreters do not give one answer. The Didache applies the warning to judging speech believed to come from the Spirit, while also testing prophets by conduct. Chrysostom acknowledges forgiveness after repentance, but surrounds this acknowledgment with severe punishment language and anti-Jewish polemic. Augustine identifies the decisive condition as final impenitence and cautions against despairing of a living person. Aquinas distinguishes rejection of the means of pardon from a limit on divine power. Calvin understands the completed sin as irreversible hardening. [R-01–R-05](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-01)

Modern Catholic teaching locates the problem in refusal of mercy. MacDonald questions whether spiritual closure must be final and envisages a remedial rather than merely retributive divine response. His discussion of a possible age of unforgiven sin occurs in a different sermon from the famous statement rejecting a “fixed” condition. [R-06–R-08](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-06)

**To expand:** What does each interpretation add? An explicit “while”? A boundary at death? A distinction between ignorance and recognized truth? A doctrine of reprobation? A universal restorative purpose? These additions may be defensible, but they should remain visible as additions.

## 7. Paul and the possibility of changed seeing — central argument

Paul puts pressure on any account that treats deep religious misrecognition as necessarily incurable. In Galatians he describes trying to destroy the church and being intensely zealous for ancestral traditions before a revelation changed his direction. Philippians places the descriptions “Pharisee” and persecutor beside one another. These are not portraits of mild uncertainty. They concern active opposition with consequences for others. [B-05–B-06](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-05)

Acts gives the transformation a narrative involving blindness, regained sight, and the Holy Spirit. First Timothy presents Paul as a former blasphemer who received mercy, while expressly distinguishing his earlier ignorance and unbelief. These witnesses should not be flattened into one autobiographical statement: Acts is a narrative about Paul, and the authorship of 1 Timothy is disputed. They nevertheless form part of the Christian canon’s account of transformation. [B-07–B-08](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-07)

Paul does not prove that someone who committed the exact act condemned in Mark was subsequently forgiven. No source places him in that scene or attributes that accusation to him. The distinction between ignorant opposition and knowing rejection also deserves attention. But his life makes a broader claim difficult to sustain: that sincere, destructive opposition to what will later be recognized as God’s work must seal a person permanently against recognition.

The question is therefore not whether Paul cancels Mark. It is whether the two must be interpreted together. What can the Spirit do in a person who has been acting against the community that claims to live by that Spirit? Does the possibility of receiving a new understanding belong only to those whose earlier misunderstanding was comparatively mild?

Here is the interpretive proposal to test: if we refuse to see, we do not see. If healing is named as evil, it cannot be welcomed as healing. The difficulty is not necessarily that mercy confronts a repentant person and turns that person away. It may be that the person’s relation to mercy is itself one of repudiation.

But the proposal needs a boundary. Mark does not explicitly say “only while refusal continues.” The ordinary force of his temporal expression is stronger, and Matthew’s reference to both ages is substantial counterevidence. Calling refusal a condition rather than an essence does not prove that the condition will change. Nor should inability, trauma, uncertainty, or disagreement automatically be renamed refusal.

Even with these qualifications, a distinction remains worth investigating. “This condition rejects the means of healing” and “this condition can never be transformed” are different propositions. Moving from the first to the second requires more than observing present resistance. Aquinas’s discussion is relevant precisely because it distinguishes resistance to a remedy from a limit on what God can do. [R-04](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-04)

We might therefore ask: does the warning describe a person’s enduring relation to what they continue to malign, or pronounce that no future encounter with grace could change that relation? What warrants the second conclusion? What warrants denying it? Paul does not answer every question, but he requires that the possibility of transformation remain part of the inquiry.

## 8. Time and practice — bridge paragraphs

The Plum Village understanding that past, present, and future inter-are offers a comparative resource here. Sister Annabel Laity explicitly connects it with changing how one acts in the present in response to what one regrets in the past. This is not evidence for the semantics of aiōn. It is a way of asking whether continuation must mean immutability. A past action can remain consequential without constituting the whole identity or future of its agent. [PV-04](../sources/plum-village/time-and-transformation.md#pv-04)

Present transformation does not undo another person’s suffering. It does not oblige that person to forgive, reconcile, or restore trust. But neither does accountability require that a human being be understood as incapable of becoming different. This is the constructive use of the comparison, not a claim that Greek eschatology and Buddhist time are secretly the same.

John offers a related but distinct question about lived knowing. Abiding in Jesus’s word is joined to knowing truth and freedom. The discipleship clause is present tense—“you are truly my disciples”—not a promised intermediate stage of becoming a disciple. John 7:17 connects willingness to do God’s will with discernment about the teaching; John 3:21 speaks of doing truth. These passages do not authorize experience independent of Jesus’s revelation, but they do resist reducing discipleship to possession of propositions. [B-09–B-11](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-09)

**Still to write:** develop the relationship between John 9's claimed sight and section 4's rigidity proposal without repeating that discussion; distinguish eschatological duration, conceptual models of time, and ethical continuity. No inference about nonlinear time follows merely from the absence of chronos in Mark.

## 9. An open conclusion

A responsible reading may end without dissolving the tension. Mark’s wording is severe. It cannot simply be translated into a temporary obstacle because that interpretation is more congenial. Yet neither the word “sin” nor the phrase “forever” arrives without a history. Translation gives us necessary constraints, not a complete theology of divine action.

The human character of the tradition becomes important at this point. Jesus as remembered, the evangelists who arrange the memory, the theologians who seek coherence, and the readers who inherit their language do not occupy a position outside interpretation. Their humanity is not a reason to dismiss them. It is a reason to attend to the work each is doing and to acknowledge that our own preferred reading also has conditions and consequences.

We can name those consequences without making an enemy of the people who transmitted the teaching. A narrow interpretation can cause suffering even when sincerely held. A generous interpretation can also cause suffering when it is used to silence a harmed person or bypass accountability. The criterion cannot simply be which reading comforts us. It must include what the text permits, what history supports, what our reasoning adds, and what our practice produces.

The provisional thesis is modest: Mark’s warning does not arrive as a finished treatise on everything forgiveness can mean. It stands within a story of profound misrecognition and has generated differing accounts of repentance and divine mercy. One interpretation understands the unrelieved condition as resistance to release itself. Whether that resistance can ultimately be transformed remains a question that the severity of Mark, the life of Paul, and the history of Christian interpretation require us to keep examining.

To find the humanity of Jesus and his interpreters need not mean abandoning the possibility of revelation. It may mean refusing to separate truth from the human lives through which it is spoken, heard, enacted, and sometimes misunderstood. Whether that fuller humanity also discloses divinity is a question to live and study, not a result that can be extracted from an etymology.

The question with which we began therefore remains, but it is more precise. What is unforgivable: a past act that permanently determines a person, an ongoing relation of resistance, or a refusal whose finality belongs to a horizon we do not fully understand? And when someone begins to see what they once opposed, what has changed in the relation named by the warning?

We need not pretend that all these possibilities are equally supported. We also need not decide that the only faithful response is to stop asking. The text, its interpreters, and our own responsibilities remain before us.
