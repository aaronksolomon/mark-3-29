# All Will Be Forgiven?
## Words, Time, and the Humanity of Interpretation

> *“Truly I tell you, people can be forgiven all their sins and every slander they utter, but whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit will never be forgiven; they are guilty of an eternal sin.”*
>
> — Mark 3:28–29, New International Version (2011). [RD-00](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-00--niv-opening)

## 1. A tension before an answer

In one movement, this saying opens forgiveness across the range of human sin and slander, then names a stark and apparently permanent exception. What distinguishes the second saying from the first? Is Jesus, as Mark presents him, identifying a particular act whose consequences cannot be overcome, or a condition in which the very possibility of forgiveness is refused? The words themselves do not offer an easy answer. There is a need for us to look deeply and consider what kind of teaching this is. Paradox and tension are not unfamiliar in the Gospel narratives. The severity of the words does not relieve us of the need to interpret them. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

Mark gives the warning a specific setting. The scribes have attributed Jesus’s liberating work to an unclean power. Mark explains: “Because they were saying, ‘He has an unclean spirit’” (3:30, study rendering). One possibility is that the accusation reveals a rigidity of perception: a judgment so defended that what might challenge or heal it is itself named harmful. Blasphemy names the maligning of the Spirit; rigidity is our proposal about the condition sustaining that accusation. Could the judgment prevent the very recognition through which it might change? This concerns interpreters as well as the people in the story. Can our religious understanding become so fixed that defending it takes precedence over recognizing the good before us? At what point does conviction become dogmatism?

We bring our own histories to these words. Sin may suggest moral failure, inherited corruption, broken relationship, or violation of divine or human law. Forgiveness may suggest pardon, release from debt, relinquished resentment, or reconciliation. Guilt can mean being answerable for an act or feeling guilty about it. Forever may suggest an endless succession of moments. These notions and associations are not necessarily interchangeable. Which are appropriate here, and which do we supply without noticing?

The inquiry therefore concerns both the saying and the human work of receiving and understanding it. What happens when words attributed to Jesus in a particular conflict are remembered, arranged within a Gospel, translated, preached, and incorporated into doctrine? Respect for Jesus does not require us to settle his intentions beforehand. Could severe words themselves be a warning offered out of compassion? What in the story supports that possibility, and what remains difficult? Respect for scripture need not require that every reported sentence be treated as a complete theological account detached from its occasion. Jesus, the evangelists, and their later readers belong to this inquiry as human participants, whose words arise within real lives and circumstances.

### The two clauses

**Greek:** πάντα ἀφεθήσεται — *panta aphethēsetai*.

**Segmented gloss:** πάντα, all things; ἀφεθήσεται, will be forgiven.

**Natural English:** “All will be forgiven,” with the following words specifying human sins and blasphemies. This is not, by itself, an assertion that every individual will eventually be reconciled. The NIV lead-in uses “can be forgiven”; the study gloss here makes the Greek future passive visible without silently altering the named translation.

**Greek:** οὐκ ἔχει ἄφεσιν εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα — *ouk echei aphesin eis ton aiōna*.

**Segmented gloss:** οὐκ, not; ἔχει, has; ἄφεσιν, forgiveness or release; εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, into/unto the age.

**Natural English:** “Never has forgiveness” or “has no forgiveness forever.” “Into the age” is a mechanical gloss, not better idiomatic English. It does not say “until the end of the age.” [B-01–B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-01)

Both clauses deserve to be heard. The breadth of the first does not make the second disappear. The severity of the second does not prevent us from looking deeply into meaning, intention, setting, and subsequent interpretation.

## 2. Words moving through worlds — section to develop

The most useful starting point is not a contrast between an innocent ancient meaning and a punitive modern one. Ancient religious language could already be juridical. The word translated “guilty,” enochos, can express liability; “offense” is a possible English gloss for hamartēma, although it is not a separate noun in Mark’s sentence. Conversely, Mark’s preferred critical reading says “eternal sin,” not explicitly “eternal punishment.” The distinction matters without abolishing judgment. [L-02](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-02), [L-04](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-04)

**Develop with a small historical corpus:** Greek error and wrongdoing vocabulary; Jewish scriptural uses of burden, debt, covenant, pardon, and judgment; the Septuagint; later reception; contemporary English. Consult Anderson and Barr before proposing a trajectory. The present notes do not yet establish a comprehensive semantic history.

The wider range of aphesis includes release and remission. That makes “release” a useful question to bring to Mark, but not permission to replace forgiveness with a purely psychological process. Nor does the present tense “does not have” cancel the accompanying “forever.” A sound reading must hold the syntax together. [L-03](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-03), [claim audit](../notes/claim-audit.md)

## 3. What the story names

Mark supplies a reason for the warning: the scribes were saying that Jesus had an unclean spirit. Within the story, what Jesus is doing overcomes destructive power; the accusation attributes that work to the ruler of demons. The issue is not simply an irreverent word. It is a reversal in the recognition of what is taking place. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

**Greek:** ὅτι ἔλεγον· Πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον ἔχει — *hoti elegon: pneuma akatharton echei*.

**Segmented gloss:** ὅτι, because; ἔλεγον, they were saying; πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον, an unclean spirit; ἔχει, he has.

**Natural English:** “Because they were saying, ‘He has an unclean spirit.’”

Jesus answers the accusation through images: a divided kingdom, a divided house, and a strong man who must be bound before his house can be plundered. The images ask the hearers to reconsider their explanation of what is happening. They do not remove the distinction between liberation and destructive power; they challenge the way that distinction is being applied. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

This brings the warning closer to a recognizable human danger: we may reject something good because receiving it would unsettle our understanding. Mark’s perspective is not, however, a transcript of the historical opponents’ minds. The story does not explicitly tell us that they privately recognized divine action and knowingly lied about it. Neither should we assume that sincerity makes harmful judgment harmless.

**Still to develop:** the place of forgiveness within God’s reign, and the family and disciples in Mark’s larger scene. The following section develops the hardening comparison without equating narrative opponents with Jewish people or religious teachers as a class.

## 4. Rigidity and dogmatism: when conviction prevents recognition

### A condition, not a class of people

Rigidity here does not mean having convictions, belonging to a tradition, or holding firmly to a moral obligation. It means protecting a judgment from correction: refusing to let what happens, what another person says, or what our actions produce call that judgment into question. The problem is not simply that we have an interpretation. It is that our interpretation has become exempt from examination.

Dogmatism gives this rigidity a doctrinal form. A formulation is treated not as an understanding that must remain answerable to life, but as the final measure by which every encounter is judged. What fails to fit can then be dismissed before it is heard. The problem need not lie in the existence of doctrine. It lies in our attachment to it, and in what that attachment permits us to disregard.

The First of the Order of Interbeing’s Fourteen Mindfulness Trainings, Openness, addresses this danger directly. It includes Buddhist teachings within its warning against attachment to doctrine and commits practitioners to transforming dogmatism. The Second Training develops nonattachment to views through listening and continuing inquiry. These are strong commitments, not the abandonment of guidance. They offer a practice for keeping a teaching open to the understanding it is meant to cultivate. [ND-01](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-01--openness-and-nonattachment-to-views)

Mark offers a nearby point of contact. Earlier in the same chapter, Jesus asks whether the Sabbath permits doing good and saving life. The response is silence. Mark describes Jesus as angry and grieving at the opponents’ hardness of heart before he restores the man’s hand. [RD-01](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-01--hardness-before-the-blasphemy-controversy)

**Greek:** συλλυπούμενος ἐπὶ τῇ πωρώσει τῆς καρδίας αὐτῶν.

**Romanization:** *syllypoumenos epi tē pōrōsei tēs kardias autōn*.

**Segmented gloss:** συλλυπούμενος, grieving; ἐπὶ τῇ πωρώσει, at the hardness/hardening; τῆς καρδίας, of the heart; αὐτῶν, of them/their.

**Natural English:** “grieved at their hardness of heart,” or “grieving over their hardened hearts” (Mark 3:5).

Hardness of heart and blasphemy are not thereby established as synonyms. But the chapter presents a recurring question: what happens when an established judgment prevents a person from responding to restoration? In the later controversy, the accusation can protect itself against correction. What the story presents as evidence of liberation becomes evidence of demonic power. Could this be the condition that gives the warning its force?

Mark also attributes hardened hearts to the disciples (6:52; 8:17). Jesus questions their understanding and memory and continues to teach them. Hardening is not a quality that divides Jewish opponents from Christian insiders. It can describe those who follow Jesus. Its presence does not automatically mean that a person is past all possibility of response. [RD-02](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-02--the-disciples-are-not-exempt)

John offers a related comparison. At the end of the healing story in John 9, the assertion of sight is connected with sin’s remaining. The problem concerns a claim to understanding in the face of what the story discloses. Yet John’s opening also rejects a simple attribution of the man’s physical blindness to sin. We should not turn disability, uncertainty, or lack of capacity into moral failure. [RD-03](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-03--claimed-sight-and-remaining-sin)

### When religious profession displaces care

This possibility connects Mark’s warning to a wider tension between professed allegiance and enacted faithfulness. In Matthew 7:21–23, calling Jesus Lord is not enough. Those rejected even appeal to prophecy, exorcisms, and powerful deeds performed in his name. Religious language and impressive claims do not exempt a life from examination. The surrounding teaching asks readers to consider fruits, examine themselves, and act on what they hear. A claimed healing is not, simply because it is claimed, proof of a teacher’s goodness or authority. [RD-04](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-04--profession-claimed-power-and-conduct)

Matthew 25:31–46 makes care for hungry, displaced, sick, and imprisoned people decisive within a scene of judgment. Its identification of the least brothers is disputed, and its ending retains severe punishment language. Recognition and response to concrete need nevertheless belong at the center of the scene. Mark’s criticism of piety that exploits widows and James’s injunction to care for widows and orphans belong to related, but separate, passages. [RD-05](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-05--hunger-imprisonment-recognition-and-judgment), [RD-06](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-06--widows-exploitation-and-care)

These comparisons ask whether a religious identity can become a substitute for the life it claims to express. The question is already present within Jewish scripture: Isaiah 58 joins acceptable fasting to the release of the oppressed and provision for people in need. It would distort this inheritance to tell a story in which Christianity supplies compassion to a Judaism that lacked it. [RD-07](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-07--the-ethical-criticism-is-already-jewish)

### A warning to the interpreters

The most difficult application is inward. If we read Mark only as a warning about other people’s resistance, we may reproduce the very pattern we are proposing to examine. A Christian interpreter can defend an account of goodness while refusing to hear the people harmed by its application. Even forgiveness can be used this way when it requires an injured person to cease speaking before responsibility has been acknowledged.

The history of Christian anti-Judaism makes this responsibility concrete. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum documents how inherited Christian prejudices and scriptural interpretations converged with Nazi racial antisemitism. It also identifies other political causes and records differences among complicity, accommodation, resistance, and rescue. Christian theology did not singly cause the Holocaust. But an account that excludes Christian teaching and conduct from its conditions is also inadequate. [RD-09](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-09--christian-interpretation-and-the-holocaust)

This history is not proof that a particular theological opponent has committed the unforgivable sin. Nor should Jewish suffering become merely an illustration in a Christian account of self-improvement. The question it presses upon Christian interpreters is more demanding: when an inherited reading helps us disregard another person’s dignity or suffering, what would allow that reading to be questioned? What practices and institutions enable correction before harm is repeated?

The alternative need not be abandonment of doctrine. In Nostra aetate, the Second Vatican Council rejected collective blame for Jesus’s death and the presentation of Jews as divinely rejected or accursed. Authoritative teaching can also restrict harmful interpretation. That does not erase earlier injury or establish that the work is finished; it shows that fidelity and correction need not be opposites. [RD-10](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-10--authoritative-teaching-can-also-correct-harmful-interpretation)

### What might interrupt rigidity?

The texts suggest practices rather than a formula. Self-examination comes before correcting another person in Matthew 7:1–5. Luke introduces the Pharisee and tax collector’s prayer as a challenge to confidence in one’s righteousness joined to contempt for others. The reader is asked to relinquish an exemption from correction, not exchange one supposedly inferior group for another. [RD-04](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-04--profession-claimed-power-and-conduct), [RD-08](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-08--practices-that-may-interrupt-rigidity)

Care is another interruption. In Luke 10, Jesus and the legal expert begin with the commandments to love God and neighbor. The Samaritan story then shifts attention from deciding who qualifies as our neighbor toward recognizing who acts as a neighbor. Mercy is enacted across a boundary, within a conversation already grounded in Jewish scripture. [ND-06](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-06--the-neighbor-and-worship-beyond-rival-locations)

Mark supplies an even closer comparison. The disciples try to stop someone casting out demons in Jesus’s name because he does not follow their group (9:38–40). Jesus tells them not to stop him. Here it is the disciples whose sense of belonging threatens to obstruct a healing. The story does not abolish discernment; it challenges the assumption that legitimate action must remain within our circle. [ND-05](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-05--a-healing-outside-the-disciples-group)

These encounters give us a reason to listen across the boundaries of our own tradition. We can value where a teaching comes from without making its origin the test of its truth. A Buddhist teaching may help us hear Mark differently; Mark may question what we believe we understand through Buddhism. What matters is what becomes visible through the encounter, and whether it can bear further attention in study and life.

Sustained learning belongs here too. In Mark 8, Jesus asks the disciples to remember and reconsider. In John, abiding in the teaching and doing truth are joined to knowing. Study, prayer, practice, and attention to consequences can correct one another. Listening to someone harmed by an institution must include the possibility of changing its practices, not merely receiving testimony while leaving everything unchanged. [RD-02](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-02--the-disciples-are-not-exempt), [B-09–B-11](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-09)

These are possible antidotes, not demands to place on people who have been harmed. A boundary against further abuse is not evidence of dogmatism. Nor can everyone become receptive by an act of will. Paul’s story allows encounter and the Spirit to interrupt an understanding that did not initiate its own correction.

The proposal remains open: while healing is treated as an enemy, it cannot be welcomed as healing. If that relation changes, what happens to the condition named by the warning? Mark’s emphatic language, Matthew’s extension to both ages, and Mark 4:10–12’s difficult account of seeing and forgiveness remain part of this question. [RD-11](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-11--hard-evidence-the-proposal-must-retain)

The antidote to rigidity may not be a lack of conviction. It may be a form of fidelity that remains answerable to truth, to those affected by its claims, and to the possibility of correction. That requirement applies to this essay’s interpretation as well.

## 5. The humanity of transmission

Matthew retains the controversy but explicitly excludes forgiveness in both this age and the coming age. Luke places the saying among instructions about acknowledgment, denial, and the Spirit’s help before authorities. These differences show interpretation within the Gospel tradition itself. They do not, without further argument, establish which words Jesus originally spoke or which Gospel introduced an element. [B-03–B-04](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-03)

A saying does something within a relationship. It can warn, rebuke, invite, unsettle, or console. Its meaning is not exhausted by deciding which proposition it asserts. A severe warning may seek to interrupt a course of action; a later reader may receive the same words as a classification of people whose destiny is already fixed. Could this difference matter here? To ask is not to decide that Mark intended only a temporary condition. It is to consider what changes when speech addressed to someone becomes doctrine pronounced about someone.

A teaching comes through a human teacher, hearers, rememberers, communities, writers, and readers. This need not be a story of degeneration. Human participation can preserve insight as well as change it. Dei verbum, while affirming the truth of the Gospels, recognizes the evangelists’ selection, synthesis, and explanation of inherited material. Interpretation is not only what later readers do to an already completed object. It participates in the form in which the teaching reaches us. [R-09](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-09)

Jesus’s humanity belongs within this inquiry too. We need not protect him from every difficult saying by assigning it to someone else. Neither can we confidently reconstruct his thoughts, intentions, or mistakes across two millennia. We can attend to the ways the Gospels present him speaking and acting: within relationships, through stories and arguments, in response to particular lives. Perhaps humanity is not an obstacle through which revelation must somehow pass untouched. Perhaps it is the medium through which revelation becomes available to us. What would this allow us to see about the relationship between Jesus’s humanity and his divinity?

## 6. The tradition’s differing answers — researched scaffolding

The earlier interpreters do not give one answer. The Didache applies the warning to judging speech believed to come from the Spirit, while also testing prophets by conduct. Chrysostom acknowledges forgiveness after repentance, but surrounds this acknowledgment with severe punishment language and anti-Jewish polemic. Augustine identifies the decisive condition as final impenitence and cautions against despairing of a living person. Aquinas distinguishes rejection of the means of pardon from a limit on divine power. Calvin understands the completed sin as irreversible hardening. [R-01–R-05](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-01)

Modern Catholic teaching locates the problem in refusal of mercy. MacDonald questions whether spiritual closure must be final and envisages a remedial rather than merely retributive divine response. His discussion of a possible age of unforgiven sin occurs in a different sermon from the famous statement rejecting a “fixed” condition. [R-06–R-08](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-06)

**To expand:** What does each interpretation add? An explicit “while”? A boundary at death? A distinction between ignorance and recognized truth? A doctrine of reprobation? A universal restorative purpose? These additions may be defensible, but they should remain visible as additions.

## 7. Paul and the possibility of changed seeing — central argument

Paul puts pressure on any account that treats deep religious misrecognition as necessarily incurable. In Galatians he describes trying to destroy the church and being intensely zealous for ancestral traditions before a revelation changed his direction. Philippians places the descriptions “Pharisee” and persecutor beside one another. These are not portraits of mild uncertainty. They concern active opposition with consequences for others. [B-05–B-06](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-05)

Acts gives the transformation a narrative involving blindness, regained sight, and the Holy Spirit. First Timothy presents Paul as a former blasphemer who received mercy, while expressly distinguishing his earlier ignorance and unbelief. These witnesses should not be flattened into one autobiographical statement: Acts is a narrative about Paul, and the authorship of 1 Timothy is disputed. They nevertheless form part of the Christian canon’s account of transformation. [B-07–B-08](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-07)

Paul does not prove that someone who committed the exact act condemned in Mark was subsequently forgiven. No source places him in that scene or attributes that accusation to him. The distinction between ignorant opposition and knowing rejection also deserves attention. But his life makes a broader claim difficult to sustain: that destructive opposition to what will later be recognized as God’s work must seal a person permanently against recognition.

The question is therefore not whether Paul cancels Mark. It is whether the two must be interpreted together. What can the Spirit do in a person who has been acting against the community that claims to live by that Spirit? Does the possibility of receiving a new understanding belong only to those whose earlier misunderstanding was comparatively mild?

Here is the interpretive proposal to test: if we refuse to see, we do not see. If healing is named as evil, it cannot be welcomed as healing. The difficulty is not necessarily that mercy confronts a repentant person and turns that person away. It may be that the person’s relation to mercy is itself one of repudiation.

Mark does not explicitly say “only while refusal continues.” His temporal expression is stronger, and Matthew’s reference to both ages is substantial counterevidence. Calling refusal a condition rather than an essence does not prove that the condition will change. Nor should inability, trauma, uncertainty, or disagreement automatically be renamed refusal.

Even so, “this condition rejects the means of healing” and “this condition can never be transformed” are different propositions. Moving from the first to the second requires more than observing present resistance. Aquinas’s discussion is relevant precisely because it distinguishes resistance to a remedy from a limit on what God can do. [R-04](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-04)

Does the warning describe a person’s enduring relation to what they continue to malign, or pronounce that no future encounter with grace could change that relation? What warrants the second conclusion? What warrants denying it? Paul does not answer every question, but he requires that transformation remain part of the inquiry.

## 8. Holding the teaching without fixing the person

### Reasoning and what a formulation leaves out

Dogmatism can attach not only to a doctrine but to a way of describing reality. We may assume that a person must be placed in one settled category, or that a sentence spoken in one circumstance carries an identical force in every other. Before asking whether our reasoning is correct, we may need to ask what its terms have already assumed.

This is not a choice between logic and insight. An inference can be valid while its premises or description remain inadequate. Logic can also expose what we have added: present refusal does not entail permanent refusal without a further premise about the future. Conversely, openness alone does not logically guarantee forgiveness. Mark’s language may be understood to supply a claim of finality; whether it should be understood that way remains the question. Reasoning helps us locate the question rather than replacing it. [ND-08](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-08--logic-and-the-choice-of-a-description)

### Two truths, one life

Thích Nhất Hạnh’s teaching offers a way to examine this attachment to descriptions. In a 2006 interview, he approaches relative and ultimate truth through historical and ultimate dimensions, illustrated by wave and water. He also resists treating interbeing as the claim that everything is simply one. Neither absolute sameness nor complete separation exhausts what can be seen. [ND-02](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-02--historical-and-ultimate-neither-monism-nor-separation)

Applied here, this lets us distinguish without turning distinctions into permanent divisions. The person who caused harm remains answerable for it. The person is also not exhausted by that act. We need neither identify someone wholly with a past action nor imagine that transformation produces an unrelated person with no responsibility for the past. A nondual approach can hold continuity and change together. It does not require harm and healing to become indistinguishable.

Plum Village teaching likewise brings the three times and the two truths together. Sister Annabel Laity connects present practice with how we live in relation to what we regret in the past. The past remains consequential; the future is being formed through how we respond now. This gives us another way to consider Paul’s continuity with his earlier life, without making that life a fixed identity. [PV-04](../sources/plum-village/time-and-transformation.md#pv-04), [ND-03](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-03--the-times-and-the-truths)

The conventional and ultimate are not two compartments in which responsibility is first acknowledged and then dismissed. No appeal to nonduality can require a harmed person to reconcile or erase the distinction between what happened and what we hope can change. Our question is whether the human being is more than a fixed description, not whether the description of harm is dispensable.

### A question that includes the reader

John offers a point of contact in Jesus’s prayer that his followers may be one: the Father in Jesus, Jesus in the Father, and the disciples in them (17:20–23). This language of mutual indwelling describes communion without simply eliminating relationship. It can be read alongside nondual teaching, while retaining John’s particular account of God, Jesus, and discipleship. What might such language allow us to experience that the image of wholly separate beings leaves out? [ND-07](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-07--unity-without-erasing-relation)

Thích Nhất Hạnh’s letter “Bat Nha: a Koan” offers another connection. Addressing a crisis of persecution, he asks the practitioner to encounter the question as something involving their own life, not merely a puzzle concerning other people. The work of understanding includes how the question is lived. [ND-04](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-04--a-koan-involving-the-reader)

Mark’s warning can be approached in this koan-like way without calling it a Zen koan. We study what the words mean, but also allow them to question the one studying. What would we refuse to recognize in order to preserve our interpretation? Could defending forgiveness itself become a way of refusing to listen? Could a warning about the refusal of truth become an invitation to see that refusal in ourselves?

This does not make the opening promise and its exception two interchangeable truths, or remove Mark’s severity. It changes our relation to the tension. We no longer need to stand outside it as though its only purpose were to classify others.

John’s language of abiding, knowing, and doing truth belongs here too (8:31–36; 7:17; 3:21). It joins understanding with a life in relation to Jesus and his teaching. The inquiry is not finished when we possess a satisfactory formulation. We still have to discover what that understanding becomes in practice. [B-09–B-11](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-09)

## 9. An open conclusion

We may end without dissolving the tension. Mark’s wording is severe. It cannot simply be translated into a temporary obstacle because that interpretation is more congenial. Yet neither the word “sin” nor the phrase “forever” arrives without a history. Translation gives us necessary constraints, not a complete theology of divine action.

Jesus as remembered, the evangelists who arrange the memory, the theologians who seek coherence, and the readers who inherit their language do not occupy a position outside interpretation. Their humanity is not a reason to dismiss them. It is a reason to attend to their lives and circumstances, and to acknowledge that our preferred reading also has conditions and consequences.

We can name those consequences without making an enemy of the people who transmitted the teaching. A narrow interpretation can cause suffering even when sincerely held. A generous interpretation can also cause suffering when it silences a harmed person or bypasses accountability. The criterion cannot simply be which reading comforts us. It must include what the text permits, what history supports, what our reasoning adds, and what our practice produces.

The provisional thesis remains: Mark’s warning does not arrive as a finished treatise on everything forgiveness can mean. It stands within a story of profound misrecognition and has generated differing accounts of repentance and divine mercy. One interpretation understands the unrelieved condition as resistance to release itself. Whether that resistance can ultimately be transformed is a question that the severity of Mark, the life of Paul, and the history of interpretation require us to keep examining.

Buddhist teaching can participate fully in that inquiry. It can help us question the permanence we attribute to a person or a view, and the separation we assume between ourselves and those we judge. It does not need to make Mark say something else. Its contribution may be to change how we attend to what he says, and what we notice in ourselves as we read.

To find the humanity of Jesus and his interpreters need not mean abandoning revelation. Perhaps it means refusing to separate truth from the human lives through which it is spoken, heard, enacted, and sometimes misunderstood. Could this fuller humanity itself disclose something of divinity—not by erasing the human, but through the relationships in which a life is given and received?

The question with which we began therefore remains, but it is more precise. What is unforgivable: a past act that permanently determines a person, an ongoing relation of resistance, or a refusal whose finality belongs to a horizon we do not fully understand? When someone begins to see what they once opposed, what has changed in the relation named by the warning? And what would it mean for us to remain open to that change?

We need not pretend that all these readings are equally supported. Neither need we decide that faithfulness requires us to stop asking. The text, its interpreters, and the lives affected by our interpretations remain before us.
