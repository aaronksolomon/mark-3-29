# All Will Be Forgiven?
## Compassion, Warning, and our Shared Humanity

> *“Truly I tell you, people can be forgiven all their sins and every slander they utter, but whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit will never be forgiven; they are guilty of an eternal sin.”*
>
> — Mark 3:28–29, New International Version (2011). [RD-00](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-00--niv-opening)

## 1. Hearing the warning

In one movement, this saying opens forgiveness across the range of human sin and slander, then names a stark exception. Read as two detached statements, the words seem to pull us in opposite directions. Heard within an encounter, they may do something more: the breadth of the first makes the warning that follows all the more startling. Against the possibility of forgiveness for so much, Jesus places the gravity of what his hearers are saying. The contrast may be part of the force with which he is trying to reach them. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

The warning can be heard as a sharp act of compassion, an effort to awaken people whose certainty has closed off listening. Jesus uses the language of judgment to confront the judgment being made about his work. The danger is not an abstract error about holiness: the Spirit through whom healing and forgiveness come is being treated as an enemy. The severity draws attention to what that rejection means. A concern to heal and restore need not always sound reassuring; here it may take the form of an urgent call to hear what one is saying.

We bring our own histories to these words. Forgiveness may suggest pardon, release, or reconciliation; judgment may suggest moral discernment or condemnation. Jesus and those who heard him also spoke within a world of inherited meanings, loyalties, and disputes. A saying addressed to particular people carries that setting with it, even when it reaches far beyond the occasion. What happens when a specific expression in a lived moment becomes a judgment we pronounce about someone, or a group?

A teaching from another tradition can make familiar assumptions visible. Buddhist practice brings a particular question to the encounter: what happens when we become attached not only to an opinion but to our sense of possessing the truth? The Order of Interbeing’s commitment to nonattachment includes its own teachings, “even Buddhist ones.” Such an encounter can deepen understanding without requiring the traditions to become identical. Jewish interpretation has an additional, indispensable place: it returns us to Jesus’s own religious inheritance and to a living tradition that continues to think about repentance and mercy on its own terms. [ND-01](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-01--openness-and-nonattachment-to-views), [JC-01–JC-07](../sources/jewish-context-and-cross-tradition-inquiry.md#jc-01)

The Gospels themselves unsettle the assumption that recognition belongs only within one’s group. Jesus responds to a centurion’s trust with astonishment: “I have not found anyone in Israel with such great faith” (Matthew 8:10, NIV). In Mark 7:24–30, a non-Jewish woman’s reply is heard within an exchange that begins with a difficult image of children and dogs; her daughter is freed from the demon. These encounters do not erase religious differences. They show people meeting across them, where a person outside the familiar circle can disclose something that those within it have not seen. [JC-08](../sources/jewish-context-and-cross-tradition-inquiry.md#jc-08)

## 2. A teacher among people

### The story before the saying

Mark’s account begins with the Spirit descending upon Jesus, followed by his proclamation that “The kingdom of God has come near” (1:15, NIV). Teaching, healing, and release from impure spirits give that proclamation a visible form. People gather around him; he also withdraws to pray. From the beginning, the Gospel presents divine activity through a life moving among other lives, not through a list of propositions delivered outside any human setting. [HT-01](../sources/humanity-and-provocative-teaching.md#ht-01)

Questions develop around what this activity means. When Jesus forgives a paralyzed man, some teachers of the law think, “He’s blaspheming!” (2:7, NIV). His meals with people identified as sinners, and his disciples’ conduct on the Sabbath, bring further challenges. Jesus responds through questions and images, including the physician who attends the sick and new wine that cannot be contained in old wineskins (2:17–22). Jesus and those questioning him share a Jewish scriptural world in which mercy, law, and faithfulness already matter deeply. Their disputes concern how that inheritance is being lived. [HT-01](../sources/humanity-and-provocative-teaching.md#ht-01)

Sabbath observance belongs to that inheritance. In the Torah it is holy rest, extending to servants, resident foreigners, and animals; Deuteronomy also recalls liberation from slavery. Determining what counted as prohibited work required interpretation. When chapter 3 opens with a man whose hand needs healing, some of those present watch to see whether Jesus will heal on the Sabbath so that they can accuse him. The scene concerns the application of a commandment, not a simple opposition between care and a Judaism that forbids care. Jesus asks about doing good and saving life; the response is silence. Mark describes him as angry and “deeply distressed at their stubborn hearts” (3:5, NIV), then shows him restoring the man’s hand. [JC-01](../sources/jewish-context-and-cross-tradition-inquiry.md#jc-01), [RD-01](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-01--hardness-before-the-blasphemy-controversy)

Later, the crowd becomes so demanding that Jesus and those with him cannot eat, and his family comes to take charge of him (3:20–21). Even those closest to him struggle to understand what is happening. The pressure of these encounters reaches into family and community as well as religious debate. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

### Mercy and warning within the Jewish inheritance

Compassion does not enter this world as a correction from outside it. Exodus’s proclamation of the divine name speaks of “forgiving wickedness, rebellion and sin” and also says that God “does not leave the guilty unpunished” (34:7, NIV). Forgiveness and the seriousness of wrongdoing are already held together in the same sacred language. [JC-02](../sources/jewish-context-and-cross-tradition-inquiry.md#jc-02)

Ezekiel gives severe warning an explicitly life-seeking purpose. God declares, “I take no pleasure in the death of the wicked,” then calls, “Turn! Turn from your evil ways!” (33:11, NIV). The following verses connect turning with restitution and changed conduct. The warning names a real danger, and the call to change is equally real. This does not decide the meaning of Mark’s exception. It locates concern, judgment, and the possibility of turning within a Jewish scriptural inheritance that Jesus and his hearers share. [JC-03](../sources/jewish-context-and-cross-tradition-inquiry.md#jc-03)

### From accusation to warning

The teachers who arrive from Jerusalem give Jesus’s work a particularly damaging interpretation: “By the prince of demons he is driving out demons” (3:22, NIV). They associate his power with Beelzebul, the ruler of demons. They do not simply deny that something has happened. They name its source as evil. This is their accusation; the warning that follows is Jesus’s response. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

Jesus responds by calling them over and asking, “How can Satan drive out Satan?” (3:23, NIV). A divided kingdom or household cannot stand; taking a strong man’s possessions requires first overcoming him. These images challenge the accusation at its center. In Mark’s telling, Jesus is breaking the hold of destructive power, not serving it. He addresses those making the accusation with reasons and images through which they might reconsider. The exchange remains an effort at contact, even amid sharp disagreement.

Then the address becomes sharper. Jesus speaks of the breadth of forgiveness and immediately follows it with the warning about blaspheming against the Holy Spirit. Mark explains the connection: they were saying, “He has an impure spirit” (3:30, NIV). The contrast between the Holy Spirit and an impure spirit is crucial. What the Gospel presents as God’s liberating activity has been named as its opposite. The warning brings the seriousness of that judgment back to the people making it.

Seen in this light, the opening promise of forgiveness is not merely a reassuring statement that the next sentence interrupts; it serves to heighten the warning. The movement from familiar images to words of such severity can be heard as an urgent attempt to awaken the hearers. Mark records no reply between the argument and the warning. The rising intensity belongs to one continuing address, not a separately recorded failure of persuasion. [HT-01](../sources/humanity-and-provocative-teaching.md#ht-01)

There is a further reversal across these chapters. Earlier, Jesus is accused of blasphemy because he forgives. Now the language of judgment turns back upon the judgment directed against his work. Jesus and the healing he offers are being denounced as instruments of evil. His response can be heard as a compassionate wake-up call, using words his hearers take seriously to make them hear the seriousness of their own words. To oppose the Spirit of holiness is to turn against the very source of healing and forgiveness. So understood, the warning does more than classify a wrong: it confronts the rejection of what could bring release. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02), [R-02](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-02)

### A saying that unsettles its hearers

A saying does something within a relationship. It can warn, rebuke, invite, unsettle, or console; its meaning is not exhausted by identifying the proposition it asserts. A teacher may speak within familiar categories in order to expose what those categories have prevented someone from noticing. That possibility does not place Jesus outside his own culture. It asks how he works within it, addressing particular people rather than simply assigning them a place in a system.

Zen practice offers a related way of encountering difficult speech. A saying can interrupt the understanding with which we approach it, rather than supply an answer that leaves us unchanged. Thích Nhất Hạnh describes this involvement in *Bat Nha: a Koan*, where the question becomes one’s “own problem.” Applied here, the question is no longer only which other person falls under the warning. It includes the judgments through which we ourselves hear it. A conclusion about the teachers in the story may leave those judgments untouched; entering the question can make them visible. Jesus’s reply contains parables, and the severe saying belongs to that encounter. [ND-04](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-04--a-koan-involving-the-reader)

This hearing also has a Christian precedent. John Chrysostom, a late-fourth-century preacher in Antioch, in Roman Syria, describes Jesus’s purpose as “to do good to them that were sinning” (*Homily 41 on Matthew*, §1). Commenting on Matthew’s parallel, he hears correction within confrontation. His interpretation retains severe judgment, but concern is not confined to reassuring speech. [R-02](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-02), [HT-08](../sources/humanity-and-provocative-teaching.md#ht-08)

The human danger coming into view is not simply mistaken perception. It is clinging to a judgment so closely that we can no longer receive what might correct it. If liberation is named evil, liberation itself becomes something to resist. What could then be welcomed as healing? Mark does not explain the inner motives of those speaking, but his account makes this reversal of recognition visible. The possibility that such resistance can change remains a further question.

## 3. What the words keep open—and what they do not

Jesus’s warning responds to the judgment that his liberating work is evil. Careful study of the language keeps that setting in view. The Gospel of Mark is commonly dated around 70 CE and understood to have been composed in Greek, several decades after Jesus’s ministry. That is a date for composition, not for the surviving copies, and Greek composition is not the same as a transcript of an earlier spoken encounter. Earlier traditions lie behind the Gospel, but a lost Aramaic Gospel of Mark from which our text was translated has not been established. The text below follows the *SBL Greek New Testament*, edited by Michael W. Holmes. [B-01](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-01), [HT-07](../sources/humanity-and-provocative-teaching.md#ht-07), [JC-09](../sources/jewish-context-and-cross-tradition-inquiry.md#jc-09)

The human work of translation was already recognized within Jewish tradition. In the prologue to Ben Sira, a Jewish wisdom book also called Sirach, the translator observes that Hebrew expression “does not have exactly the same effect when translated into another language” (prologue, lines 21–22, NRSVUE). This is not a reason to abandon the words. It is a reason to study them in relation to the life, language, and practice through which they are transmitted. [JC-04](../sources/jewish-context-and-cross-tradition-inquiry.md#jc-04)

### The words of the passage

**Mark 3:28**

**Greek:** Ἀμὴν λέγω ὑμῖν ὅτι πάντα ἀφεθήσεται τοῖς υἱοῖς τῶν ἀνθρώπων, τὰ ἁμαρτήματα καὶ αἱ βλασφημίαι ὅσα ἐὰν βλασφημήσωσιν.

**Romanization:** *Amēn legō hymin hoti panta aphethēsetai tois huiois tōn anthrōpōn, ta hamartēmata kai hai blasphēmiai hosa ean blasphēmēsōsin.*

**Literal English:** “Truly I say to you that all [*panta*] will be forgiven [*aphethēsetai*] to the sons of human beings [humanity]—the sins and the blasphemies, whatever they may blaspheme.”

In typical English: “Truly I tell you, all human sins and whatever blasphemies people utter will be forgiven.” The Greek verb is “will be forgiven”; the NIV opening renders the statement “can be forgiven.” The entire movement matters, rather than any isolated phrase. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

**Mark 3:29**

**Greek:** ὃς δ’ ἂν βλασφημήσῃ εἰς τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον, οὐκ ἔχει ἄφεσιν εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, ἀλλὰ ἔνοχός ἐστιν αἰωνίου ἁμαρτήματος.

**Romanization:** *Hos d’ an blasphēmēsē eis to pneuma to hagion, ouk echei aphesin eis ton aiōna, alla enochos estin aiōniou hamartēmatos.*

**Literal English:** “But whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit does not have forgiveness [ἄφεσιν, *aphesin*: remission or release] into the age [εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, *eis ton aiōna*], but is answerable for an eternal sin [αἰωνίου ἁμαρτήματος, *aiōniou hamartēmatos*].”

In typical English: “But whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit never has forgiveness, but is guilty of an eternal sin.” “Has no forgiveness forever” is another close rendering. The idiom containing “age” gives an emphatic denial; it does not specify a period after which forgiveness resumes. “Answerable” distinguishes being held accountable for wrongdoing from the inward feeling of guilt. It does not reduce the passage to a financial debt or remove its seriousness. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02), [L-01](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-01), [L-04](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-04)

**Mark 3:30**

**Greek:** ὅτι ἔλεγον· Πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον ἔχει.

**Romanization:** *Hoti elegon: pneuma akatharton echei.*

**Literal English:** “Because they were saying, ‘An unclean spirit he has.’”

In ordinary English: “Because they were saying, ‘He has an unclean spirit.’” The NIV uses “impure” for *akatharton*. Mark’s explanation joins the warning to the accusation already being made. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

The language is forceful. The breadth of forgiveness heightens the exception without becoming empty rhetoric, and the Greek does not remove the severity. Yet even a faithful translation leaves questions about the relation between the statement, the person making it, and the forgiveness being denied.

The Syriac Peshitta, a later Gospel version in a literary form of Aramaic, preserves that force. It contrasts *rūḥā d-qudšā*, “Spirit of holiness,” with *rūḥā ṭanpṯā*, “an impure spirit,” and retains an emphatic denial of forgiveness. Its ending speaks of enduring judgment rather than the critical Greek’s eternal sin. This is another witness to the teaching’s reception, not a recovered transcript of Jesus. The repeated word for spirit makes the reversal audible: what is named holy is being called impure. Holiness, however, is not simply another word for purity. [AS-03–AS-04](../sources/semitic-gospel-witnesses.md#as-04--peshitta-mark-32830-checked-wording)

The word translated “age,” *aiōn*, also stands behind English *eon*, through Latin. That history invites attention to the horizon of a saying, but it does not turn “forever” into a fixed number of years. An age can name a world-order as well as an extent of time; Matthew’s contrast between this age and the coming one makes that horizon explicit. What the idiom asserts and how its hearers experience time are related questions, not identical ones. [TA-01](../sources/time-age-and-eternity.md#ta-01--aiōn-and-english-eon), [B-03](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-03)

### Forgiveness, turning, and responsibility

Our English words bring other associations into those questions. In the Lord’s Prayer, Matthew has “forgive us our debts” (6:12, NIV), while Luke begins “Forgive us our sins” and then uses language of indebtedness, as the NIV’s Greek-text footnote explains (11:4). The noun for forgiveness in Mark 3:29, *aphesis*, likewise belongs to the language of release and remission. Pardon, freedom from a burden, and the restoration of a relationship can illuminate one another without being the same thing. None can be reduced simply to feeling better about the past. [L-03](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-03), [L-08](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-08)

Jewish reflection already joins seeking mercy to how we treat another person. Ben Sira says, “Forgive your neighbor the wrong he has done” (Sirach 28:2, NRSVUE), then connects that act with asking forgiveness in prayer. Later rabbinic teaching distinguishes wrongs against God from wrongs against another person: the latter are not atoned for by the Day of Atonement alone, “until one has appeased one’s fellow” (Mishnah Yoma 8:9; study rendering from Hebrew). A religious act does not simply discharge the responsibility to the person harmed. [JC-04](../sources/jewish-context-and-cross-tradition-inquiry.md#jc-04), [JC-05](../sources/jewish-context-and-cross-tradition-inquiry.md#jc-05)

This continuing tradition also contains difficult accounts of hardening. The medieval Jewish philosopher and legal scholar Moses Maimonides allows that grave, deliberate wrongdoing can result in a judgment by which “repentance is withheld from him” (*Laws of Repentance* 6:3; study rendering). These later teachings are not a record of what Jesus’s contemporaries all believed. They show a living inquiry into mercy, responsibility, and the possibility of turning—not a uniformly reassuring alternative to Christian judgment. [JC-06](../sources/jewish-context-and-cross-tradition-inquiry.md#jc-06)

The final phrase in Mark requires the same care. The critical text reads “eternal sin”; another manuscript tradition reads “eternal judgment.” The former does not itself specify a punishment, but it does speak of being answerable for wrongdoing. Neither a complete account of criminal punishment nor a purely inward obstacle to healing is contained in those words alone. Their meaning is sought within the story, the wider scriptural witness, and the continuing experience of repentance, mercy, and consequence. [L-01–L-04](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-01)

### Interpretations over time

The need for interpretation is not simply a modern discomfort with severe words. John Chrysostom asks whether even repentance would be ineffective, then writes, “For even this was forgiven upon repentance” (*Homily 41*, §5). He says some who made the accusation subsequently believed. Yet he also describes grave punishment, and his homily contains anti-Jewish polemic that interprets collective suffering punitively. His hopeful sentence belongs to that troubling larger account. He is a human interpreter whose insights and harmful judgments both require attention. [R-02](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-02)

Augustine of Hippo identifies the decisive condition with final impenitence: refusal of repentance maintained until death. For the living, however, he insists, “For we are not to despair of any” (*Sermon 71*, §21). Thomas Aquinas takes up this reading as a warning against reaching that condition. He also distinguishes rejection of the means of pardon from a limit on an “all-powerful and merciful God” (*Summa theologiae*, II–II, q.14, aa.1, 3). Neither removes final judgment, but both leave reason to hesitate before declaring a living person unreachable. [R-03](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-03), [R-04](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-04)

The Reformation commentator John Calvin draws the boundary around knowing rejection of recognized truth. Those who enter this condition, he says, “never have any desire towards repentance.” His reading is not that God refuses a repentant person; it is that repentance will not arise. The Catholic Catechism also takes refusal seriously, beginning with “There are no limits to the mercy of God” (§1864) before describing rejection of repentance and possible final loss. The writer and preacher George MacDonald presses further, refusing to regard the condition as “a fixed, a final condition” (*It Shall Not Be Forgiven*). Whether resistance can itself ultimately change is where these readings diverge. [R-05](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-05), [R-07](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-07), [R-06](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-06)

Each account brings something consequential to the saying: a distinction between ignorance and knowing rejection, a boundary at death, or trust in a further possibility of transformation. These are efforts to understand the warning within a larger vision of God and human life. Their differences cannot be settled by a dictionary alone, but neither are they detached from the words. Interpretation involves both attention to the particular saying and judgment about the life and teaching to which it belongs.

### The severity remains

Matthew’s parallel states that forgiveness will not come “either in this age or in the age to come” (12:32, NIV). This is a substantial reason for taking the warning’s finality seriously. A compassionate purpose can coexist with a conviction that the danger is irreversible. [B-03](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-03)

Mark 4 raises a related but different question. Drawing on Isaiah’s account of seeing without understanding, it connects parables with failure to turn: “otherwise they might turn and be forgiven!” (4:12, NIV). Yet the chapter also presents those around Jesus asking questions and urges, “Consider carefully what you hear” (4:24, NIV). Hearing can fail; understanding calls for attention. The passage can be heard as a warning about not seeing, but its association of parables with concealment and judgment remains difficult. It does not independently say that someone’s exclusion lasts eternally, nor does it make every parable an uncomplicated promise of awakening. [JC-10](../sources/jewish-context-and-cross-tradition-inquiry.md#jc-10)

What changes when we hear the saying as an address is not necessarily its truth, but our position in relation to it. Before it becomes an answer about someone else’s destiny, it can become a question about our own way of seeing. The language remains severe enough to disturb us. We need not settle everything about eternity before allowing that disturbance to reveal something in our lives.

## 4. When certainty closes the heart

### Clinging to a view

The teachers’ description of Jesus’s work gives clinging a concrete form: liberation is named evil and becomes something to resist rather than receive. Jesus’s warning confronts that description. Across generations of interpretation, the danger can recur whenever keeping a judgment becomes more important than discovering whether it is true. We stop listening when listening might require us to change. Dogmatism gives this attachment a doctrinal form, making a formulation the measure of every encounter while exempting the formulation itself from examination.

The Order of Interbeing, founded by Thích Nhất Hạnh, places this danger within the First Mindfulness Training. Practitioners commit to “transform dogmatism and violence in ourselves and the world.” Buddhist doctrines are included within its warning against attachment. The practice concerns how we hold our own tradition, not only the errors we identify outside it. The Second Training develops the commitment through listening and continued learning. Nonattachment is not an absence of moral direction; it is part of how understanding and compassion are cultivated. [ND-01](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-01--openness-and-nonattachment-to-views)

Mark directs this concern about failed recognition toward the disciples too. Jesus asks them, “Are your hearts hardened?” and “Don’t you remember?” (8:17–18, NIV), then continues to teach them. Later they try to stop someone driving out demons in Jesus’s name because “he was not one of us”; Jesus answers, “Do not stop him” (9:38–39, NIV). Attachment to belonging threatens to obstruct the work they claim to serve. Difficulty in recognizing what is good belongs within the circle of disciples as well as beyond it. [RD-02](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-02--the-disciples-are-not-exempt), [ND-05](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-05--a-healing-outside-the-disciples-group)

These episodes keep the people in the earlier encounter human. The scribes, the Pharisees, Jesus’s family, and his disciples are not interchangeable, but neither need any become the embodiment of a permanent defect. Each meets what they do not understand through commitments and histories of their own. The seriousness of a harmful accusation does not give a complete account of the person who makes it.

### Paul and changed seeing

Paul’s life gives the possibility of change particular weight. He recalls, “I persecuted the church of God and tried to destroy it,” before describing God’s revelation of the Son (Galatians 1:13–16, NIV). Acts tells the change through blindness, restored sight, and Ananias’s words: “you may see again and be filled with the Holy Spirit” (9:17, NIV). The person who had worked to destroy the community comes to recognize what he had opposed. [B-05](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-05), [B-07](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-07)

First Timothy qualifies its account of mercy: “I acted in ignorance and unbelief” (1:13, NIV). This canonical portrayal has disputed authorship and should not simply be merged with Paul’s own account in Galatians or with Acts’ narrative. The distinction between ignorance and knowing rejection matters, and none of these passages establishes that Paul made the particular accusation in Mark. His life does not cancel the warning. It does prevent destructive religious opposition from becoming, by itself, proof that a person cannot change. [B-08](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-08)

Nor does receptivity have to begin as an achievement a person produces alone. An encounter can interrupt the understanding through which everything else has been interpreted. In the Christian account, revelation and the Spirit participate in that change. “If we refuse to see, we do not see” remains a searching formulation, but refusal need not be the last thing that happens to us. What becomes possible when we encounter what our settled understanding could not receive?

That question concerns transformation, not automatic absolution. Changed perception does not erase harm, establish divine forgiveness, or oblige another person to restore trust. A changing person can bear a lasting consequence. Responsibility and the possibility of becoming different remain together.

### The warning turns toward its interpreters

Clinging to a view can also shape Christian interpretation. We can defend an account of goodness while refusing to hear those harmed by its application. Even forgiveness can be used this way when it requires an injured person to stop speaking before responsibility has been acknowledged. A boundary against further harm is not closed-heartedness, and reluctance to trust a teacher’s claimed healing is not blasphemy.

The Gospel’s own test of profession is demanding. Matthew warns about those who say “Lord, Lord” (7:21, NIV), including people who claim powerful deeds in Jesus’s name. The judgment scene in chapter 25 brings recognition into acts of care for those who are hungry, sick, or imprisoned: “whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers and sisters of mine, you did for me” (25:40, NIV). The identity of those described as the least remains debated, and the ending is severe. Yet religious profession is plainly not a substitute for responding to a life before us. [RD-04](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-04--profession-claimed-power-and-conduct), [RD-05](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-05--hunger-imprisonment-recognition-and-judgment)

Christian anti-Judaism makes the stakes historical as well as personal. In its account of the German churches under Nazism, the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum records that “traditional interpretations of religious scriptures seemed to support these prejudices.” Inherited Christian teaching, Nazi racial ideology, state power, and different forms of complicity and resistance belong to this history. Christian teaching was not its single cause, but its part cannot be omitted. Nor can the suffering of Jewish people be reduced to a lesson about someone else’s spiritual growth. [RD-09](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-09--christian-interpretation-and-the-holocaust)

The responsibility includes questioning interpretations that enabled people and institutions to disregard Jewish lives. It also includes correction: *Nostra aetate* §4 states that Jews “should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God.” The declaration does not undo earlier injury, but shows that authoritative teaching can restrain harm as well as be used to sustain it. The problem is not simply that there is a doctrine. It is whether that doctrine and its application remain answerable to the truth and human dignity they claim to serve. [RD-10](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-10--authoritative-teaching-can-also-correct-harmful-interpretation)

The antidote to dogmatism is therefore not indifference. It includes study, prayer, listening, concrete care, and willingness to change what we do when harm becomes visible. It also leaves room for honest disagreement. Another person’s refusal to accept our interpretation does not establish that their heart is closed.

## 5. Humanity without distance from the divine

### A human teacher, a human transmission

The teaching reaches us through lives as well as words. Matthew retains the exchange about Jesus’s power; Luke places the warning beside the promise that “the Holy Spirit will teach you at that time what you should say” (12:12, NIV). The different settings guide different acts of understanding. *Dei verbum* §19 recognizes the evangelists as “selecting some things from the many which had been handed on,” while affirming the truth of the Gospels. Interpretation already participates in how the tradition is given, not simply in what later readers add. [B-03–B-04](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-03), [R-09](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-09)

Jesus’s humanity belongs within this stream. Luke says that he “grew in wisdom” (2:52, NIV); John shows him “tired as he was from the journey” (4:6, NIV). Mark gives us prayer, anger, grief, questions, and encounters under pressure. These are not incidental concessions to a human appearance. They are part of the life through which his teaching is encountered. A human teacher speaks to particular hearers, with words that may console in one moment and confront in another. [HT-02](../sources/humanity-and-provocative-teaching.md#ht-02)

To attend to this humanity is not to take something from his divinity. The Catholic Catechism itself holds together “true God and true man” (§464), and, quoting the Second Vatican Council, says that he “thought with a human mind” and “with a human heart he loved” (§470). That tradition makes claims beyond describing a divinely inspired teacher, but it also gives genuine humanity a central place. Human thought, love, and relationship are not what must be removed before the divine can be encountered. [HT-02](../sources/humanity-and-provocative-teaching.md#ht-02)

### The only child and the whole world

The language of divine sonship can seem to place Jesus at an unreachable distance. John calls him God’s “one and only Son” (3:16, NIV), yet also speaks of those who receive him becoming “children of God” (1:12, NIV). These statements are not identical, but neither is the second insignificant. The Gospel’s particular claim about Jesus accompanies an invitation into relationship with God. [HT-03](../sources/humanity-and-provocative-teaching.md#ht-03)

The word *monogenēs*, traditionally translated “only begotten,” identifies the only or unique one in a relationship; in these passages it refers to Jesus. Alongside that particular claim, the image of an only child can open another question about love. Could each person be loved *as if an only child*, with a care that does not treat anyone as expendable? This is a contemplative extension of the image, not an account of where John’s expression historically came from. It asks about the quality of a love that the same verse extends toward the world.

The Buddhist *Discourse on Love* makes a related movement. In Thích Nhất Hạnh’s translation, the example is “just as a mother loves and protects her only child at the risk of her own life.” Such wholehearted loving-kindness is to be extended to all beings. Matthew’s call to “love your enemies” (5:44, NIV), grounded in the Father’s care for the evil and the good, offers a Christian point of encounter. Neither passage makes harmful conduct harmless. Both unsettle the division of care according to who belongs, who agrees, or who has become difficult to love. [HT-04](../sources/humanity-and-provocative-teaching.md#ht-04)

The people addressed in Mark remain within that question. Their accusation is serious, but its seriousness need not first remove them from the field of care. Jesus can confront them without their humanity disappearing. Nor must concern be attributed only to those who already understand him.

### A person is more than a description

A statement about what someone has done can become the final account of who they are. This is not a choice between logic and insight. Reasoning can help us notice what a conclusion requires; it cannot decide in advance that our description has captured the whole person. A truthful account of harmful actions remains necessary without becoming an exhaustive account of a life. [ND-08](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-08--logic-and-the-choice-of-a-description)

Thích Nhất Hạnh approaches historical and ultimate dimensions through the image of wave and water. The wave’s arising and passing are not denied, but neither do they exhaust what the wave is. He cautions that “interbeing does not mean that everything is one or that everything is different.” This invites attention to two forms of closure: identifying someone completely with a past act, or imagining that transformation produces an unrelated person with no responsibility for it. [ND-02](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-02--historical-and-ultimate-neither-monism-nor-separation)

### Time as lived, remembered, and anticipated

How a person carries the past is not only a question of elapsed time. Jewish Passover practice makes a past deliverance part of present identity and obligation. Mishnah Pesachim asks each generation to see itself “as though it had come out of Egypt” (10:5; study rendering from Hebrew). This later rabbinic formulation develops the first-person language of Exodus 13:8. The event is not simply over and absent; remembrance enters how life is lived now. [TA-03](../sources/time-age-and-eternity.md#ta-03--passover-the-past-enters-present-practice)

Augustine offers a different but related attention to time in his *Confessions*: “a present of things past, a present of things present, and a present of things future” (XI.20.26). He locates these in memory, present awareness, and expectation. John’s Gospel also speaks of someone who already “has eternal life,” while retaining a future resurrection and judgment (5:24–29, NIV). These are not one philosophy, but they show that ancient religious reflection cannot be reduced to an endless succession of identical moments. Nor does attending to lived time require abandoning chronology. [TA-04](../sources/time-age-and-eternity.md#ta-04--augustines-three-presents), [TA-05](../sources/time-age-and-eternity.md#ta-05--eternal-life-as-present-and-future)

Sister Annabel Laity (True Virtue) gives this a temporal expression: “The three times inter-are.” Past action continues in its consequences; how we respond now helps form what comes next. Her discussion of regret turns toward acting differently in the present, not making the past unhappen. Transformation can mean entering responsibility more fully. It does not require another person to forgive, reconcile, or restore access, and it does not decide how long a consequence may endure. [PV-04](../sources/plum-village/time-and-transformation.md#pv-04)

The relation among the times therefore matters alongside their duration. A past act is not changed into something that never happened, yet its continuation can involve remorse, repair, renewed harm, or a different way of living. This does not make “never” mean “for a while.” It asks how the life named by a judgment continues, and whether an interpretation of eternity has made that life seem incapable of further encounter. [TA-06–TA-07](../sources/time-age-and-eternity.md#ta-06--method-chronology-and-lived-time-are-not-mutually-exclusive)

### Compassion that is not merely reassurance

Freedom of thought need not leave us silent before a harmful view. The Third Mindfulness Training joins respect for another person’s freedom with helping to transform fanaticism and narrowness through “loving speech and compassionate dialogue.” Its commitment “not to force others” is not a commitment never to challenge them. The distinction concerns whether speech helps understanding become possible or demands submission to the speaker. [ND-01](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-01--openness-and-nonattachment-to-views)

An authentic warning draws attention to a real danger. It may need to be urgent, and its discomfort does not make it coercive. The Buddhist discourse to Prince Abhaya similarly describes speech that is “factual, true, beneficial, but unendearing & disagreeable to others,” with attention to the proper time for speaking it (*Majjhima Nikāya* 58, translated by Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu). That discourse, too, arises around a severe judgment. Compassion is not identified simply with what sounds reassuring. [ND-09](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-09--truthful-beneficial-and-timely-speech)

Heard in this light, Jesus’s summons, questions, parables, and warning can belong to compassionate dialogue. He is addressing people caught in a judgment about his work, using language forceful enough to bring that judgment before them. The possibility rests on the danger being real and the speech serving understanding, not on severity being valuable in itself. For human teachers, clear seeing, the needs of those addressed, and the effects of speech all matter; certainty about one’s loving intention cannot replace attention to them.

### Remaining in the teaching

A koan-like engagement carries that attention into the act of interpretation. The question includes the one asking it. What needs to change may be not only a proposed answer, but how we listen, speak, and respond to someone we had already decided we understood. The warning can touch our own attachment rather than remain a means of classifying other people. [ND-04](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-04--a-koan-involving-the-reader)

John gives this relation between teaching and lived understanding a direct expression:

> “If you hold to my teaching, you are really my disciples. Then you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free.”
>
> — John 8:31–32, NIV. [B-09](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-09)

The verb *menō* means to remain or abide. Knowing is joined to remaining in the word, not simply to possessing a settled explanation of it. In John’s setting, the freedom concerns sin and the liberating work of the Son. The invitation has a practical depth: forgiveness, love, and repentance become known through the lives in which they are practiced. Study and inherited teaching belong to that process; so do the encounters that reveal where our understanding has not yet become action.

John also speaks of “whoever lives by the truth” coming into the light (3:21, NIV). The Greek phrase is ποιῶν τὴν ἀλήθειαν, *poiōn tēn alētheian*: literally, “doing the truth.” Truth is not only something affirmed; it is enacted. An interpretation of mercy must become more than a persuasive account of mercy. Its meaning appears in whether we can tell the truth about harm, care for those affected, and remain willing to learn. [B-11](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-11)

## 6. The invitation before us

Returning to Mark 3, the saying can be heard as a sharp, compassionate wake-up call. The promise of forgiveness and its breadth give the warning its startling force. Jesus speaks to people who have called the work of liberation evil, and the language of judgment confronts their own judgment. The warning brings the danger into the present encounter: while healing is treated as an enemy, it cannot be welcomed as healing. Its force lies in calling people to recognize what they are turning away from.

The meaning of “eternal sin” remains challenging. The Greek is emphatic, other Gospel passages sustain its severity, and interpreters have not reached one answer. A compassionate purpose does not settle those differences. Yet uncertainty about a final outcome need not suspend the work of seeing, repentance, and responsibility. When understanding changes, the suffering already caused still calls for acknowledgment and whatever repair is possible; a new beginning is not an erased past.

Humanizing Jesus does not require making him ordinary in the sense of insignificant. It means allowing his teaching to arise within a human life, grounded in time, place, culture, relationships, conflicts, grief, courage, and love. His Jewish inheritance belongs to that humanity, as do the people who shared it and understood it differently. The disciples, religious teachers, evangelists, and later interpreters belong to that arc of human experience. Reverence can bring us closer to these lives rather than place them beyond understanding.

An understanding of forgiveness can remain generous in its claims while becoming anemic in practice. We can defend mercy and fail to hear someone asking for accountability; we can defend truth while refusing an encounter that might deepen it. The question is not whether we have found a formulation that no longer troubles us. It is whether we can remain with the teaching in a way that changes how we live. Whom have we already decided we understand, and what might become possible if we listened again?
