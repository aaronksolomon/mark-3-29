# All Will Be Forgiven?
## Compassion, Warning, and the Humanity of Interpretation

> *“Truly I tell you, people can be forgiven all their sins and every slander they utter, but whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit will never be forgiven; they are guilty of an eternal sin.”*
>
> — Mark 3:28–29, New International Version (2011). [RD-00](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-00--niv-opening)

## 1. The tension

In one movement, this saying opens forgiveness across the range of human sin and slander, then names a stark and apparently permanent exception. What distinguishes the second saying from the first? The words may identify a culpable act, expose the condition expressed through it, and announce a consequence. They may also confront the hearers with a danger they have not understood. These possibilities need not exclude one another. There is a need for us to look deeply and consider what kind of teaching this is. The severity of the words does not relieve us of the need to interpret them. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

One reading draws our attention especially: could the severity belong to a warning offered out of compassion? An effort to reach someone need not always take the form of reassurance. It may challenge a judgment that has become protected from correction. This is the leading possibility explored here, not a conclusion about Jesus’s unrecorded intentions. It also leaves a difficult question open. A compassionate warning might seek to avert a real and irreversible loss; its purpose would not, by itself, make the threatened consequence temporary.

We bring our own histories to these words. Forgiveness may suggest pardon, release from debt, relinquished resentment, or reconciliation. Guilt may mean being answerable for an act or feeling guilty about it. These associations are not interchangeable. The inquiry concerns both the saying and the human work of receiving it: how words spoken within a conflict are remembered, arranged within a Gospel, translated, and incorporated into teaching. What happens when an address to someone becomes a judgment we pronounce about someone?

## 2. A warning within an encounter

Mark gives the warning a specific setting. Teachers of the law from Jerusalem attribute Jesus’s work against destructive powers to the ruler of demons. Jesus does not simply turn away from them. He “called them over to him” and asks, “How can Satan drive out Satan?” (Mark 3:23, NIV). The divided kingdom, divided house, and bound strong man ask the hearers to reconsider their explanation. Within the story, the power they call demonic is overcoming demonic domination. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

Mark then connects the warning to what they are already saying: “He has an impure spirit” (3:30, NIV). The accusation is not merely a possible future mistake. It is present speech through which the liberating work is being repudiated. A preventive reading must account for that present conduct, not move the danger into an entirely different situation.

A saying does something within a relationship. It can warn, rebuke, invite, unsettle, or console. Its meaning is not exhausted by deciding which proposition it asserts. The summons and the argument give us a reason to examine continued engagement: Jesus is still addressing the people whose account he rejects. Earlier in the same chapter, in a separate healing encounter, anger accompanies grief at their response: he is “deeply distressed at their stubborn hearts” (3:5, NIV). These details do not settle the meaning of 3:29, but they make room for concern and confrontation together. [RD-01](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-01--hardness-before-the-blasphemy-controversy)

This way of hearing severe speech has a history within Christianity. Commenting on Matthew’s parallel, John Chrysostom describes Jesus’s purpose as “to do good to them that were sinning” (*Homily 41 on Matthew*, §1). In §5 he treats the alarming warning as part of correction. His reading does not eliminate punishment, and the homily’s anti-Jewish polemic must also be faced. It nevertheless offers an early precedent for asking what the warning seeks to do for those addressed, rather than only which category it assigns them. [R-02](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-02)

We can attend to this possibility without imagining that we possess the historical opponents’ thoughts. Mark does not explicitly say that they inwardly recognized divine action and knowingly lied about it. Nor does their possible sincerity make the accusation harmless. The question is how an act of judgment can become so defended that what might correct it is taken as further evidence against the one offering correction.

That is the proposed connection with rigidity. It is not a replacement for the meaning of blasphemy, and it cannot yet tell us what the warning’s finality means. For that we need to return to the words themselves.

## 3. What the words require and interpreters add

### The breadth and the exception

The first clause is compact:

**Greek:** πάντα ἀφεθήσεται.

**Romanization:** *panta aphethēsetai*.

**Literal gloss:** πάντα, all things; ἀφεθήσεται, will be forgiven.

**Natural English:** “All will be forgiven,” with the following words specifying human sins and blasphemies. The NIV’s “can be forgiven” is retained in the opening quotation; the study gloss makes the Greek future passive visible. The clause is not, by itself, a promise that every individual will ultimately be reconciled. [B-01–B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-01)

The exception is no less direct:

**Greek:** οὐκ ἔχει ἄφεσιν εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, ἀλλὰ ἔνοχός ἐστιν αἰωνίου ἁμαρτήματος.

**Romanization:** *ouk echei aphesin eis ton aiōna, alla enochos estin aiōniou hamartēmatos*.

**Literal gloss:** οὐκ ἔχει, does not have; ἄφεσιν, forgiveness/remission; εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, into/unto the age; ἀλλὰ, but; ἔνοχός ἐστιν, is guilty/liable; αἰωνίου ἁμαρτήματος, of/for an eternal sin.

**Natural English:** “Never has forgiveness, but is guilty of an eternal sin.” An alternative is “has no forgiveness forever, but is answerable for an eternal sin.” The negative idiom has the force of “never”; the mechanical gloss “into the age” does not supply an endpoint. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02), [L-01](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-01)

Mark’s explanation also deserves close attention:

**Greek:** ὅτι ἔλεγον· Πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον ἔχει.

**Romanization:** *hoti elegon: pneuma akatharton echei*.

**Literal gloss:** ὅτι, because; ἔλεγον, they were saying; πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον, an unclean/impure spirit; ἔχει, he has.

**Natural English:** “Because they were saying, ‘He has an unclean spirit.’” This is a study rendering; the NIV uses “impure.” The imperfect presents the accusation as background speech in the scene. It does not add a promise of forgiveness when that speech stops. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

### Release, liability, and the scope of a translation

The word translated “forgiveness,” *aphesis*, also belongs to the language of release and remission. That wider range invites us to ask what it means to receive release, but the context of sin makes forgiveness an appropriate English word. Recognition, healing, forgiveness, reconciliation, and restored trust may be related; they are not interchangeable. In the reading proposed here, receptivity to correction is relevant to receiving mercy, not a complete definition of divine forgiveness. The Spirit remains an agent in the Christian account, not simply another name for human insight. [L-03](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-03)

The closing words also matter. The SBL Greek New Testament prints “eternal sin,” rather than the “eternal judgment” found in another textual tradition. The sentence does not explicitly name a punishment, but *enochos* already carries the possibility of liability in ancient Greek. We should neither import an entire modern criminal code nor remove the judicial associations that are genuinely available. A translation constrains the inquiry without supplying a finished account of divine judgment. [L-02](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-02), [L-04](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-04)

### What the tradition has made of the difficulty

The interpreters differ partly because they locate the decisive condition differently. Chrysostom acknowledges, “For even this was forgiven upon repentance” (*Homily 41*, §5), while continuing to describe grave culpability and punishment. The tension remains within his interpretation; the acknowledgment should not be isolated as though the homily simply taught universal restoration. [R-02](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-02)

Augustine identifies the unforgiven condition with final impenitence: resistance maintained to the end of life. For someone still living, however, he insists, “For we are not to despair of any” (*Sermon 71*, §21; Sermon 21 in the cited English collection). The condition cannot be pronounced final in advance. This interpretation draws on a larger account of death and repentance; the boundary at death is not stated in Mark’s sentence. [R-03](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-03)

Aquinas preserves several readings. In presenting Augustine’s account, he understands Jesus’s words as a warning against reaching final impenitence, rather than a declaration that the hearers have already reached it (*Summa theologiae*, II–II, q.14, a.1). Elsewhere in the same question, he distinguishes a condition that rejects the means of pardon from a limit on an “all-powerful and merciful God” (a.3). Prevention and genuine finality can therefore coexist in his theology. His allowance for extraordinary healing is not a promise of universal restoration. [R-04](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-04)

Calvin gives the completed condition a more explicitly irreversible form: those who fall into it “never have any desire towards repentance.” He distinguishes this rejection from wrongdoing committed in ignorance, including Paul’s persecution. His interpretation does not require a repentant person to seek mercy and be refused; it holds that such repentance will not arise. [R-05](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-05)

The Catholic Catechism begins its explanation, “There are no limits to the mercy of God” (§1864), but immediately discusses deliberate refusal of repentance and the possibility of final loss. George MacDonald goes further toward restoration, rejecting the idea that the condition must be “a fixed, a final condition” (*It Shall Not Be Forgiven*). These positions share concern with refusal, but do not share an answer about its possible end. [R-07](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-07), [R-06](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-06)

The differences are substantive. An appeal to ignorance, a boundary at death, irreversible hardening, and a restorative divine purpose are not alternative dictionary definitions. They are judgments about how the saying belongs within a larger understanding of God and human life. Their presence is not automatically evidence of distortion: interpretation has to make connections. We should be able to see which connections each account makes.

### The objection that remains

A contrary reading can accept much of the compassionate proposal and still insist that Jesus warns because the loss is real and final. Matthew’s parallel says the blasphemy “will not be forgiven, either in this age or in the age to come” (12:32, NIV). Mark 4 presents a further difficulty: parables are joined to outsiders’ failure to understand, “otherwise they might turn and be forgiven!” (4:12, NIV). The latter resists a simple account in which every difficult saying transparently invites awakening. [B-03](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-03), [RD-11](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-11--hard-evidence-the-proposal-must-retain)

These are not marginal qualifications. A warning’s compassionate purpose does not settle its truth conditions, its effect on the hearer, or the duration of its consequence. We can give that purpose serious attention while leaving the stronger question of finality unresolved. A reader who concludes differently need not be less compassionate or less willing to examine the evidence.

## 4. Rigidity, changed seeing, and responsibility

### Conviction that remains answerable

Rigidity here does not mean having convictions, belonging to a tradition, or holding firmly to a moral obligation. It means protecting a judgment from correction: refusing to let what happens, what another person says, or what our actions produce call that judgment into question. The problem is not simply that we have an interpretation. It is that our interpretation has become exempt from examination.

Dogmatism gives this rigidity a doctrinal form. A formulation becomes the final measure by which every encounter is judged; what fails to fit may be dismissed before it is heard. The First Mindfulness Training of the Order of Interbeing commits practitioners to “transform dogmatism and violence in ourselves and the world.” It includes Buddhist doctrines within its warning. The Second develops this commitment through listening and continuing inquiry. These are practices for keeping a teaching answerable to the understanding and compassion it is meant to cultivate. [ND-01](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-01--openness-and-nonattachment-to-views)

Mark directs a related question toward the disciples. Jesus asks them, “Are your hearts hardened?” and “Don’t you remember?” (8:17–18, NIV). He continues to question and teach them. Later they try to stop an exorcist because “he was not one of us”; Jesus answers, “Do not stop him” (9:38–39, NIV). Their own sense of belonging threatens to obstruct the work they encounter. Neither passage equates every misunderstanding with the sin of 3:29, but both prevent us from locating the problem only in Jesus’s opponents. [RD-02](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-02--the-disciples-are-not-exempt), [ND-05](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-05--a-healing-outside-the-disciples-group)

### Paul as a test, not a solution

Paul recalls, “I persecuted the church of God and tried to destroy it,” before describing God’s revelation of the Son (Galatians 1:13–16, NIV). The decisive fact is the persecution: active opposition with consequences for others, followed by a profound change in judgment. Acts gives that change a narrative involving sight and the Spirit: “you may see again and be filled with the Holy Spirit” (9:17, NIV). [B-05](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-05), [B-07](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-07)

First Timothy makes a qualification that matters: “I was shown mercy because I acted in ignorance and unbelief” (1:13, NIV). Acts and 1 Timothy are not interchangeable with Paul’s own testimony; the authorship of 1 Timothy is disputed. Nor do the sources examined here place Paul in Mark’s scene or attribute its particular accusation to him. An interpretation distinguishing ignorant opposition from knowing rejection therefore deserves to be heard. [B-08](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-08)

Paul does not cancel Mark. His life places a question beside it: what can the Spirit do in someone who has been acting against the community that claims to live by that Spirit? Receptivity need not begin as a successful act of individual will. An encounter can interrupt the understanding through which a person has been interpreting everything else.

The proposal remains: if we refuse to see, we do not see. While healing is named evil, it cannot be welcomed as healing. But moving from present refusal to permanent refusal requires a further claim. So does moving from changed perception to forgiveness. **A lasting consequence can be attributed to a changing person.** Recognizing that someone is not fixed does not, by itself, settle what Mark says about liability. The question concerns both the condition and what may follow from it.

### When the interpreter is implicated

The most difficult application is inward. A Christian interpreter can defend an account of goodness while refusing to hear the people harmed by its application. Even forgiveness can be used this way when it requires an injured person to cease speaking before responsibility has been acknowledged. A boundary against further harm is not rigidity, and reluctance to trust a teacher’s claim of healing is not blasphemy.

The Gospels themselves distinguish profession from conduct. Matthew warns about those who say “Lord, Lord” (7:21, NIV), including people who claim exorcisms and powerful deeds in Jesus’s name. A claim of healing is not its own verification. The judgment scene in Matthew 25 names care for people who are hungry, sick, or imprisoned; “whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers and sisters of mine, you did for me” (25:40, NIV). The identity of those described as the least is disputed, and the passage’s conclusion remains severe. It still asks interpretation to attend to a life, not merely an assertion of allegiance. [RD-04](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-04--profession-claimed-power-and-conduct), [RD-05](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-05--hunger-imprisonment-recognition-and-judgment)

Christian responsibility during the Holocaust makes the stakes of interpretation concrete. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum observes that “traditional interpretations of religious scriptures seemed to support these prejudices.” Its account distinguishes inherited Christian anti-Judaism, Nazi racial ideology, political conditions, and different forms of complicity and resistance. This history does not establish our proposed reading of Mark. It requires Christian interpreters to examine what their teaching can permit people and institutions to disregard. Jewish suffering cannot be reduced to material for someone else’s spiritual development. [RD-09](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-09--christian-interpretation-and-the-holocaust)

Doctrine can also constrain harmful interpretation. *Nostra aetate* §4 states that Jews “should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God.” This does not erase earlier injury; it makes correction part of authoritative teaching. The question is not whether we can live without any formulation, but whether our formulations remain open to the truth and responsibility they claim to serve. [RD-10](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-10--authoritative-teaching-can-also-correct-harmful-interpretation)

## 5. Receiving a teaching without fixing the person

### The humanity of transmission

The saying reaches us through distinguishable acts of understanding. Matthew retains the controversy and expands its temporal expression. Luke places the warning beside instructions about testimony before authorities, where “the Holy Spirit will teach you at that time what you should say” (12:12, NIV). These differences show interpretation within the Gospel tradition. They do not establish exactly which words Jesus originally spoke. [B-03–B-04](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-03)

Human participation need not be a story of degeneration. It can preserve insight as well as change it. *Dei verbum* §19 describes the evangelists as “selecting some things from the many which had been handed on,” while also affirming Gospel truth. The document recognizes synthesis and explanation in response to the situations of their churches. Doctrine may preserve a warning’s urgency, make its implications explicit, or prevent a harmful use; it need not turn every living encounter into a dead classification. [R-09](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-09)

Jesus’s humanity belongs here too. We need not protect him from every difficult saying by assigning it to someone else. Neither can we confidently reconstruct his thoughts or mistakes across two millennia. We can attend to the ways the Gospels present him speaking and acting: within relationships, through stories and arguments, in response to particular lives. Humanity may be the medium through which revelation becomes available, rather than an obstacle it must pass through untouched. The inquiry into divinity can begin there, without first removing anger, grief, or historical circumstance from the life we are trying to understand.

### Continuity without an exhaustive description

A formulation may be accurate without exhausting the person it describes. Logic helps us distinguish a valid inference from adequate premises; it does not require that persons be immutable. The problem arises when a description is treated as complete before the inquiry begins. Our distinction between present refusal and permanent refusal helps locate an additional premise, not dispense with Mark’s temporal language.

Thích Nhất Hạnh’s account of historical and ultimate dimensions offers a further practice of attention. The wave can be described through its arising and passing while also being encountered as water. In the same interview he rejects the conclusion that “everything is one.” The teaching asks us to examine attachment to both absolute sameness and complete separation, rather than merely choose a different fixed category. [ND-02](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-02--historical-and-ultimate-neither-monism-nor-separation)

Applied here, this lets us distinguish without turning distinctions into permanent divisions. The person who caused harm remains answerable for it and is not exhausted by that act. We need neither identify someone wholly with a past action nor imagine that transformation produces an unrelated person with no responsibility for the past. A nondual approach can hold continuity and change together. It does not make harm and healing indistinguishable or give the harmed person an obligation to reconcile.

This is a contribution to how we attend to a person, not a proof that every consequence must end. The historical and ultimate are not separate compartments in which responsibility is first acknowledged and then dismissed. Nondual attention can deepen accountability by refusing both the fixed identity of the condemned person and the fiction that a changed person has no past.

### Compassionate speech must also be examined

Buddhist teaching can question the essay’s preferred reading, not merely confirm it. The Third Mindfulness Training commits practitioners “not to force others” to adopt their views, and explicitly names authority and threat among the means it rejects. That raises a difficult question for compassionate severity: when does a warning help someone recognize a danger, and when does it pressure that person to submit to the speaker? [ND-01](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-01--openness-and-nonattachment-to-views)

Calling a warning compassionate is not enough to justify its use. We still need to ask whether it truthfully names a danger, whose interests it serves, and what room it leaves for response and disagreement. A teacher’s confidence in loving intentions cannot settle those questions. Neither is every unwelcome warning coercive. Mark’s account can challenge the assumption that care must always reassure, while the training asks us not to turn spiritual authority into an exemption from scrutiny.

Thích Nhất Hạnh’s letter *Bat Nha: a Koan* gives another point of contact: a question becomes one’s “own problem,” rather than merely a puzzle concerning other people. Mark’s warning can be approached in this koan-like way without identifying it historically as a Zen koan. We study what the words mean and also allow them to question the one studying. What might we refuse to recognize in order to preserve our interpretation? [ND-04](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-04--a-koan-involving-the-reader)

John’s connection between understanding and practice belongs here too: “whoever lives by the truth comes into the light” (3:21, NIV). The inquiry is not finished when we possess a satisfactory formulation. We still have to discover what that understanding becomes in our conduct, especially toward someone whose account is difficult for us to hear. [B-11](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-11)

## 6. The warning before us

We have begun to hear a possibility within the severity: Jesus’s address may be an effort to reach people whose judgment has become resistant to correction. The scene does not present the saying as a detached classification alone. It places the warning within an encounter in which the accusers are summoned, questioned, and confronted. Later interpreters have also understood warning as an act of concern, even while disagreeing about the finality of what it announces.

That disagreement remains. The temporal language does not become mild because we find a compassionate purpose in it. Matthew’s parallel and Mark’s difficult account of parables prevent us from treating every obstacle as a promise of eventual awakening. A person can change and still bear a lasting consequence. We have not established a final account of forgiveness by showing that interpretation, identity, or perception can change.

What has become clearer is the responsibility involved in receiving the warning. Before making it a judgment about another person’s ultimate condition, we can ask how it addresses us: what our convictions permit us to notice, whose suffering we discount, and whether a claim to speak for mercy has made us less willing to listen. This does not require abandoning conviction or allowing further harm. It requires that our understanding remain answerable to the lives it affects.

The humanity of Jesus and his interpreters is not something to remove in order to reach the teaching. It is part of where the teaching is encountered. We can carry forward this way of listening without declaring that all theological outcomes are settled. Its test is not whether everyone agrees with us, but whether we remain capable of hearing what could correct us. Whom would we need to hear differently if we received this warning as an address to ourselves?
