# All Will Be Forgiven?
## Compassion, Warning, and our Shared Humanity

> *“Truly I tell you, people can be forgiven all their sins and every slander they utter, but whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit will never be forgiven; they are guilty of an eternal sin.”*
>
> — Mark 3:28–29, New International Version (2011). [RD-00](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-00--niv-opening)

## 1. Hearing the warning

In one movement, this saying opens forgiveness across the range of human sin and slander, then names a stark and permanent exception. Read as two detached statements, the words seem to pull us in opposite directions. Heard within an encounter, they may do something more: the breadth of the first makes the warning that follows all the more startling. Against the possibility of forgiveness for so much, Jesus places the gravity of what his hearers are saying. The contrast may be part of the force with which he is trying to reach them. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

This is the way of hearing we will explore: a provocative warning, possibly offered out of compassion, that confronts people with what their judgment has prevented them from seeing. We need not make the language mild to consider that possibility. Nor does a compassionate purpose settle what an “eternal sin” means. The question concerns not only what the words assert, but what speaking them may seek to bring about in those who hear them.

We bring our own histories to these words. Forgiveness may suggest pardon, release, or reconciliation; judgment may suggest moral discernment or condemnation. Jesus and those who heard him also spoke within a world of inherited meanings, loyalties, and disputes. Before deciding how this saying applies to everyone, everywhere, we can return to the people and the story in which it appears. What happens when a specific expression in a lived moment becomes a judgment we pronounce about someone, or a group?

## 2. A teacher among people

### The story before the saying

Mark’s account begins with the Spirit descending upon Jesus, followed by his proclamation that “The kingdom of God has come near” (1:15, NIV). Teaching, healing, and release from impure spirits give that proclamation a visible form. People gather around him; he also withdraws to pray. From the beginning, the Gospel presents divine activity through a life moving among other lives, not through a list of propositions delivered outside any human setting. [HT-01](../sources/humanity-and-provocative-teaching.md#ht-01)

Conflict develops around what this activity means. When Jesus forgives a paralyzed man, some teachers of the law think, “He’s blaspheming!” (2:7, NIV). His meals with people identified as sinners, and his disciples’ conduct on the Sabbath, bring further challenges. Jesus responds through questions and images, including the physician who attends the sick and new wine that cannot be contained in old wineskins (2:17–22). These are disputes within a Jewish scriptural world shared by Jesus and his interlocutors, not a contrast between a religion of compassion and a religion without it. [HT-01](../sources/humanity-and-provocative-teaching.md#ht-01)

Chapter 3 opens with a Sabbath healing. Jesus asks about doing good and saving life; the response is silence. Mark describes him as angry and “deeply distressed at their stubborn hearts” (3:5, NIV), then shows him restoring the man’s hand. Later, the crowd becomes so demanding that Jesus and those with him cannot eat, and his family comes to take charge of him (3:20–21). Misunderstanding is not confined to religious opponents. Mark places the teacher within relationships that are strained by what he is doing. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02), [RD-01](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-01--hardness-before-the-blasphemy-controversy)

### From accusation to warning

The teachers who arrive from Jerusalem give Jesus’s work a particularly damaging interpretation: “By the prince of demons he is driving out demons” (3:22, NIV). They associate his power with Beelzebul, the ruler of demons. They do not simply deny that something has happened. They name its source as evil. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

Jesus “called them over to him” and asks, “How can Satan drive out Satan?” (3:23, NIV). A divided kingdom or household cannot stand; taking a strong man’s possessions requires first overcoming him. These images challenge the accusation at its center. In Mark’s telling, Jesus is breaking the hold of destructive power, not serving it. The teachers are being asked to look again at the work they have condemned.

Then the address becomes sharper. Jesus speaks of the breadth of forgiveness and immediately follows it with the warning about blaspheming against the Holy Spirit. Mark explains the connection: they were saying, “He has an impure spirit” (3:30, NIV). The contrast between the Holy Spirit and an impure spirit is crucial. What the Gospel presents as God’s liberating activity has been named as its opposite. The warning brings the seriousness of that judgment back to the people making it.

Heard this way, the opening promise is not merely a reassuring statement that the next sentence interrupts. It also heightens the warning. The movement from familiar images to words of such severity can be heard as an urgent attempt to awaken the hearers. Mark gives us no reply between the argument and the warning, so we cannot reconstruct a failed exchange that he does not record. But the sequence itself allows us to hear the intensity of one continuing address. [HT-01](../sources/humanity-and-provocative-teaching.md#ht-01)

There is a further reversal across these chapters. Earlier, Jesus is accused of blasphemy because he forgives. Now he warns of blasphemy in an accusation directed against his work. The language of religious judgment turns back upon the act of judging. Could Jesus be using a vocabulary his hearers take very seriously to disturb their confidence in how they are applying it? This is a way of attending to Mark’s narrative, not evidence that the teachers had themselves pronounced anyone eternally unforgivable.

### A saying that unsettles its hearers

A saying does something within a relationship. It can warn, rebuke, invite, unsettle, or console; its meaning is not exhausted by identifying the proposition it asserts. A teacher may speak within familiar categories in order to expose what those categories have prevented someone from noticing. That possibility does not place Jesus outside his own culture. It asks how he works within it, addressing particular people rather than simply assigning them a place in a system.

A reader formed by Zen practice may recognize something here: a difficult saying can interrupt the understanding with which we approach it, rather than supply an answer that leaves us unchanged. Thích Nhất Hạnh describes this involvement in *Bat Nha: a Koan*, where the question becomes one’s “own problem.” We can bring that practice of attention to Mark without making his warning a Zen text or treating paradox as permission to disregard its words. The reply contains parables; the severe saying asks to be heard within that encounter. [ND-04](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-04--a-koan-involving-the-reader)

This hearing also has a Christian precedent. John Chrysostom, an early Christian preacher commenting on Matthew’s parallel, describes Jesus’s purpose as “to do good to them that were sinning” (*Homily 41 on Matthew*, §1). He hears correction within the confrontation. His interpretation retains severe judgment, as we will see, but it reminds us that compassion need not be identified only with reassuring speech. [R-02](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-02)

The human difficulty coming into view is not simply error. It is clinging to a judgment so closely that we can no longer receive what might correct it. If even liberation is evidence of evil, what could be allowed to count against the accusation? Mark does not explain the teachers’ inner motives. We can nevertheless recognize the danger of an understanding that has ceased to be open to encounter.

## 3. What the words keep open—and what they do not

Careful attention to language helps us stay with this difficulty rather than explain it away. The Greek gives real breadth to the forgiveness of sins and blasphemies, then uses an emphatic denial of forgiveness in the exception. The expression commonly translated “never” does not mean “until the end of the age.” The first clause can heighten the second rhetorically without ceasing to affirm forgiveness; neither should be detached from the movement of the whole saying. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02), [L-01](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-01)

Our English vocabulary also needs care. Matthew’s prayer speaks of debts, while Luke joins sin and indebtedness. The word for forgiveness in Mark, *aphesis*, belongs to the language of release and remission. These associations are not interchangeable, but they remind us that biblical forgiveness is not simply a modern psychological feeling or the cancellation of a criminal sentence. The phrase “eternal sin” contains liability language without itself giving a complete account of punishment. [L-03](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-03), [L-04](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-04), [L-08](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-08)

### Readers who have stayed with the difficulty

The history of interpretation shows that the problem is not simply a modern discomfort with severe words. John Chrysostom asks whether even repentance would be ineffective, then writes, “For even this was forgiven upon repentance” (*Homily 41*, §5). He says some who made the accusation subsequently believed. Yet he also describes grave punishment, and his homily contains anti-Jewish polemic that interprets collective suffering punitively. His hopeful sentence cannot be separated from that context. He is a human interpreter whose insights and harmful judgments both require attention. [R-02](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-02)

Augustine of Hippo identifies the decisive condition with final impenitence: refusal of repentance maintained until death. For the living, however, he insists, “For we are not to despair of any” (*Sermon 71*, §21). Thomas Aquinas takes up this reading as a warning against reaching that condition. He also distinguishes rejection of the means of pardon from a limit on an “all-powerful and merciful God” (*Summa theologiae*, II–II, q.14, aa.1, 3). Neither removes final judgment, but both leave us reason to hesitate before declaring a living person unreachable. [R-03](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-03), [R-04](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-04)

The Reformation commentator John Calvin draws the boundary around knowing rejection of recognized truth. Those who enter this condition, he says, “never have any desire towards repentance.” His reading is not that God refuses a repentant person; it is that repentance will not arise. The Catholic Catechism likewise takes refusal seriously, beginning with “There are no limits to the mercy of God” (§1864) before describing rejection of repentance and possible final loss. The writer and preacher George MacDonald presses further, refusing to regard the condition as “a fixed, a final condition” (*It Shall Not Be Forgiven*). Whether resistance can itself ultimately change is where these readings diverge. [R-05](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-05), [R-07](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-07), [R-06](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-06)

We do not need to choose an authority and close the inquiry. We can notice what each account brings to the saying: a distinction between ignorance and knowing rejection, a boundary at death, or trust in a further possibility of transformation. These are efforts to understand the warning within a larger vision of God and human life. They are not meanings that can be obtained from a dictionary alone.

### The severity remains

Matthew’s parallel states that forgiveness will not come “either in this age or in the age to come” (12:32, NIV). Mark’s next chapter also unsettles an easy account of parables as invitations that invariably open understanding: “otherwise they might turn and be forgiven!” (4:12, NIV). A reader can hear compassion in a warning and still understand its consequence as final. Our contemplative approach must leave that reading a real place, rather than treating disagreement as a failure of openness. [B-03](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-03), [RD-11](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-11--hard-evidence-the-proposal-must-retain)

What changes when we hear the saying as an address is not necessarily its truth, but our position in relation to it. Before it becomes an answer about someone else’s destiny, it can become a question about our own way of seeing. The language remains severe enough to disturb us. We need not settle everything about eternity before allowing that disturbance to reveal something in our lives.

## 4. When certainty closes the heart

### Clinging to a view

“Rigidity” was our earlier name for the condition under examination. Clinging to certainty may describe it more closely. The difficulty is not that we have a conviction, but that keeping it has become more important than discovering whether it is true. We stop listening when listening might require us to change. Dogmatism gives this attachment a doctrinal form: the formulation becomes the measure of every encounter, while remaining exempt from examination itself.

The First Mindfulness Training of the Order of Interbeing commits practitioners to “transform dogmatism and violence in ourselves and the world.” Its inclusion of Buddhist doctrines is important. The practice concerns our attachment to our own teaching, not only the mistakes we recognize in someone else’s. The Second Training develops that commitment through listening and continued learning. These are substantial commitments; nonattachment is not the absence of moral direction. [ND-01](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-01--openness-and-nonattachment-to-views)

Mark directs the question inward too. Jesus asks his disciples, “Are your hearts hardened?” and “Don’t you remember?” (8:17–18, NIV), then continues to teach them. Later they try to stop an exorcist because “he was not one of us”; Jesus answers, “Do not stop him” (9:38–39, NIV). The concern is not confined to those who oppose Jesus. His followers can become so attached to belonging that they obstruct the work they claim to serve. [RD-02](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-02--the-disciples-are-not-exempt), [ND-05](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-05--a-healing-outside-the-disciples-group)

These episodes also help us humanize the people in the first controversy. The scribes, the Pharisees, Jesus’s family, and his disciples are not interchangeable, but neither need any become the embodiment of a permanent defect. They encounter what they do not understand through commitments and histories of their own. We can recognize the seriousness of a harmful accusation without supposing that we know everything about the person who makes it.

### Paul and changed seeing

Paul’s life places this possibility under considerable pressure. He recalls, “I persecuted the church of God and tried to destroy it,” before describing God’s revelation of the Son (Galatians 1:13–16, NIV). Acts tells the change through blindness, restored sight, and Ananias’s words: “you may see again and be filled with the Holy Spirit” (9:17, NIV). The person who had worked to destroy the community comes to recognize what he had opposed. [B-05](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-05), [B-07](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-07)

First Timothy qualifies its account of mercy: “I acted in ignorance and unbelief” (1:13, NIV). This is a later canonical portrayal whose authorship is disputed, not simply another passage from Paul’s undisputed autobiographical testimony. The distinction between ignorance and knowing rejection matters, and none of these sources establishes that Paul made the particular accusation in Mark. His life does not cancel the warning. It does prevent destructive religious opposition from becoming, by itself, proof that a person cannot change. [B-08](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-08)

Nor does receptivity have to begin as an achievement a person produces alone. An encounter can interrupt the understanding through which everything else has been interpreted. In the Christian account, revelation and the Spirit participate in that change. “If we refuse to see, we do not see” remains a searching formulation, but refusal need not be the last thing that happens to us. What becomes possible when we encounter what our settled understanding could not receive?

That question concerns transformation, not automatic absolution. Changed perception does not erase harm, establish divine forgiveness, or oblige another person to restore trust. A changing person can bear a lasting consequence. We can hold those realities together without reducing either responsibility or hope to a slogan.

### The warning turns toward its interpreters

The same inquiry turns toward Christian interpretation. We can defend an account of goodness while refusing to hear those harmed by its application. Even forgiveness can be used this way when it requires an injured person to stop speaking before responsibility has been acknowledged. A boundary against further harm is not closed-heartedness, and reluctance to trust a teacher’s claimed healing is not blasphemy.

The Gospel’s own test of profession is demanding. Matthew warns about those who say “Lord, Lord” (7:21, NIV), including people who claim powerful deeds in Jesus’s name. The judgment scene in chapter 25 brings the question into care for those who are hungry, sick, or imprisoned: “you did for me” (25:40, NIV). The passage has its own interpretive difficulties and severe ending, but it does not allow religious language to replace attention to actual lives. [RD-04](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-04--profession-claimed-power-and-conduct), [RD-05](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-05--hunger-imprisonment-recognition-and-judgment)

Christian anti-Judaism makes the stakes historical as well as personal. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum records that “traditional interpretations of religious scriptures seemed to support these prejudices” in its account of the German churches under Nazism. Inherited Christian teaching, racial ideology, state power, and differing forms of complicity and resistance must be distinguished, not collapsed into one cause. The history asks what religious interpretation can permit institutions to disregard; Jewish suffering must not become merely material for Christian self-improvement. *Nostra aetate* §4 offers an instance of authoritative correction: Jews “should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God.” Doctrine can restrain harm as well as be used to sustain it. [RD-09](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-09--christian-interpretation-and-the-holocaust), [RD-10](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-10--authoritative-teaching-can-also-correct-harmful-interpretation)

The antidote to dogmatism is therefore not indifference. It includes study, prayer, listening, concrete care, and willingness to change what we do when harm becomes visible. It also includes allowing a person to disagree with this essay without making that disagreement evidence against their heart.

## 5. Humanity without distance from the divine

### A human teacher, a human transmission

The teaching reaches us through lives as well as words. Matthew retains the controversy; Luke places the warning beside the promise that “the Holy Spirit will teach you at that time what you should say” (12:12, NIV). The different settings guide different acts of understanding. *Dei verbum* §19 recognizes the evangelists as “selecting some things from the many which had been handed on,” while affirming the truth of the Gospels. Interpretation is already part of how the tradition is given, not simply something later readers add. [B-03–B-04](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-03), [R-09](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-09)

Jesus’s humanity belongs within this stream. Luke says that he “grew in wisdom” (2:52, NIV); John shows him “tired as he was from the journey” (4:6, NIV). Mark gives us prayer, anger, grief, questions, and encounters under pressure. These are not incidental concessions to a human appearance. They are part of the life through which his teaching is encountered. A human teacher speaks to particular hearers, with words that may console in one moment and confront in another. [HT-02](../sources/humanity-and-provocative-teaching.md#ht-02)

To attend to this humanity is not necessarily to take something from his divinity. The Catholic Catechism itself holds together “true God and true man” (§464), and, quoting the Second Vatican Council, says that he “thought with a human mind” and “with a human heart he loved” (§470). That tradition makes claims beyond describing a divinely inspired teacher, but it does not require us to treat genuine humanity as an embarrassment. The question is whether we can encounter the human life more fully without imagining that the divine must therefore recede. [HT-02](../sources/humanity-and-provocative-teaching.md#ht-02)

### The only child and the whole world

The language of divine sonship can seem to place Jesus at an unreachable distance. John calls him God’s “one and only Son” (3:16, NIV), yet also speaks of those who receive him becoming “children of God” (1:12, NIV). These statements are not identical, but neither is the second insignificant. The Gospel’s particular claim about Jesus accompanies an invitation into relationship with God. [HT-03](../sources/humanity-and-provocative-teaching.md#ht-03)

We should not make “only begotten” mean something the Greek does not say. The word *monogenēs* identifies the only or unique one in a relationship; in John these passages refer to Jesus. It does not translate as “each of us is God’s only child.” But we can ask a further theological question: could the image of an only child help us receive and extend a love that does not treat anyone as expendable? To speak of each person as loved *as if an only child* would then be a contemplative image, not a correction of John’s wording.

The Buddhist *Discourse on Love* makes a related movement. In Thích Nhất Hạnh’s translation, the example is “a mother loves and protects her only child at the risk of her own life.” The teaching asks that such wholehearted care be extended to all beings. Matthew’s call to “love your enemies” (5:44, NIV), grounded in the Father’s care for the evil and the good, offers a Christian point of encounter. Neither passage asks us to call harmful conduct harmless. They ask whether love must be divided according to who belongs, who agrees, or who has become difficult to love. [HT-04](../sources/humanity-and-provocative-teaching.md#ht-04)

This brings us back to the people addressed in Mark. Can we hear the warning without first removing its hearers from the field of care? Can we see Jesus’s concern, their humanity, and the seriousness of the accusation together? We do not have to flatten the differences between them to refuse a picture in which some human lives cease to matter.

### A person is more than a description

A statement about what someone has done can become the final account of who they are. This is not a choice between logic and insight. Reasoning can help us notice what a conclusion requires; it cannot decide in advance that our description has captured the whole person. A truthful account of wrongdoing remains necessary without becoming an exhaustive account of a life. [ND-08](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-08--logic-and-the-choice-of-a-description)

Thích Nhất Hạnh approaches historical and ultimate dimensions through the image of wave and water. The wave’s arising and passing are not denied, but neither do they exhaust what the wave is. He cautions that “interbeing does not mean that everything is one or that everything is different.” Applied to our inquiry, this asks us to resist two forms of closure: identifying someone completely with a past act, or imagining that transformation produces an unrelated person with no responsibility for it. [ND-02](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-02--historical-and-ultimate-neither-monism-nor-separation)

Sister Annabel Laity gives this a temporal expression: “The three times inter-are.” Past action continues in its consequences; how we respond now helps form what comes next. Her discussion of regret turns toward acting differently in the present, not making the past unhappen. Transformation can mean entering responsibility more fully. It does not require another person to forgive, reconcile, or restore access, and it does not decide how long a consequence may endure. [PV-04](../sources/plum-village/time-and-transformation.md#pv-04)

### Allowing the question to work on us

The nondual inquiry is not a way around Mark’s words. It asks whether we are standing outside them, classifying other people, or allowing the warning to touch our own attachment. This is the koan-like element of the reading: the question includes the person asking it. We may discover that what needs to change is not only our proposed answer, but how we listen, speak, and respond to someone we had already decided we understood. [ND-04](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-04--a-koan-involving-the-reader)

Such attention also tests our preferred picture of Jesus’s severity. The Third Mindfulness Training commits practitioners “not to force others” to adopt their views. A warning may help someone recognize danger, or it may pressure them to submit to authority. Claiming a compassionate intention does not settle the difference. Truthfulness, power, freedom to respond, and the effects on those addressed remain questions for any teacher—including teachers who invoke Zen provocation to defend their conduct. [ND-01](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-01--openness-and-nonattachment-to-views)

John’s phrase “whoever lives by the truth comes into the light” (3:21, NIV) joins understanding with a life that can be examined. An interpretation of mercy must become more than a persuasive account of mercy. Its meaning also appears in whether we can tell the truth about harm, care for those affected, and remain willing to learn. [B-11](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-11)

## 6. The warning before us

We can return to the opening now as part of an encounter rather than an isolated rule. The promise of forgiveness does not simply soften the warning. Its breadth may be what gives the warning its startling force. Jesus speaks to people who have called the work of liberation evil, and the language of judgment confronts their own judgment. Heard as a provocative act of teaching, the saying asks what they—and we—have become unable to receive.

This does not settle the meaning of finality. The Greek remains emphatic, the other Gospel passages remain difficult, and the interpreters do not reach one answer. Nor does a new understanding undo suffering or automatically restore a relationship. Those questions retain their seriousness. But they need not keep us from receiving what the warning may ask of us now.

Humanizing Jesus does not require making him ordinary in the sense of insignificant. It means allowing his teaching to arise within a human life, with its relationships, conflicts, grief, courage, and love. The disciples, religious teachers, evangelists, and later interpreters belong to that human world too. Reverence can bring us closer to these lives rather than place them beyond understanding.

Our own account of forgiveness can remain generous in its claims while becoming closed in practice. We can defend mercy and fail to hear someone asking for accountability; we can defend truth while refusing an encounter that might deepen it. The question is not whether we have found a formulation that no longer troubles us. It is whether we can remain with the teaching in a way that changes how we live. Whom have we already decided we understand, and what might become possible if we listened again?
