# All Will Be Forgiven?
## Compassion, Warning, and our Shared Humanity

> *“Truly I tell you, people can be forgiven all their sins and every slander they utter, but whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit will never be forgiven; they are guilty of an eternal sin.”*
>
> — Mark 3:28–29, New International Version (2011). [RD-00](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-00--niv-opening)

## 1. The tension

In one movement, this saying opens forgiveness across the range of human sin and slander, then names a stark and permanent exception. What distinguishes the second saying from the first? The words may name a harmful act, expose the condition expressed through it, and warn of its consequences. They may also be an attempt to awaken the hearers to a danger they have not understood. These possibilities need not exclude one another. There is a need for us to look deeply and consider this teaching; the severity of the words does not relieve us of the need to interpret them. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

One consideration is that the severity of the warning is offered out of compassion. An effort to reach someone may need to be sharp enough to interrupt a judgment that has become protected from correction. This is the possibility we will explore, while keeping a distinction in view: a compassionate warning might seek to avert a real and irreversible loss. Its purpose does not, by itself, give us the meaning of “eternal sin.”

We bring our own histories to these words. Forgiveness may suggest pardon, release from debt, relinquished resentment, or reconciliation; guilt may mean being answerable for an act or feeling guilty about it. As we look at the passage, we will need to ask both what its words mean and how they are being used. They come to us through a human encounter, remembered and arranged within a Gospel, then translated and received as teaching. What happens when an address to someone becomes a judgment we pronounce about someone?

## 2. A warning within an encounter

The controversy concerns the source of Jesus’s power. Earlier in the chapter, Mark describes people coming to be healed and impure spirits falling before Jesus (3:7–12). Teachers of the law arrive from Jerusalem and give a very different explanation: “By the prince of demons he is driving out demons” (3:22, NIV). They attribute his power to Beelzebul, identified in their accusation with the ruler of demons. Their claim does not simply deny that something has happened. It interprets Jesus’s power over demons as evidence that he serves their ruler. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

Jesus answers the explanation rather than simply dismissing the people who offer it. He “called them over to him” and asks, “How can Satan drive out Satan?” (3:23, NIV). A kingdom or household divided against itself cannot stand. If Satan is attacking his own dominion, then that dominion is coming apart. The image of the strong man develops the reply: someone who enters a strong man’s house and takes his possessions must first overpower him (3:24–27). In these images, Jesus’s work is not an alliance with destructive power but an assault upon its hold. The hearers are being asked to reconsider what they have taken as evidence against him.

Only then does Jesus speak of forgiveness and the sin against the Holy Spirit. Mark explains why: they were saying, “He has an impure spirit” (3:30, NIV). This returns us to the accusation in verse 22. The contrast is between the Holy Spirit and the impure spirit to which the teachers attribute Jesus’s work. What the narrative presents as liberation through God’s power is being identified as the activity of its enemy. The warning is directed at this reversal, not at an unrelated verbal mistake.

That connection also matters for a compassionate reading. Jesus is responding to something the hearers are already doing. We cannot move the danger entirely into the future, as though the warning concerned a state with no relation to their present words. We can, however, ask whether the severity is intended to make them recognize where their judgment is leading. The summons, the argument, and the warning belong to one continuing address. Jesus is still speaking to the people whose explanation he rejects.

A saying does something within a relationship. It can warn, rebuke, invite, unsettle, or console; its meaning is not exhausted by identifying the proposition it asserts. Earlier in Mark 3, in a separate healing encounter, Jesus’s anger is accompanied by grief: he is “deeply distressed at their stubborn hearts” (3:5, NIV). Concern and confrontation are not presented as opposites. Commenting on Matthew’s parallel, John Chrysostom likewise describes Jesus’s purpose as “to do good to them that were sinning” (*Homily 41 on Matthew*, §1). His interpretation will need closer attention, but it already gives a Christian precedent for hearing severe speech as an attempt to reach those addressed. [RD-01](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-01--hardness-before-the-blasphemy-controversy), [R-02](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-02)

We need not claim to know the accusers’ private thoughts to notice the danger in their explanation. If every sign of liberation can be treated as further evidence of evil, what could lead them to reconsider? A judgment may become protected by the very reasoning through which it is defended. This is what we are beginning to call rigidity: not simply being mistaken, but holding an understanding in a way that prevents what challenges it from being heard. Mark does not explicitly say that the teachers recognized divine action inwardly and knowingly lied. Rigidity is our proposal about what may sustain their accusation, not a substitute meaning for blasphemy.

The proposal brings the warning into contact with a recognizable human difficulty. It does not yet explain why Mark speaks so absolutely about forgiveness. To follow that question, we need to examine the words and the different ways Christians have understood them.

## 3. What the words require and interpreters add

### The breadth and the exception

The movement from forgiveness to its exception is especially clear when the two clauses are placed beside one another. The first is compact:

**Greek:** πάντα ἀφεθήσεται.

**Romanization:** *panta aphethēsetai*.

**Literal gloss:** πάντα, all things; ἀφεθήσεται, will be forgiven.

**Natural English:** “All will be forgiven,” with the following words specifying human sins and blasphemies. The NIV’s “can be forgiven” remains in the opening quotation; the study rendering makes the Greek future passive visible. The scope is the whole range of sins and blasphemies, qualified by the exception that follows, rather than a separate promise that every individual will ultimately be reconciled. [B-01–B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-01)

The exception is no less direct:

**Greek:** οὐκ ἔχει ἄφεσιν εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, ἀλλὰ ἔνοχός ἐστιν αἰωνίου ἁμαρτήματος.

**Romanization:** *ouk echei aphesin eis ton aiōna, alla enochos estin aiōniou hamartēmatos*.

**Literal gloss:** οὐκ ἔχει, does not have; ἄφεσιν, forgiveness/remission; εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, into/unto the age; ἀλλὰ, but; ἔνοχός ἐστιν, is guilty/liable; αἰωνίου ἁμαρτήματος, of/for an eternal sin.

**Natural English:** “Never has forgiveness, but is guilty of an eternal sin.” An alternative is “has no forgiveness forever, but is answerable for an eternal sin.” The negative idiom has the force of “never”; the mechanical gloss “into the age” does not supply an endpoint. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02), [L-01](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-01)

Mark attaches his explanation directly to this warning:

**Greek:** ὅτι ἔλεγον· Πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον ἔχει.

**Romanization:** *hoti elegon: pneuma akatharton echei*.

**Literal gloss:** ὅτι, because; ἔλεγον, they were saying; πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον, an unclean/impure spirit; ἔχει, he has.

**Natural English:** “Because they were saying, ‘He has an unclean spirit.’” This is a study rendering; the NIV uses “impure.” The imperfect locates the accusation in the ongoing scene. It does not itself promise forgiveness when the speech stops. [B-02](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-02)

The grammar therefore holds together something we may be tempted to separate: a judgment on what people are saying in this encounter and an emphatic statement about forgiveness. A reading that understands the condition as changeable has to explain that temporal force, not simply appeal to the present tense of “has.”

### Sin, release, and liability

Our English words can make the passage sound more familiar than it is. Even within the Gospels, sin and forgiveness are approached through different images. Matthew’s prayer asks God to “forgive us our debts” (6:12, NIV); Luke’s begins, “Forgive us our sins,” then speaks of forgiving those indebted to us (11:4, NIV). Wrongdoing is understood through an obligation that can be remitted, not only through a rule that has been broken. These neighboring uses do not give us a complete history of sin, but they show why one modern definition cannot carry every association of the biblical language. [L-08](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-08)

The word for forgiveness in Mark, ἄφεσις (*aphesis*), likewise belongs to the language of release and remission. Luke uses it in the proclamation to “set the oppressed free” (4:18, NIV), and later for the forgiveness of sins (24:47). The contexts are different: liberation from oppression is not simply another name for pardon, nor is pardon merely relief from an inward feeling. The comparison gives substance to the question of release while requiring us to attend to what someone is being released from. [L-03](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-03), [L-08](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-08)

In Mark, forgiveness remains the appropriate ordinary translation. To relate it to receptivity is an interpretation of how mercy may be received, not a complete definition of mercy. A changed understanding, divine forgiveness, reconciliation with another person, and restored trust can be connected without being the same event. The Spirit also remains an agent in the Christian account, not simply a name for human insight.

The final phrase places another limit on our interpretation. The SBL Greek New Testament reads “eternal sin,” rather than the “eternal judgment” found in another textual tradition. The noun ἁμάρτημα (*hamartēma*) names sin or wrongdoing; “offense” is a possible English rendering, not an additional word in the sentence. Meanwhile, ἔνοχος (*enochos*), translated “guilty” or “liable,” concerns being answerable, not merely feeling guilty. Judicial language is already available in the ancient text, even though the sentence does not explicitly name a punishment. Its meaning cannot be reduced either to a modern criminal sentence or to a purely psychological obstacle. [L-01–L-04](../sources/greek-and-semantic-history.md#l-01)

### Where does the tradition locate the refusal?

These distinctions help us understand why the same words have supported different accounts of repentance. One recurring question is whether the warning concerns an accusation, the disposition expressed through it, or a refusal maintained until it becomes final. The interpreters differ not only over the seriousness of the sin, but over what would have to change for forgiveness to become possible.

Chrysostom confronts the problem within his own reading. After considering whether even repentance would fail, he writes, “For even this was forgiven upon repentance” (*Homily 41*, §5). He says some who made the accusation later believed and received pardon. Yet he continues to describe the offense as exceptionally culpable and its consequences as extending into the coming life. His acknowledgment is not a simple resolution of the tension. Nor can it be separated from the homily’s anti-Jewish polemic, including its punitive interpretation of collective suffering. He helps us see that correction and severity have long been held together, but his account also needs the scrutiny we bring to any inherited interpretation. [R-02](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-02)

Augustine places the decisive boundary at the end of a life. The unforgiven condition is final impenitence: refusing the gift of repentance until death. While someone is living, he insists, “For we are not to despair of any” (*Sermon 71*, §21; Sermon 21 in the cited English collection). On this account, we cannot look at someone’s present opposition and know that it is their final condition. The interpretation preserves hope for the living, although the boundary at death comes from Augustine’s larger theology rather than an explicit reference to death in Mark. [R-03](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-03)

Aquinas takes up this reading and makes its preventive purpose explicit: Jesus warns the hearers against reaching final impenitence through such utterances (*Summa theologiae*, II–II, q.14, a.1). In article 3, he also considers a different sense in which the sin rejects the means by which pardon comes. He compares it to a disease that defeats its ordinary remedies, while maintaining that this does not exclude healing by an “all-powerful and merciful God.” The distinction matters: resistance to a remedy need not be a limit on divine action. It also remains a distinction within a theology that affirms final loss, not a guarantee that every refusal will eventually end. [R-04](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-04)

Calvin locates the boundary differently. He distinguishes wrongdoing committed in ignorance from a knowing, malicious rejection of recognized truth. Those who have entered the latter condition, he writes, “never have any desire towards repentance.” His account does not require God to reject a repentant person: it holds that repentance will not arise. This is a stronger claim than observing that someone is presently resistant. It also shows why a defender of finality need not regard every grave act or every religious error as irreversible. [R-05](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-05)

The Catholic Catechism’s account of refusal begins with “There are no limits to the mercy of God” (§1864), then explains that deliberate rejection of repentance can lead to final impenitence and loss. George MacDonald presses a further question: must the refusal itself ever become beyond transformation? He rejects its being “a fixed, a final condition” (*It Shall Not Be Forgiven*) and asks whether the Spirit may still act in ways that prepare a person to receive what they have resisted. The difference is not whether refusal matters, but whether it must ever be the last word. [R-07](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-07), [R-06](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-06)

These accounts make different connections between the saying and a wider understanding of God and human life. An appeal to knowledge, a boundary at death, a claim of irreversible hardening, or confidence in further divine action does work that a dictionary cannot do. This is not automatically a distortion; interpretation necessarily makes connections. But recognizing them allows us to ask where each account finds its support and what remains unresolved.

### The objection that remains

A reader may agree that Jesus is speaking out of concern and still conclude that his words announce an actual, final loss. Matthew makes the temporal scope explicit: the blasphemy “will not be forgiven, either in this age or in the age to come” (12:32, NIV). Mark’s next chapter presents a further difficulty. His account of parables connects outsiders’ lack of understanding with a failure to turn and receive forgiveness: “otherwise they might turn and be forgiven!” (4:12, NIV). This complicates any account in which every challenging saying is transparently intended to open understanding. [B-03](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-03), [RD-11](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-11--hard-evidence-the-proposal-must-retain)

We cannot answer these passages merely by naming compassion. The purpose of a warning, the danger it describes, and what finally happens to its hearers are related but distinct questions. Our exploration gives special attention to the possibility of a compassionate address while leaving its relation to finality open. A contrary conclusion may also arise from an attentive and compassionate reading.

## 4. Rigidity, changed seeing, and responsibility

### Conviction that remains answerable

With these difficulties in view, we can return to the proposed condition underlying the accusation. Rigidity does not mean having convictions, belonging to a tradition, or holding firmly to a moral obligation. It means protecting a judgment from correction: refusing to let what happens, what another person says, or what our actions produce call that judgment into question. The problem is not simply that we have an interpretation, but that our interpretation has become exempt from examination.

Dogmatism gives this rigidity a doctrinal form. A formulation becomes the final measure by which every encounter is judged; what fails to fit may be dismissed before it is heard. The First Mindfulness Training of the Order of Interbeing commits practitioners to “transform dogmatism and violence in ourselves and the world.” Its inclusion of Buddhist doctrines is important. The commitment concerns our attachment to our own teachings, not only the errors we can identify in someone else’s. The Second Training develops this through listening and continuing inquiry. The teachings remain guidance for understanding and compassion rather than an exemption from having to learn. [ND-01](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-01--openness-and-nonattachment-to-views)

Mark directs a related question toward the disciples. Jesus asks them, “Are your hearts hardened?” and “Don’t you remember?” (8:17–18, NIV), then continues to question and teach them. Later they try to stop an exorcist because “he was not one of us”; Jesus answers, “Do not stop him” (9:38–39, NIV). Their sense of belonging threatens to obstruct the work they encounter. These passages do not equate every misunderstanding with the sin of 3:29. They do make it difficult to locate the problem only in Jesus’s opponents, or to assume that every hardened response has already become final. [RD-02](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-02--the-disciples-are-not-exempt), [ND-05](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-05--a-healing-outside-the-disciples-group)

### Paul and the possibility of changed seeing

Paul brings the question into a life marked not just by misunderstanding but by persecution. He recalls, “I persecuted the church of God and tried to destroy it,” before describing God’s revelation of the Son (Galatians 1:13–16, NIV). Acts tells the story through blindness, restored sight, and Ananias’s words: “you may see again and be filled with the Holy Spirit” (9:17, NIV). The person who had acted against the community comes to recognize what he had opposed. [B-05](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-05), [B-07](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-07)

First Timothy makes a qualification that Calvin’s reading takes seriously: “I was shown mercy because I acted in ignorance and unbelief” (1:13, NIV). Acts and 1 Timothy are not interchangeable with Paul’s own testimony; the authorship of 1 Timothy is disputed. Nor do the sources examined here place him in Mark’s scene or attribute its particular accusation to him. His life therefore does not establish that the exact sin named in Mark was committed and then forgiven. It asks us instead to consider what the Spirit can do in someone actively opposed to the community that claims to live by that Spirit. [B-08](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-08)

That question also prevents openness from becoming an achievement we demand of a person before anything can change. Paul does not merely decide to improve his account of the world. An encounter interrupts the understanding through which he has been interpreting it. In the Christian narrative, the Spirit is involved in making a new response possible.

Our proposal can now be stated more carefully. If we refuse to see, we do not see; while healing is named evil, it cannot be welcomed as healing. Yet observing present refusal does not establish that no encounter could change it. Nor does showing that a person can change settle forgiveness: a lasting consequence can be attributed to a changing person. Mark does not explicitly give us an “only while” condition, and we have to hold the possibility of changed seeing beside the force of his words. Paul keeps transformation within the inquiry without resolving all its questions.

### When the interpreter is implicated

The same inquiry turns toward us. A Christian interpreter can defend an account of goodness while refusing to hear the people harmed by its application. Even forgiveness can be used this way when it requires an injured person to cease speaking before responsibility has been acknowledged. We would then be protecting our interpretation from the very experience that calls it into question. A boundary against further harm is not rigidity, and reluctance to trust a teacher’s claim of healing is not blasphemy.

The Gospels themselves distinguish profession from conduct. Matthew warns about those who say “Lord, Lord” (7:21, NIV), including people who claim exorcisms and powerful deeds in Jesus’s name. A claim of healing is not its own verification. The judgment scene in Matthew 25 makes care concrete: “whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers and sisters of mine, you did for me” (25:40, NIV). The identity of those described as the least is disputed, and the ending retains severe judgment. Nevertheless, the scene places recognition within acts of care, not profession alone. [RD-04](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-04--profession-claimed-power-and-conduct), [RD-05](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-05--hunger-imprisonment-recognition-and-judgment)

The history of Christian anti-Judaism makes this responsibility more than an individual matter. In its account of the German churches under Nazism, the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum observes that “traditional interpretations of religious scriptures seemed to support these prejudices.” It distinguishes inherited Christian anti-Judaism, Nazi racial ideology, political conditions, and different forms of complicity and resistance. Christian doctrine did not singly cause the Holocaust, but Christian teaching and conduct belong to the history that must be examined. This is not evidence that our reading of Mark is correct. It is a reason to ask what a religious interpretation can permit people and institutions to disregard, without reducing Jewish suffering to a lesson for someone else’s spiritual development. [RD-09](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-09--christian-interpretation-and-the-holocaust)

Correction can also become part of authoritative teaching. *Nostra aetate* §4 states that Jews “should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God.” That declaration does not undo earlier injury, but it gives a doctrinal basis for opposing an inherited harm. The problem is therefore not the existence of a formulation. It is whether our relationship to it permits us to recognize when it needs correction. [RD-10](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md#rd-10--authoritative-teaching-can-also-correct-harmful-interpretation)

## 5. Receiving a teaching without fixing the person

### The humanity of transmission

These questions of responsibility begin with how a teaching reaches us. Matthew retains the controversy and makes the temporal expression explicit; Luke places the warning beside instructions about testimony before authorities, where “the Holy Spirit will teach you at that time what you should say” (12:12, NIV). The settings direct our attention differently: in one, the Spirit’s work is being maligned; in the other, allegiance, testimony, and the Spirit’s assistance are closely joined. The Gospels already interpret the saying as they hand it on. Their differences do not establish exactly which words Jesus originally spoke, but they show that receiving the tradition involves more than repetition. [B-03–B-04](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-03)

Human participation need not be a story of degeneration. It can preserve insight as well as change it. *Dei verbum* §19 describes the evangelists as “selecting some things from the many which had been handed on,” while also affirming Gospel truth. It recognizes synthesis and explanation in response to the situations of their churches. A later formulation may likewise preserve a warning’s urgency, make its implications explicit, or prevent a harmful use. The question is what is carried forward from the encounter, and what happens when the words are addressed to different people under different conditions. [R-09](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-09)

Jesus’s humanity belongs here too. We need not protect him from every difficult saying by assigning it to someone else, nor claim to reconstruct his thoughts or mistakes across two millennia. We can attend to the life the Gospels present: a teacher speaking within relationships, through stories and arguments, in response to particular people. His anger, grief, and willingness to confront are not details to remove before we can consider his compassion. Humanity may be the medium through which revelation becomes available, rather than an obstacle it must pass through untouched.

### Continuity without an exhaustive description

One thing that can change in transmission is the kind of judgment we believe a saying authorizes. Words addressed to a person in a grave encounter may later be used to define that person altogether. A statement about what someone has done becomes the final account of who they are. We should ask whether that further step belongs to the text or to the way we have come to use it.

This is not a choice between logic and insight. An inference can be valid while its premises or description remain inadequate. Logic helps us identify what would have to be added to a claim about present refusal before we could conclude that it will never change. It does not settle whether Mark’s words supply that additional claim. Nor does a truthful description of wrongdoing cease to be true merely because it does not describe everything about the person. [ND-08](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-08--logic-and-the-choice-of-a-description)

Thích Nhất Hạnh’s teaching offers a way to examine our attachment to such descriptions. In a 2006 interview with Melvin McLeod, he approaches historical and ultimate dimensions through the image of wave and water. The wave’s arising and passing are not denied, but neither do they exhaust what the wave is. He cautions that “interbeing does not mean that everything is one or that everything is different.” The practice is not to exchange one fixed category for another, but to look beyond the separation or sameness our categories assume. [ND-02](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-02--historical-and-ultimate-neither-monism-nor-separation)

Applied here, this lets us distinguish without turning distinctions into permanent divisions. The person who caused harm remains answerable for it and is not exhausted by that act. We need neither identify someone wholly with a past action nor imagine that transformation produces an unrelated person with no responsibility for the past. A nondual approach can hold continuity and change together. It does not make harm and healing indistinguishable or require the harmed person to reconcile.

Sister Annabel Laity gives this a temporal expression in her account of the Plum Village teaching, “The three times inter-are.” Past action is present in its consequences; how we respond now participates in what follows. Her discussion of regret turns toward acting differently in the present, not making the past unhappen. In this light, transformation need not mean leaving responsibility behind. It may mean entering into responsibility more fully, through acknowledgment and changed conduct. This is a way of attending to a life, not a new translation of Mark’s language of eternity. [PV-04](../sources/plum-village/time-and-transformation.md#pv-04)

The historical and ultimate are therefore not separate compartments in which responsibility is first acknowledged and then dismissed. Nor does this way of seeing prove that every consequence must end. Its contribution is to prevent either condemnation or a claim to transformation from making us stop attending to the person and the lives affected.

### Compassionate speech must also be examined

That attention also asks something of our preferred reading. The Third Mindfulness Training commits practitioners “not to force others” to adopt their views, and explicitly names authority and threat among the means it rejects. When does a severe warning help someone recognize a danger, and when does it pressure them to submit to the speaker? Calling the purpose compassionate cannot answer that question in advance. [ND-01](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-01--openness-and-nonattachment-to-views)

We still need to ask whether a warning truthfully names a danger, whose interests it serves, and what room it leaves for response and disagreement. Neither is every unwelcome warning coercive. Mark’s account can challenge the assumption that care must always reassure, while the training asks us not to turn spiritual authority into an exemption from scrutiny. Our reading of Jesus’s severity must remain attentive to how similar language is used by teachers whose authority is itself in question.

Thích Nhất Hạnh’s letter *Bat Nha: a Koan* offers another point of contact: a question becomes one’s “own problem,” rather than merely a puzzle concerning other people. Approaching Mark in this koan-like way does not turn the passage historically into a Zen koan. It allows the inquiry to include the one asking. We study the words, and also notice what happens when they question our confidence in our own understanding. [ND-04](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-04--a-koan-involving-the-reader)

John’s connection between understanding and practice belongs here too: “whoever lives by the truth comes into the light” (3:21, NIV). The inquiry is not finished when we possess a satisfactory formulation. We still have to discover what that understanding becomes in our conduct, especially toward someone whose account is difficult for us to hear. [B-11](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-11)

## 6. The warning before us

We can now return to the opening with a more particular way of hearing its severity. Jesus’s warning may be an effort to reach people whose judgment has become resistant to correction. Mark places it within an encounter: the accusers are called over, their explanation is questioned, and the consequences of their words are brought before them. Concern need not be absent from such an address because the language is severe.

Neither does concern settle the question of finality. Mark’s temporal expression, Matthew’s reference to both ages, and the differing accounts of repentance remain before us. We have not shown that changed perception necessarily brings forgiveness, or that the possibility of transformation removes every lasting consequence. What we have is a way of hearing the warning that attends to the people being addressed before turning them into examples of a fixed condition.

That attention includes ourselves. Our understanding of forgiveness can remain generous in its claims while becoming closed in its practice. We can defend mercy and fail to hear someone asking for accountability; we can defend truth and disregard the life that calls our formulation into question. Receiving the warning as an address to us does not require abandoning conviction or permitting further harm. It asks whether our conviction still allows us to listen and to change.

The humanity of Jesus and his interpreters is part of where the teaching is encountered. To attend to that humanity is not to set reverence aside, but to consider what reverence asks of us in relation to actual lives. We can carry this way of listening forward without declaring every theological outcome settled. Whom would we need to hear differently if we received the warning as an address to ourselves?
