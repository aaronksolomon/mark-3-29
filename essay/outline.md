# Essay outline — a saying, its changing meanings, and its human interpreters

**Status:** active outline, v0.3. Continues Aaron’s revision `6801a0b`: shared inquiry in the introduction; dogmatism alongside rigidity; a developed human-transmission section; a focused exploration of logic, nonduality, and koan-like reading. This is a research architecture, not a settled conclusion.

**Working title:** *All Will Be Forgiven? Words, Time, and the Humanity of Interpretation*

**Underlying question:** How does a saying attributed to Jesus within a particular conflict become a doctrine about forgiveness and eternity? What changes in that passage through languages, memories, communities, texts, and readers—and what remains resistant to reinterpretation?

**Authorial direction:** Clear prose, careful references, and genuine questions. Humility includes Jesus, the evangelists, Paul, religious opponents, theologians, people harmed by interpretation, and ourselves. Do not make any group the convenient villain. Use Aaron’s inclusive, searching voice rather than imitation of another teacher; see [AGENTS.md](../AGENTS.md). Buddhist teaching participates directly in the inquiry. Personal narrative can be added later.

## Provisional thesis to test

Mark 3:28–30 preserves a severe warning within a controversy, not an independently complete account of repentance, divine judgment, or the afterlife. Its broad announcement of forgiveness is qualified by the warning against maligning the Spirit. One proposal understands the accusation as arising from rigidity: the judgment is protected by treating what might correct or heal it as evil. Dogmatism gives this attachment a doctrinal form. Mark’s hardening language, including its application to disciples, provides a narrative foothold without establishing a synonym for blasphemy. Paul’s transformation raises a canonical question about whether profound religious misrecognition can change. Buddhist nonattachment and nondual teaching help examine the permanence and separation we may import into a description. This reading must still face Mark’s emphatic temporal language and Matthew’s explicit exclusion of forgiveness in both ages.

**Do not assert as established:** that Jesus could not have said it; that legal ideas entered only later; that aiōnios necessarily means a finite age; that Paul demonstrably committed the exact sin; that every hardened heart commits that sin; or that openness alone guarantees forgiveness. Nonduality does not make contradictory formulations interchangeable or establish Buddhist influence on the Gospels. See [claim audit](../notes/claim-audit.md).

## 1. Introduction — the tension before the explanation

**Question:** What happens when an announcement of exceptionally broad forgiveness contains an exceptionally severe exception?

Begin with the complete NIV (2011) text of Mark 3:28–29 as an italicized block quotation. Keep the named translation exact: it says “can be forgiven,” whereas the subsequent Greek study gloss supplies “will be forgiven.” Follow Aaron’s questions about the act, the underlying condition, and the human responsibility of interpretation. Complete the Mark 3:30 quotation and introduce dogmatism as a question, not an established translation.

“All” refers to sins and blasphemies; it is not, by itself, a theorem that every person is eventually reconciled. Conversely, “eternal sin” should not be silently replaced by an entire doctrine of endless punishment.

Distinguish literary paradox from formal contradiction: a universal-sounding statement followed by an exception can be grammatically coherent and still create a deep theological tension. Ask whether severity can be compassionate warning without assuming Jesus’s intention has been recovered.

## 2. Words moving through worlds

**Question:** Are ancient sin, remission, liability, and age-language the same concepts modern English readers hear in sin, forgiveness, guilt, crime, punishment, and forever?

Give special attention to five words or families, not an exhaustive glossary: hamartēma, blasphēmeō, aphesis, enochos, aiōn/aiōnios. For every substantial Greek quotation use the four-part presentation in the source notes: Greek, romanization, segmented gloss, natural English.

Build a layered history rather than a single progression: classical Greek usages; Greek Jewish scriptures; diverse Second Temple settings; Mark’s sentence; later Greek and Latin reception; English translations; contemporary theological and psychological usage. Jewish law, covenant, mercy, sacrifice, debt, and judgment cannot be reduced to one model.

Compare actual ancient passages before claiming a change of meaning. Distinguish etymology from contextual usage. “Missing the mark” is not a complete definition of New Testament sin. “Release” illuminates aphesis but does not prove an exclusively psychological mechanism. Enochos already carries liability language in antiquity.

## 3. What Mark’s story permits us to say

**Question:** Is this about forbidden words, conscious malice, misrecognition, resistance to restoration, or some combination?

Read the whole scene, Mark 3:20–35: family, scribes from Jerusalem, divided kingdom/household, strong man, forgiveness saying, and verse 30’s explanation. The story interprets liberation as God’s work and the scribes’ accusation as its reversal. It does not grant modern readers direct access to the historical opponents’ inner motives.

The images of division and the strong man challenge an explanation of what is happening; they do not remove the distinction between good and harm. This matters when a later section introduces nonduality.

Attend to Mark’s wider pattern: 1:14–15 (God’s reign), 2:1–12 (forgiveness and accusations of blasphemy), 3:5 (anger and grief), 4:10–12 (a difficult seeing/forgiveness passage), and 11:25 (forgiving others). Do not omit the difficult controls.

## 4. Rigidity and dogmatism: when conviction prevents recognition

**Question:** Could a defended refusal of correction be the underlying condition of the accusation—and could interpreters reproduce that condition themselves?

**Working definitions:** rigidity protects a judgment from correction. Dogmatism gives that protection a doctrinal form, treating a formulation as an unquestionable measure of encounter. Neither term is a translation of blasphemy. Strong moral commitments and boundaries against harm are not thereby dogmatic.

### A. A practice of nonattachment

Introduce the First and Second of the Fourteen Mindfulness Trainings briefly, with [ND-01](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-01--openness-and-nonattachment-to-views). Their inclusion of Buddhist teachings within the warning is essential. Do not present them as evidence that Buddhist communities are automatically non-dogmatic. Keep this discussion within the rigidity section rather than creating a separate history of dogmatism.

### B. Narrative foothold and insider application

Read Mark 3:1–6 with 3:22–30. Give the Greek phrase for grieving at hardness of heart in the four-part format. Compare Mark 6:52 and 8:17: the disciples also have hardened hearts, and Jesus continues questioning them. The comparison applies inwardly; it does not establish that every hardened person commits the precise sin of 3:29. John 9:39–41 offers a distinct comparison between claimed sight and remaining sin. Preserve John 9:1–3’s refusal to identify physical blindness with personal sin.

### C. Profession and care

Keep the strands distinct: Matthew 7:15–27 (fruits, Lord-language, claimed exorcisms and powerful deeds, doing); Matthew 25:31–46 (care and recognition in judgment); Mark 12:38–40 (piety and exploitation of widows); James 1:22–27 (doing and care for widows and orphans; an epistle, not a saying spoken by Jesus). Matthew 7 complicates the assumption that every claimed healing validates the claimant. Matthew 25’s least brothers are disputed, and its ending retains eternal punishment.

Include Isaiah 58:6–10 to keep this ethical critique within its Jewish scriptural inheritance rather than opposing Jewish legalism to Christian mercy.

### D. Christian interpretation under its own warning

Ask whether an interpretation can protect itself by disqualifying the suffering it helps produce. Use the Holocaust with historical specificity: inherited Christian anti-Judaism intersected with Nazi racial antisemitism, nationalism, state power, and other causes. Distinguish support, accommodation, silence, resistance, and rescue. Do not claim that doctrine alone or Mark 3:29 caused the genocide. Do not diagnose past people as eternally condemned or use Jewish victims merely as illustrations in Christian self-reflection.

Pair responsibility with correction: Nostra aetate 4–5 rejects collective blame and presentations of Jews as divinely accursed. Doctrine can also prohibit harmful interpretation. Its existence does not mean the harms or their conditions have ended.

### E. Antidotes and encounters across boundaries

Self-examination; concrete care; learning and practice; testimony that can lead to institutional correction. Use Luke 10:25–37 with its Jewish scriptural opening, and Mark 9:38–40, where the disciples’ objection is to the exorcist’s nonmembership in their group. These are distinct forms of boundary-crossing, not automatic proofs of nondual metaphysics. John 4 remains a supporting comparison in the source notes rather than another full exposition in the draft.

Grace and encounter may interrupt refusal. Do not make receptivity a guaranteed act of individual will or require harmed people to undertake the transformation of those who harmed them.

**Question for author and reader:** what could require us to revise the rigidity interpretation itself?

## 5. A reported utterance and the humanity of its transmission

**Question:** What changes when speech addressed to someone becomes doctrine pronounced about someone?

Compare Mark 3:28–30, Matthew 12:31–32, and Luke 12:10 in context. Do not treat three Gospels as automatically three independent witnesses. Q and other source models are hypotheses. Do not reconstruct an Aramaic original from desired theology.

Develop the difference between what a saying asserts and what it does within an encounter: warning, rebuke, invitation, consolation, disruption. This does not eliminate its propositional content or decide its original intention. It keeps occasion and relationship present in the inquiry.

The teacher, hearer, rememberer, author, copyist, translator, and reader all participate. A linear diagram is a heuristic: actual transmission includes literary dependence, overlapping memories, reinterpretation, and oral-written interaction. Human participation is not only degeneration.

Theologically, humanity need not be the opposite of revelation. Historically situated humanity alone does not prove moral error. Keep open the question of whether every remembered formulation should become a timeless proposition. Classical Christology and the essay’s constructive proposal should not be conflated.

## 6. The tradition has already interpreted the difficulty

**Question:** What does each interpretation add, and what problem does it solve or leave unresolved?

Use a compact reception sequence, not a parade of authorities: Didache 11; Chrysostom, Homily 41; Augustine, Sermon 71; Aquinas, II–II q.14 a.3; Calvin; Catholic teaching; MacDonald. Preserve hostile or punitive contexts alongside hopeful statements. Distinguish MacDonald’s *It Shall Not Be Forgiven* from the age-long treatment in *Light*.

Five interpretive possibilities: historical accusation; final impenitence; refusal of the means of pardon; irreversible hardening; ultimately restorative judgment. None may be passed off as a word-for-word translation.

## 7. Paul as a question within the canon

**Question:** Can a person who actively opposes what he later recognizes as God’s work become able to see differently?

Start with Galatians 1:13–16 and Philippians 3:5–6. Distinguish Acts 9:17–18’s narrative of sight and Spirit and 1 Timothy 1:13–16’s portrait of a blasphemer receiving mercy. Record the authorship issue rather than calling all these sources Paul’s autobiographical testimony.

Objection to face: ignorance in 1 Timothy might distinguish Paul from knowledgeable rejection. Mark does not explicitly describe the scribes’ inward recognition; Paul is not proven to occupy their exact category. His life tests a broad claim about incurability, not every possible reading of Mark.

Do not make Paul the gentle corrective to harsh Gospel authors: Galatians 1:8–9 also contains severe boundary language. All remain human interpreters.

## 8. The constructive question — can refusal change?

Retain Aaron’s formulation: “If you refuse to see, you will not see.” Ask what follows when resistance is interrupted by insight, relationship, grace, or the Spirit. Distinguish lack of capacity, trauma, honest disagreement, and culpable refusal.

While healing is called evil, it cannot be welcomed as healing. The claim that this can never change is stronger. Nevertheless, Mark’s “never” is real counterevidence, and the proposed “while” clause is not supplied by grammar. Mark 4:10–12 complicates a model in which perception is entirely a matter of individual willingness.

## 9. Holding the teaching without fixing the person

**Question:** Can we distinguish without making every distinction a permanent division?

This is one focused section, not a survey of nonclassical logic or Buddhist schools. Keep roughly the scale of the present draft section 8.

### A. Reasoning and its descriptions

Distinguish validity, true premises, adequate descriptions, and lived realization. Do not blame logic itself for every rigid category. The inference from present refusal to permanent refusal requires an added premise; openness alone also does not guarantee release. The choice and scope of the formulation are part of interpretation. [ND-08](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-08--logic-and-the-choice-of-a-description) retains a simple formal example outside the essay.

### B. The two truths and continuity

Use TNH’s specific account of historical/ultimate dimensions and his rejection of both simple sameness and complete separation. Do not treat “two truths” as two contradictory opinions or two worlds, or equate every Buddhist school’s vocabulary. Present our application clearly: a person remains answerable without being exhausted by an act. Transformation does not create an unrelated person who escapes responsibility.

Bring in the three times through [PV-04](../sources/plum-village/time-and-transformation.md#pv-04) and the official Dharma Seals article. Present practice can change how the past continues without undoing an event. No argument about the Greek idiom follows from this comparison. Nonduality does not dissolve harm, accountability, consent, or boundaries.

### C. A question that includes the reader

John 17:20–23 supplies a selective comparison: unity through mutual indwelling, with relations still present. The phrase’s Greek apparatus is in [ND-07](../sources/nonattachment-and-nondual-reading.md#nd-07--unity-without-erasing-relation). Do not add a long list of supposed nondual Gospel verses.

Use TNH’s *Bat Nha: a Koan* for the shift from observing someone else’s problem to becoming implicated in the question. A koan-like reading of Mark is a practice proposed for this essay, not a historical claim that the saying is a Zen koan. The paradox is not automatically a formal contradiction, and Mark’s exception is not simply assigned to one of the two truths.

Conclude with John’s abiding and doing truth (8:31–36; 7:17; 3:21). “You are truly my disciples” is present, not “you will become disciples.” Lived participation is not a license to substitute private feeling for inquiry.

## 10. Conclusion — inquiry without moral evasion

Return to the warning, the possibility of transformation, and the humanity of interpretation. Forgiveness does not erase harm or entitle a person to reconciliation or reinstatement. Learning from consequences does not make the suffering necessary or justified.

Buddhist teaching belongs to the inquiry without apology; it can question our assumptions and be questioned in turn. Truth is not a tradition’s possession, but neither is every reading equally supported. Keep the question about humanity and divinity open rather than trying to settle it by etymology or a blanket appeal to nonduality.
