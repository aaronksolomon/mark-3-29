# Essay outline — a saying, its changing meanings, and its human interpreters

**Status:** active outline, v0.2. Revised 2026-10-10: NIV opening and a new central section on rigidity, care, interpretive responsibility, and possible antidotes. This is a research architecture, not a settled conclusion.

**Working title:** *All Will Be Forgiven? Words, Time, and the Humanity of Interpretation*

**Underlying question:** How does a saying attributed to Jesus within a particular conflict become a doctrine about forgiveness and eternity? What changes in that passage through languages, memories, communities, texts, and readers—and what remains resistant to reinterpretation?

**Authorial direction:** Clear prose, careful references, and genuine questions. Humility should include Jesus, the evangelists, Paul, religious opponents, theologians, people harmed by interpretation, and ourselves. Do not make any group the convenient villain. Personal narrative can be added later.

## Provisional thesis to test

Mark 3:28–30 preserves a severe warning within a controversy, not an independently complete account of repentance, divine judgment, or the afterlife. Its broad announcement of forgiveness is qualified by the warning against maligning the Spirit. One proposal understands the accusation as arising from a rigidity of perception: the judgment is protected by treating what might correct or heal it as evil. Mark's hardening language, including its application to disciples, gives this proposal a narrative foothold without establishing a synonym for blasphemy. The same question can be asked of Christian interpretation when it protects harmful readings from examination. Paul’s transformation raises a canonical question about whether profound religious misrecognition can change. This reading must still face Mark’s emphatic temporal language and Matthew’s explicit exclusion of forgiveness in both ages.

**Do not assert as established:** that Jesus could not have said it; that legal ideas entered only later; that aiōnios necessarily means a finite age; that Paul demonstrably committed the exact sin; that every hardened heart commits that sin; or that openness alone mechanically produces forgiveness. See [claim audit](../notes/claim-audit.md) and the [rigidity dossier](../sources/rigidity-and-discernment.md).

## 1. Introduction — the tension before the explanation

**Question:** What happens when an announcement of exceptionally broad forgiveness contains an exceptionally severe exception?

Begin with the complete NIV (2011) text of Mark 3:28–29 as an italicized block quotation. Keep the named translation exact: it says “can be forgiven,” whereas the subsequent Greek study gloss supplies “will be forgiven.” Follow with questions about the act, the underlying condition, and the human responsibility of interpretation. Foreshadow rigidity without deciding that the hypothesis has solved the verse.

“All” refers to sins and blasphemies; it is not, by itself, a theorem that every person is eventually reconciled. Conversely, “eternal sin” should not be silently replaced by an entire doctrine of endless punishment.

Distinguish literary paradox from formal contradiction: a universal-sounding statement followed by an exception can be grammatically coherent and still create a deep theological tension.

## 2. Words moving through worlds

**Question:** Are ancient sin, remission, liability, and age-language the same concepts modern English readers hear in sin, forgiveness, guilt, crime, punishment, and forever?

Give special attention to five words or families, not an exhaustive glossary: hamartēma, blasphēmeō, aphesis, enochos, aiōn/aiōnios. For every substantial Greek quotation use the four-part presentation in the source notes: Greek, romanization, segmented gloss, natural English.

Build a layered history rather than a single progression: classical Greek usages; Greek Jewish scriptures; diverse Second Temple settings; Mark’s sentence; later Greek and Latin reception; English translations; contemporary theological and psychological usage. Jewish law, covenant, mercy, sacrifice, debt, and judgment cannot be reduced to one model.

Compare actual ancient passages before claiming a change of meaning. Distinguish etymology from contextual usage. “Missing the mark” is not a complete definition of New Testament sin. “Release” illuminates aphesis but does not prove an exclusively psychological mechanism. Enochos already carries liability language in antiquity.

## 3. What Mark’s story permits us to say

**Question:** Is this about forbidden words, conscious malice, misrecognition, resistance to restoration, or some combination?

Read the whole scene, Mark 3:20–35: family, scribes from Jerusalem, divided kingdom/household, strong man, forgiveness saying, and verse 30’s explanation. The story interprets liberation as God’s work and the scribes’ accusation as its reversal. It does not grant modern readers direct access to the historical opponents’ inner motives.

Attend to Mark’s wider pattern: 1:14–15 (God’s reign), 2:1–12 (forgiveness and accusations of blasphemy), 3:5 (anger and grief), 4:10–12 (a difficult seeing/forgiveness passage), and 11:25 (forgiving others). Do not omit the difficult controls. The next section develops the hardening comparison.

## 4. Rigidity: when conviction prevents recognition

**Question:** Could a defended refusal of correction be the underlying condition of the accusation—and could Christian interpreters reproduce that condition themselves?

**Definition:** rigidity is not commitment, tradition, or firm protection against harm. It is the protection of a judgment from examination by evidence, another person's experience, or the consequences of one's actions. This is a proposed interpretive category, not a diagnosis of historical persons or the dictionary meaning of blasphemy.

### A. Narrative foothold and insider application

Read Mark 3:1–6 with 3:22–30. Give the Greek phrase for grieving at hardness of heart in the four-part format. Compare Mark 6:52 and 8:17: the disciples also have hardened hearts, and Jesus continues questioning them. The comparison makes the warning applicable inwardly; it does not establish that every hardened person commits the precise sin of 3:29. John 9:39–41 offers a separate comparison between claimed sight and remaining sin. Preserve John 9:1–3's refusal to identify physical blindness with personal sin.

### B. Profession and care

Keep the strands distinct: Matthew 7:15–27 (fruits, Lord-language, claimed exorcisms and powerful deeds, doing); Matthew 25:31–46 (care and recognition in judgment); Mark 12:38–40 (piety and exploitation of widows); James 1:22–27 (doing and care for widows and orphans; an epistle, not a saying spoken by Jesus). Matthew 7 complicates the assumption that every claimed healing validates the claimant. Matthew 25's least brothers are disputed, and its ending retains eternal punishment. These are controls, not inconvenient details to omit.

Include Isaiah 58:6–10 to keep this ethical critique within its Jewish scriptural inheritance rather than opposing Jewish legalism to Christian mercy.

### C. Christian interpretation under its own warning

Ask whether an interpretation can protect itself by disqualifying the suffering it helps produce. Use the Holocaust with historical specificity: inherited Christian anti-Judaism intersected with Nazi racial antisemitism, nationalism, state power, and other causes. Distinguish support, accommodation, silence, resistance, and rescue. Do not claim that doctrine alone or Mark 3:29 caused the genocide. Do not diagnose past people as eternally condemned or use Jewish victims merely as illustrations in Christian self-reflection.

Pair responsibility with a concrete instance of correction: Nostra aetate 4–5 rejects collective blame and presentations of Jews as divinely accursed. Doctrine can also prohibit harmful interpretation. Its existence does not mean the harms or their conditions have ended.

### D. Antidotes as practices to investigate

Self-examination rather than contempt; receiving testimony without making affected people responsible for one's transformation; concrete care rather than profession alone; sustained study, prayer, and practice; conduct and consequences open to scrutiny; acknowledgment and institutional correction when harm is shown. Tie these to passages rather than presenting a free-standing modern checklist. Grace and encounter may interrupt refusal: do not make receptivity a guaranteed act of individual will.

**Question for the author and reader:** what could require us to revise the rigidity interpretation itself?

## 5. A reported utterance and the humanity of its transmission

**Question:** What can we know about the historical occasion, and what belongs to each evangelist’s literary and theological work?

Compare Mark 3:28–30, Matthew 12:31–32, and Luke 12:10 within their different contexts. Avoid treating three Gospels as automatically three independent witnesses. Q and other source models are hypotheses. Do not reconstruct an Aramaic original from desired theology.

The teacher, hearer, rememberer, author, copyist, translator, and reader all participate. A linear diagram is a heuristic: actual transmission includes literary dependence, overlapping memories, reinterpretation, and oral-written interaction.

Theologically, genuine humanity need not be treated as the opposite of revelation. Nor does historically situated humanity alone prove moral error. Keep open the stronger question of whether every remembered formulation should become a timeless proposition. Classical Christology and the essay’s constructive proposal should not be conflated.

**Balancing source:** [R-09, Dei verbum](../sources/interpretation-history.md#r-09) recognizes authorial selection and explanation while retaining Catholic truth claims.

## 6. The tradition has already interpreted the difficulty

**Question:** What does each interpretation add to the wording, and what problem does it solve or leave unresolved?

Use a compact reception sequence, not a parade of authorities: Didache 11; Chrysostom, Homily 41; Augustine, Sermon 71; Aquinas, II–II q.14 a.3; Calvin; Catholic teaching; MacDonald. Preserve hostile or punitive contexts alongside hopeful statements. Distinguish MacDonald’s *It Shall Not Be Forgiven* from the age-long treatment in *Light*.

Five interpretive possibilities: historical accusation; final impenitence; refusal of the means of pardon; irreversible hardening; ultimately restorative judgment. None may be passed off as a word-for-word translation.

**Sources:** [reception notes](../sources/interpretation-history.md).

## 7. Paul as a question within the canon

**Question:** Can a person who actively opposes what he later recognizes as God’s work become able to see differently?

Start with Galatians 1:13–16 and Philippians 3:5–6. Then distinguish the narrative of sight and Spirit in Acts 9:17–18 and the portrait of a blasphemer receiving mercy in 1 Timothy 1:13–16. Record the authorship issue rather than calling all these sources Paul’s autobiographical testimony.

Objection to face: ignorance in 1 Timothy might distinguish Paul from knowledgeable rejection. Reply must remain modest: Mark does not explicitly describe the scribes’ inward recognition; Paul is not proven to occupy their exact category. His life tests a broad claim about incurability, not Mark’s every possible interpretation.

Do not make Paul the gentle corrective to harsh Gospel authors: Galatians 1:8–9 also contains severe boundary language. All remain human interpreters.

## 8. The constructive question — can refusal change?

Retain Aaron’s formulation: “If you refuse to see, you will not see.” Ask what follows when resistance is interrupted by insight, relationship, grace, or the Spirit. Distinguish lack of capacity, trauma, honest disagreement, and culpable refusal; unwillingness is not an adequate explanation of every inability to see.

Proposed interpretation: while healing is called evil, it cannot be welcomed as healing. The additional claim that this can never change is substantially stronger. Nevertheless, Mark’s “never” is real counterevidence, and the proposed “while” clause is not supplied by grammar. Mark 4:10–12 complicates a model in which perception is entirely a matter of individual willingness.

## 9. Time, continuation, and lived knowing

**Question:** Can present transformation change a person’s relationship to past action without erasing the action or its consequences?

Introduce the Plum Village interbeing of the three times explicitly as comparison, not ancient Greek semantics or a physics claim. Sister Annabel Laity’s direct formulation has now been located; distinguish her article from TNH talk descriptions.

Bring in John 8:31–36, 7:17, and 3:21 selectively. “You are truly my disciples” is present, not “you will become disciples.” Abiding and knowing are related; John is not merely endorsing autonomous experience. John 9:39–41 is now introduced in the rigidity section; do not repeat the same exposition here.

**Sources:** [time and transformation](../sources/plum-village/time-and-transformation.md), [B-09–B-12](../sources/biblical-texts.md#b-09).

## 10. Conclusion — inquiry without moral evasion

Return to both the warning and the possibility of transformation. Do not equate forgiveness with erasure of harm, entitlement to reconciliation, or institutional reinstatement. Do not infer that suffering was necessary or justified because someone learned from it.

End with a question about the relation between continuing refusal and enduring guilt, and about the human responsibility of interpretation. Humanity and divinity can remain the constructive horizon without being settled by an etymology. The caution against rigidity must include the essay's own preferred reading.
