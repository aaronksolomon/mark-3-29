## 1. Overall assessment and scope

Keep v5 and its six-section structure. The essay now moves coherently from a particular saying, through its human and linguistic setting, into the ways a teaching is received and lived. The conclusion follows from that movement rather than merely summarizing the research. Another general compression pass would risk removing the sentences that allow the thought to develop.

The principal remaining revision is within section 4. Its present movement from Christian anti-Judaism to contemporary anti-Muslim prejudice is ethically intelligible, but the historical bridge is missing. The first discussion concerns church teaching and institutions; the second begins with society-wide evidence. A reader can understand the concern while still wondering what the second example demonstrates about Christian interpretation.

The repair should be a named instance of ecclesiastical discrimination against Muslims, followed by evidence that church teaching can also correct exclusion, then the present responsibility of Christian communities within wider society. This is more exact than either blaming the whole church for contemporary prejudice or treating institutional discrimination only as the aggregate of individual closed minds.

The proposed revision below adds one historical example, not a catalogue. It preserves the existing Gospel test of conduct, the Holocaust discussion, the contemporary evidence, and the self-questioning close. The historical and social material should deepen the essay’s inquiry into how we learn to see other people, not become a second essay.

## 2. Voice: what the additional writing suggests

The comparison involved a small selection of reflective and practice-oriented prose. Some available documents have collaborative or uncertain drafting histories; an earlier version of this same essay is not independent evidence of the author’s unaided voice. The strongest guide remains Aaron’s sentence-level revisions and explicit preferences. Private writing was used only to notice general features of rhythm and expression. No private passages, personal incidents, third-party names, or document links are reproduced here.

Five features are useful for this pass:

1. **An observation usually comes before its conceptual name.** A person speaks, protects, overlooks, listens, or changes; the paragraph then asks what that action reveals. The draft is most distant from this voice when an abstract noun is asked to perform the whole movement.
2. **Questions arise from something that has become visible.** They invite another look at an event or relationship. They are less often inventories of theoretical possibilities or requests for a final doctrinal verdict.
3. **Uncertainty is local and candid.** It belongs to what cannot be known about an intention, a historical moment, or a response. It need not weaken a direct statement that discrimination causes harm or that a person deserves care.
4. **Accountability and compassion are held through actions.** Protection, acknowledgment, repair, and changed conduct carry more weight than a succession of formulations about the compatibility of abstract principles.
5. **The rhythm is connected rather than epigrammatic.** A sentence can be simple without being isolated. An image or question often needs the following sentence to become clear. Do not replace that unfolding with a sequence of impressive-sounding conclusions.

This does not call for importing personal narrative into the essay, increasing devotional language everywhere, or imitating Thích Nhất Hạnh. It calls for slightly more direct verbs, fewer impersonal constructions, and questions whose purpose is evident from the preceding material.

A practical test for a proposed edit is: **Does this let us see more clearly what people are doing, or does it merely give the paragraph a smoother abstract summary?** The first is usually worth making; the second may not be.

## 3. Reworking the institutional and discrimination subsection

### R01 — Broaden the heading without losing the inward challenge

**Current:** “The warning turns toward its interpreters”  
**Preferred:** **“What our traditions teach us to see”**

The current heading can sound as though the essay is transferring Jesus’s judgment onto later interpreters. The proposed heading preserves the inward turn while making room for formation, institutional practice, inherited fear, and correction. “Our” includes the comparative inquiry and the person undertaking it; it does not require every reader to identify as Christian.

An alternative, if a more explicit historical emphasis is wanted, is “Receiving a teaching, examining an inheritance.” The first is more natural in the voice established here.

### R02 — Make the first paragraph an invitation rather than another diagnosis

Replace only the first two sentences of the subsection with:

> What do we learn to see through the teachings we inherit? We can defend a teaching about goodness while failing to hear people harmed by the way we apply it.

Continue with the existing sentences about forgiveness being used to silence an injured person, protective boundaries, and reluctance to trust a teacher’s claimed healing. Those safeguards belong in the main text, not solely in a note.

Keep the Matthew 7 and 25 paragraphs and their quotation. They establish that examining the consequences of religious profession is an inward Christian inquiry, not a standard imposed only from another tradition. Do not turn Matthew 25 into a simple affirmation of kindness while omitting its judgment, and do not settle the debated referent of “the least” by editorial simplification.

### R03 — Add one precise ecclesial example, not a general accusation

The Fourth Lateran Council supplies a compact example that names both Jews and Muslims. It is a better fit here than a passing reference to “the Crusades,” which would require considerably more historical work to distinguish places, actors, purposes, and testimony.

Use **the council’s prescription** as evidence of institutional discrimination. Do not infer uniform implementation from the existence of a rule. Name the medieval Latin church rather than allowing “the church” to stand for every Christian community across time. The normative document and its English translation are identified in the proposed note below.

The question then becomes more concrete: what happens when religious distinctions are given institutional authority and made part of public life? That is different from explaining an individual’s inward rigidity, although the two can be explored together.

### R04 — Proposed replacement for the historical and contemporary sequence

**Scope:** replace the passage beginning “Christian anti-Judaism makes the stakes historical as well as personal” through the end of section 4. Retain the preceding Gospel discussion. Bracketed note labels below identify essay notes to retain or add; they are editorial instructions, not prose for publication.

> The question extends beyond an individual interpreter. Teachings are carried through preaching, communal habits, and rules that shape how people live together. In 1215, the Fourth Lateran Council of the Latin church prescribed distinctive dress for Jews and Muslims, whom its text calls “Saracens.” They were to be marked apart in public:
>
> “shall be marked off in the eyes of the public from other peoples through the character of their dress”
>
> — Fourth Lateran Council, canon 68, translated by H. J. Schroeder. [n73]
>
> Here religious difference became a basis for an imposed public distinction. What might people learn to see in one another when separation is taught as part of faithfulness?
>
> Christian anti-Judaism makes the stakes of inherited teaching painfully visible. In its account of the German churches under Nazism, the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum records that “traditional interpretations of religious scriptures seemed to support these prejudices.” Christian teaching, Nazi racial ideology, state power, and differing forms of complicity and resistance met in this history. Religious teaching was not its single cause, but its part cannot be omitted. Nor can the suffering of Jewish people be reduced to a lesson about someone else’s spiritual growth. [n39]
>
> Christian teaching also contains resources for recognizing and correcting harm. In 1965, the Second Vatican Council’s declaration *Nostra aetate* said that Jews “should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God.” Its preceding section states, “The Church regards with esteem also the Moslems,” and calls Christians and Muslims to work together for understanding, justice, peace, and freedom. The declaration also rejects discrimination on grounds of religion. [n40] These words do not undo earlier injury. They do show that receiving a tradition can involve reconsidering what has been taught in its name. What is needed for such a change to become part of how people live together?
>
> That question remains before Christian communities within societies where anti-Muslim prejudice persists. U.S. polling records increased endorsement of negative stereotypes, while European research documents rising discrimination reported by Muslims in employment and housing. Discrimination and religious bullying also remain widespread in Muslim Americans’ accounts of their lives. These findings concern safety, opportunity, and belonging, not simply disagreement about belief. [n71]
>
> When Muslims are treated as collectively suspect, we cease to ask what this person has said or done; religious identity supplies the answer. A community can challenge that habit or leave it undisturbed. What do its members learn about their Muslim neighbours through its teaching, its public words, and the relationships it encourages? We can question beliefs and oppose violence while protecting the freedom to worship and taking seriously the testimony of people facing discrimination. Muslims deserve equal dignity. Their right to safety and belonging does not depend on agreeing with us.
>
> Looking at this history also asks something of how we receive our own traditions. Study, prayer, contemplation, and listening can help us notice when conviction becomes a defense against learning. Within a community, that work may include changing how we teach about others, questioning practices that exclude them, and responding when harm becomes visible. Honest disagreement must remain possible: another person’s refusal to accept our interpretation does not establish that their heart is closed. The same test returns upon a compassionate reading of Mark. If we dismiss every difficult verse or contrary interpretation as a failure of compassion, we have protected our own view from the encounter we ask of others.

This is a replacement, not an addition after the existing paragraphs. It modestly expands the subsection while removing the separate generic announcement that prejudice exists and the repeated identity/verdict formulation. It also gives the Muslim example a place within the institutional inquiry before turning to societal evidence.

**An important boundary:** this passage does not identify later Christians as having committed the particular unforgivable sin of Mark 3:29. Its purpose is to examine how teaching can participate in failed recognition and how communities might become open to correction. That distinction is part of keeping the inquiry humane and historically responsible.

### R05 — Why the transition should be responsibility, not an unproved genealogy

The new sequence is not intended to prove a line of descent from a medieval decree to every modern prejudice. Its argumentative connection is **the responsibility of religious communities for what they teach, permit, challenge, and repair in the social worlds they inhabit**.

“Co-arising” can be a useful question here, but should not become a historical result established merely by placing examples beside one another. Nor should the essay assert that religious teaching and social discrimination are never causally related. A particular causal history requires particular evidence. The existing Holocaust source supports an account of interaction; the current polling does not measure the effects of church teaching.

The distinction should have one clear home in the notes. The body need not repeat “these are not identical histories” after every example. The proposed transition makes the present ethical task explicit without claiming to have reconstructed every link in its past.

For the same reason, do not replace the present evidence with correlations between respondents’ Christian affiliation and anti-Muslim attitudes and then treat those as evidence about what a church taught. Religious identification, institutional policy, individual prejudice, discriminatory treatment, and hate crime are different objects of study.

### R06 — Keep the example specific enough to remain open to investigation

The council’s rule is a concrete action, not evidence that every participant had one psychological motive. The historical question concerns a religious institution’s reasoning, authority, and prescription, and the further question of how it was received and enforced. “Dogmatism” is the essay’s interpretive concern, not an adequate explanation of every historical circumstance.

Likewise, *Nostra aetate* should not become a reassuring ending in which the institutional problem is declared solved. Correction is significant precisely because it creates work to be done. The question following it is therefore important: how does a declaration enter teaching, habits, relationships, and public conduct?

The new material also prepares section 5. If traditions can transmit both insight and harmful judgments, the account of human transmission is no longer only about the production of the Gospels. It concerns the lives through which teaching continues. This gives the historical subsection a forward connection without moving or enlarging the conclusion.

## 6. Precision checks and limits on this pass

### E01 — Distinguish a church rule, a social practice, and a measured trend

The main new historical addition is a rule prescribed by a particular council. Enforcement would require further evidence. The contemporary sources concern measured attitudes and reported experiences. The proposed prose connects these through an ethical inquiry, not by treating them as observations of the same event or a proven causal series. Keep this distinction when adapting the passage.

### E02 — Avoid using “the church” as an undifferentiated actor

Use “the Latin church” for Lateran IV and “the Second Vatican Council” or “Catholic teaching” for *Nostra aetate*. The discussion of Germany concerns multiple churches and responses. “Christian communities” is suitable for the present invitation, but not as a substitute for the named actor in a historical claim.

The broadening of the heading must not dissolve institutional responsibility into a vague statement that everyone sometimes misunderstands. Named actions and institutions make accountability possible; the invitation can then include us without assigning identical responsibility to everyone.

### E03 — Preserve the hardest contrary evidence

Do not remove Matthew’s two-age statement, Mark 4’s disturbing use of Isaiah, Calvin’s account of hardened refusal, or the distinction between transformation and pardon. The proposed heading does not make refusal to accept this essay into an example of dogmatism. The last sentence of section 4 is particularly important to retain.

### E04 — No correction is warranted merely because a source URL looks different

The v5 bibliography’s Dukhrana link for Mark 3:28 contains `source=ubs`, unlike some adjacent links. The displayed page was checked: its footer still identifies the Peshitta verses as taken from the British and Foreign Bible Society New Testament. The URL parameter alone is not evidence of a misidentified edition. Do not introduce a source-error claim on that basis. This check concerns page identity, not a new certification of every romanized form or working translation.

### E05 — Source status and privacy

Retain the author-verified status and adaptation label for Mascaró’s verse. Do not restore the earlier pending-verification language. Do not reintroduce an independent Syriac review as a publication requirement after the author’s decision. The full published *Diamond Sutra* commentary remains a separate pending task from the checked sutra text.

The additional writings used to inform the voice comparison are not new factual sources for the essay. No autobiographical claims or private examples should be brought into the proposed section. Its source apparatus remains historical and public.

First decide on R01–R04 as one unit: the heading, the inward question, the ecclesial example, and the passage from historical correction to present responsibility. Read the resulting subsection together with the end of Paul and the start of section 5. That is the only revision here that changes the essay’s substantive development.

Next make the accompanying note and bibliography changes. Preserve existing labels and add n73 rather than renumbering the inherited apparatus. Update the source map and active outline only when the prose is actually adopted. Keep this review as a record of proposals, not a log silently rewritten to match the eventual decisions.

Then consider S01–S14 individually. They are not a mandate to regularize every sentence into one style. S05, S09, S10, and S11 offer the clearest gains; the other substitutions are choices of cadence. Leave the approved opening, Sabbath paragraph, complete language displays, time ending, safety passages, and final question intact.

The essay’s center remains the human encounter and the possibility of a warning that awakens. The historical material matters because it asks whether the communities carrying that teaching can also be taught by the lives they have misunderstood. It should leave us with work to do, not with a new group of people to declare beyond understanding.
