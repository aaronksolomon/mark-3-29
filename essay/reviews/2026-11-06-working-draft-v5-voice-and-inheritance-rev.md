## 1. Overall assessment and scope

Keep v5 and its six-section structure. The essay now moves coherently from a particular saying, through its human and linguistic setting, into the ways a teaching is received and lived. The conclusion follows from that movement rather than merely summarizing the research. Another general compression pass would risk removing the sentences that allow the thought to develop.

The principal remaining revision is within section 4. Its present movement from Christian anti-Judaism to contemporary anti-Muslim prejudice leaves two gaps: the historical connection between Christian institutions and violence against Muslims is insufficiently developed, and Jewish suffering is chiefly located in the past. Present-day anti-Jewish discrimination and violence must be named in their own right, not left implicit in the Holocaust discussion.

The repair is not to make two interchangeable histories or to balance one harm against another. It is to attend to particular acts, institutions, and people, then ask how inherited convictions can make exclusion or violence seem faithful, protective, or necessary. The Christian setting remains central to this essay; a concise Buddhist example prevents the comparative perspective from becoming a place outside the difficulty it examines.

The proposed sequence is **crusading violence → Christian anti-Judaism and the Holocaust → institutional correction → a Buddhist acknowledgment of complicity → contemporary antisemitism and anti-Muslim discrimination → responsibility without collective blame**. It replaces the current historical/contemporary block rather than adding another block after it. Keep the Gospel test of conduct before it and the self-questioning return to Mark at its end.

## 2. Voice: what the additional writing suggests

The comparison involved a small selection of reflective and practice-oriented prose. Some available documents have collaborative or uncertain drafting histories; an earlier version of this same essay is not independent evidence of the author’s unaided voice. The strongest guide remains Aaron’s sentence-level revisions and explicit preferences. Private writing was used only to notice general features of rhythm and expression. No private passages, personal incidents, third-party names, or document links are reproduced here.

Five features are useful for this pass:

1. **An observation usually comes before its conceptual name.** A person speaks, protects, overlooks, listens, or changes; the paragraph then asks what that action reveals. The draft is most distant from this voice when an abstract noun is asked to perform the whole movement.
2. **Questions arise from something that has become visible.** They invite another look at an event or relationship. They are less often inventories of theoretical possibilities or requests for a final doctrinal verdict.
3. **Uncertainty is local and candid.** It belongs to what cannot be known about an intention, a historical moment, or a response. It need not weaken a direct statement that discrimination causes harm or that a person deserves care.
4. **Accountability and compassion are held through actions.** Protection, acknowledgment, repair, and changed conduct carry more weight than a succession of formulations about the compatibility of abstract principles.
5. **The rhythm is connected rather than epigrammatic.** A sentence can be simple without being isolated. An image or question often needs the following sentence to become clear. Do not replace that unfolding with a sequence of impressive-sounding conclusions.

This does not call for importing personal narrative into the essay, increasing devotional language everywhere, or imitating Thích Nhất Hạnh. It calls for slightly more direct verbs, fewer impersonal constructions, and questions whose purpose is evident from the preceding material.

A practical test for a proposed edit is: **Does this let us see more clearly what people are doing, or does it merely give the paragraph a smoother abstract summary?** The first is usually worth making; the second may not be.

## 3. Reworking the institutional and discrimination subsection

### R01 — Broaden the heading without losing the inward challenge

**Current:** “The warning turns toward its interpreters”  
**Preferred:** **“What our traditions teach us to see”**

The current heading can sound as though the essay is transferring Jesus’s judgment onto later interpreters. The proposed heading preserves the inward turn while making room for formation, institutional practice, inherited fear, and correction. “Our” includes the comparative inquiry and the person undertaking it; it does not require every reader to identify as Christian.

An alternative, if a more explicit historical emphasis is wanted, is “Receiving a teaching, examining an inheritance.” The first is more natural in the voice established here.

### R02 — Make the first paragraph an invitation rather than another diagnosis

Replace only the first two sentences of the subsection with:

> What do we learn to see through the teachings we inherit? We can defend a teaching about goodness while failing to hear people harmed by the way we apply it.

Continue with the existing sentences about forgiveness being used to silence an injured person, protective boundaries, and reluctance to trust a teacher’s claimed healing. Those safeguards belong in the main text, not solely in a note.

Keep the Matthew 7 and 25 paragraphs and their quotation. They establish that examining the consequences of religious profession is an inward Christian inquiry, not a standard imposed only from another tradition. Do not turn Matthew 25 into a simple affirmation of kindness while omitting its judgment, and do not settle the debated referent of “the least” by editorial simplification.

### R03 — Name violence directly, with dates and responsible actors

The earlier dress-rule example establishes institutional exclusion, but does not adequately convey the gravity of this history. Lead instead with the First Crusade and the massacres associated with it. Distinguish Urban II’s authorization of the crusading undertaking from the decisions of particular bands, townspeople, and military forces. Do not imply that a surviving papal instruction ordered the Rhineland killings.

Urban’s December 1095 letter provides a particularly relevant connection: it associates the undertaking with remission of sins. The point is not an ironic flourish at Christianity’s expense. It is that the language of forgiveness itself can participate in a religious understanding of violent action.

Name the anti-Jewish massacres in the Rhineland in 1096 and the slaughter accompanying the capture of Jerusalem in 1099. The latter affected Muslim and Jewish inhabitants, with survivors, captives, and efforts at ransom as well as deaths. Do not use an inflated casualty total or say that every inhabitant was killed. A Jewish relief letter is important here: the account should not depend entirely on the conquerors’ descriptions.

The Fourth Lateran Council’s dress prescription can remain in the source note as a further institutional example. It should not take the place of the violence, and its date—1215—must not be made the starting point of events that occurred more than a century earlier. No additional crusading catalogue is needed.

### R04 — Revised replacement for the historical and contemporary sequence

**Scope:** replace the passage beginning “Christian anti-Judaism makes the stakes historical as well as personal” through the end of section 4. Retain the preceding Gospel discussion. Bracketed note labels below identify existing or proposed essay notes; they are editorial instructions, not publication prose. This version supersedes the November 6 R04.

> The consequences of a religious judgment can extend far beyond a disagreement. Pope Urban II called for the First Crusade in 1095, connecting the undertaking with “the remission of all their sins.” In 1096, crusading bands and townspeople massacred Jews in the Rhineland. The capture of Jerusalem in 1099 brought slaughter and captivity to its Muslim and Jewish inhabitants. Christian responses also included attempts to protect Jews; the history is not one of uniform belief or conduct. Yet faith could help people understand violence as a holy task. What happens when harming people comes to be understood as serving God? [n73]
>
> Christian anti-Judaism makes the stakes of inherited teaching painfully visible in another setting. In its account of the German churches under Nazism, the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum records that “traditional interpretations of religious scriptures seemed to support these prejudices.” Christian teaching, Nazi racial ideology, state power, and differing forms of complicity and resistance met in this history. Religious teaching was not its single cause, but its part cannot be omitted. Nor can the suffering of Jewish people be reduced to a lesson about someone else’s spiritual growth. [n39]
>
> Traditions can also provide resources for acknowledging and changing what has been taught in their name. In 1965, the Second Vatican Council’s declaration *Nostra aetate* said that Jews “should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God.” It also states, “The Church regards with esteem also the Moslems,” and calls for cooperation and an end to religious discrimination. [n40] Such words do not undo earlier injury. What is needed for this recognition to enter a community’s teaching, relationships, and public life?
>
> Buddhist history offers no exemption from this inquiry. In 1992, Japan’s Sōtō Zen authorities acknowledged their institution’s support for imperial aggression and colonial domination in Asia, including its part in justifying the subordination of other peoples. [n75] An inheritance of compassion had not prevented participation in harm. Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Buddhist, and secular communities all have reason to ask: when does loyalty to what we value become permission not to hear those we injure?
>
> Antisemitism belongs to the present as well as this history. European research records Jewish people’s experiences of discrimination, harassment, and physical attack, and the concealment of Jewish identity out of fear. U.S. hate-crime records also document crimes motivated by anti-Jewish bias. [n74] Anti-Muslim hostility has its own forms and histories: research documents discrimination in work and housing, religious bullying, and threats to safety, alongside increased endorsement of negative stereotypes in U.S. polling. [n71] These are not interchangeable experiences. Each asks to be heard without being made to compete with the other for recognition.
>
> Grief over violence in Palestine, Israel, Iran, and elsewhere in the region makes this care more demanding. We need to name who is harmed and who is responsible without making a whole people answerable for a government or an armed group. Criticism of policies and actions is not, by itself, hatred of a people; concern about prejudice must not be used to silence testimony about injustice. Care across communities does not require us to equate their circumstances, power, or responsibility. It asks us not to let attention to one suffering close our eyes to another.
>
> A community can challenge collective suspicion or leave it undisturbed. What do we learn about our Jewish and Muslim neighbors through what is taught, what goes unchallenged, and whom we come to know? Listening matters, and so do protecting places of worship, challenging discrimination, and responding to people whose safety is threatened. Their dignity does not depend on agreement with our beliefs or politics.
>
> Looking at this history asks us to examine our own attachment, not only someone else’s. Study, prayer, contemplation, and listening can help us notice when conviction becomes a defense against learning. The work may require changing what we teach and do when harm becomes visible. Honest disagreement must remain possible: another person’s refusal to accept our interpretation does not establish that their heart is closed. The same test returns upon a compassionate reading of Mark. If we dismiss every difficult verse or contrary interpretation as a failure of compassion, we have protected our own view from the encounter we ask of others.

This is a replacement, not an additional historical excursus. It expands the previous proposal where the author requested substance, while keeping figures, methodological qualifications, and most historical detail in the apparatus. Do not add a second paragraph explaining the same institutional pattern after it.

The regional paragraph is an ethical invitation, not a report of the current military situation. It neither equates the parties nor asks the reader to suspend judgments supported by evidence. Its work is to distinguish responsibility for actions from inherited guilt and collective suspicion. The phrase “a whole people” is deliberately wider than a religious denomination: Jewish identity is not only religious, and Palestinian, Israeli, Arab, Iranian, Muslim, and Jewish identities are not interchangeable.

**An important boundary:** the passage does not identify later Christians, Buddhists, or anyone else as having committed the particular unforgivable sin of Mark 3:29. Nor does it turn communities that have suffered violence into people incapable of causing harm. It asks how teaching participates in what people learn to recognize, overlook, justify, and change.

### R05 — Balance through attention, not equivalence

Present-day antisemitism must have its own sentence, evidence, and consequences; Jewish people should not appear only as medieval or Holocaust victims. Anti-Muslim discrimination must also retain its own evidence rather than becoming a secondary qualification. Neither is made more real by diminishing the other.

Balanced treatment is not a requirement to claim equal prevalence, the same perpetrators, parallel causes, or identical histories. Do not combine unlike survey percentages into a comparison of suffering. Do not treat a community’s fear as proof of every political claim made in its name. Equally, disagreement with a political claim does not invalidate that community’s testimony of threats or exclusion.

The historical connection is responsibility, not a demonstrated single chain of causation. Religious teaching, social fear, economic interest, nationalism, and political power can interact. Particular causal accounts need particular evidence. The Holocaust source describes such an interaction; contemporary surveys do not identify every perpetrator’s religion or establish what a church taught them.

“Co-arising” can name a question about those relationships, but should not be presented as a historical finding merely because examples appear beside each other. The sequence does not claim that a medieval prescription caused present-day incidents, or that religion is never causally involved. It asks what communities sustain, resist, and repair within the worlds they inhabit.

### R06 — Keep the shared vulnerability concrete and the framing accessible

The Japanese example does more than distribute criticism among religions. It directs the inquiry toward a tradition from which this essay draws resources. The acknowledged harm included imperial hierarchy and colonial assimilation, not simply hostility to someone else’s religious doctrine. This helps show how attachment to religious, national, or cultural identity can be enlisted together.

Do not append a catalogue of Jewish, Muslim, Buddhist, Christian, and secular atrocities merely to establish equal blame. The point is not that all have done the same thing. It is that no inherited identity places its members outside the need for examination. Further examples require the same specificity about actors, conduct, victims, and evidence; a state’s action must not automatically be attributed to every member of its majority religion.

The First–Third Mindfulness Trainings help explain Aaron’s underlying concern, but this passage need not announce a Buddhist framework. Its practical movement can be expressed in shared language: do not make an inherited view immune to correction; listen to what it prevents us from hearing; and refuse to make belonging or power a warrant to impose belief or disregard another life. These are invitations to examine conduct, not a claim that traditions are doctrinally identical.

Keep institutional responsibility visible. Historical violence cannot be explained only by an inward state called dogmatism. Institutions can authorize, fund, normalize, restrain, and sometimes repent of harm. Political arrangements and coercive power matter alongside perception. Conversely, a declaration of repentance or respect does not establish that habits and institutions have changed. The question following *Nostra aetate* is therefore necessary, not an ornamental open ending.

This development prepares section 5: human transmission includes the institutions and habits through which teaching continues, not only the original composition of the Gospels. No further reorganization of the conclusion is needed.

### New note n73 — Crusading authorization, massacres, and surviving testimony

Suggested publication note:

> Urban II, letter to the faithful in Flanders, December 1095, in August C. Krey, *The First Crusade: The Accounts of Eyewitnesses and Participants* (1921), pp. 42–43; [Internet Medieval Sourcebook, item 6](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/source/urban2-5vers.asp). The letter connects the undertaking with remission of sins and appoints Adhemar of Le Puy to lead it in the pope’s stead. For the Rhineland attacks, see [Albert of Aix and Ekkehard of Aura](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/source/1096jews.asp), translated in Krey, pp. 53–56; the accounts distinguish religious claims, plunder, and attempted protection. For Jerusalem, see [Raymond of Aguilers and the other collected accounts](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/source/cde-jlem.asp), especially the sections on the Frankish victory. A Jewish relief letter, [CUL T-S 20.113](https://www.posenlibrary.com/entry/letter-capture-jerusalem), translated by Amir Ashur and Benjamin M. Outhwaite, records killings, captivity, ransom, and survivors in the aftermath. These sources establish violence and its religious setting, not a single motive shared by everyone or an order from Urban to massacre Jews. No disputed casualty total is adopted.

**Why these sources and how they were used:**

- **Urban’s letter:** use the documentary letter rather than present one chronicler’s version of the Clermont speech as a verbatim transcript. The short phrase about remission is quoted in Krey’s English translation. The letter supports papal initiative and authorization; it does not direct the attacks in the Rhineland.
- **Albert and Ekkehard:** these are Christian narrative witnesses, not uniformly eyewitness records. Albert describes Jews seeking protection in the bishop’s residence at Mainz and attributes part of the violence to plunder; Ekkehard’s account contains hostile religious language. Their testimony supports discrimination and violence without requiring that all Christians or all clergy acted alike. Do not reproduce lurid details or their casualty estimates as settled statistics.
- **Raymond and the Jerusalem collection:** their religious interpretation of conquest makes the connection to sacred justification explicit. Their descriptions must not be treated as neutral measurements. The proposal does not adopt the extravagant imagery of blood or infer that there were no survivors.
- **The Jewish relief letter:** Posen dates the entry to 1100 and identifies its authors as Yeshaya ben Matsliaḥ, David ben Solomon, and Ḥanina Ibn ‘Ubayd. Its account of captivity and ransom preserves the work of rescue as well as the violence. It is testimony from those responding to catastrophe, not another conqueror’s justification. The published English translation was read; the underlying Judeo-Arabic manuscript was not newly collated.

The earlier [Lateran IV, canon 68](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/lat4-c68.asp) proposal remains a useful supplementary source for imposed public distinction in 1215. Keep it in the research record rather than in the preferred main-text paragraph. Its prescription does not establish uniform enforcement, and it postdates the First Crusade. The difference between these sources matters: an institutional rule, a papal appeal, and accounts of killings are not interchangeable forms of evidence.

**Bibliography additions if adopted:** Krey’s 1921 collection with the specific letter and accounts identified; the Posen letter entry with its manuscript shelfmark and translators. The prior dress-rule quotation need not be retained in the essay bibliography if it is no longer used there.

### Regional context, criticism, and collective blame

The paragraph concerning Palestine, Israel, Iran, and the region is the essay’s ethical reflection, not a new empirical claim about current operations, casualty figures, or a settled allocation of blame. It does not require a news digest. Preserve these distinctions when adapting it:

- Antisemitism concerns hostility toward Jews as Jews, including secular Jews; criticism of Israeli government actions is not automatically antisemitic, although criticism can carry antisemitic stereotypes or collective accusations.
- Muslims are not collectively answerable for Iran’s government, Hamas, or any other state or armed organization. Criticism of those actors does not itself establish anti-Muslim prejudice.
- Palestinian, Israeli, Iranian, Arab, Jewish, Muslim, and Christian identities overlap but are not equivalents. Palestinian suffering must not disappear into an exclusively Muslim category, nor Israeli lives into an exclusively Jewish one.
- Naming a specific perpetrator, institution, policy, or documented asymmetry is compatible with opposing collective blame. Equal human dignity is not an assertion of equal political power or equal responsibility.
- Grief is not itself dogmatism. The inquiry concerns what we do when fear or attachment makes another person’s testimony impossible to receive, not a diagnosis of injured people for being distressed or angry.

## 6. Precision checks and limits on this pass

### E01 — Distinguish authorization, perpetration, testimony, and a measured trend

The historical additions now concern a papal appeal, actions by crusaders and townspeople, and accounts of killings and rescue. They do not establish that one authority commanded every act. The Japanese material is a named institution’s retrospective acknowledgment; the contemporary sources concern recorded crimes and reported experiences. Keep these kinds of evidence distinct, including their dates and limits.

The account of violence is stronger because it names what happened, not because it supplies larger numbers or more emphatic adjectives. Do not replace it with a vague “religious conflict,” but do not turn complex events into evidence of a single permanent character possessed by a religion.

### E02 — Avoid undifferentiated actors and equal-blame language

Use Urban II and the crusading forces for the specific medieval actions, the German churches with their differing responses in the Nazi setting, the Second Vatican Council for *Nostra aetate*, and Sōtō Zen authorities for the 1992 acknowledgment. A papal initiative is not the same thing as every Christian’s assent. A Sōtō acknowledgment is not a finding about every Buddhist. A state or armed group is not a people.

The broadening of the heading must not dissolve institutional responsibility into the claim that everyone sometimes misunderstands. Named actions make accountability possible. Nor should responsibility be equalized by listing religions: each act must be understood on its own evidence, with attention to power and consequences.

Do not remove Matthew’s two-age statement, Mark 4’s disturbing use of Isaiah, Calvin’s account of hardened refusal, or the distinction between transformation and pardon.
