## 2. The principal revision: opening the language study

### F01 — Replace the four introductory paragraphs with three focused paragraphs

**Priority:** high.  
**Location:** section 3, from “Jesus is responding to the judgment…” through the paragraph ending with note n17, immediately before “The passage in Greek.”  
**Scope:** introductory prose only. Do not alter the original-language text, romanizations, literal renderings, or following interpretation.

Suggested replacement:

> Mark's Gospel, generally dated to around 70 CE, preserves the exchange in Greek. Aramaic, Hebrew, and Greek were used in Jesus's world. Mark retains Aramaic expressions elsewhere, including “Talitha koum” (5:41), but does not identify the language spoken in this encounter. The Greek text below follows the *SBL Greek New Testament*.
>
> Syriac, a literary form of Aramaic, offers a later witness to how the saying was received. The Peshitta New Testament became standard around 400 CE, following revision of earlier Syriac translations in relation to Greek texts. It brings us into an Aramaic-speaking Christian tradition several centuries after Jesus.
>
> The difficulty of carrying meaning between languages was recognized within Jewish tradition. In the prologue to Ben Sira, also called Sirach, the translator observes that Hebrew expression “does not have exactly the same effect when translated into another language” (prologue, lines 21–22, NRSVUE). The observation matters for a saying whose force depends on how it reaches its hearers.

**Retain the existing source calls:** n16 after the first paragraph, n22 after the second, and n17 after the third. The Sirach quotation is unchanged.

This preserves the relevant chronology and the distinction between the Greek Gospel, an earlier spoken exchange, and later Syriac reception. It removes the repeated declaration that Jesus is answering an accusation, which section 2 has just established. It also removes “We give … versions of the text below,” allowing the edition labels and the parallel displays to do that work.

The point is not to make Syriac less important. Naming it first as a literary form of Aramaic clarifies its relevance before introducing the Peshitta. The latter then appears as the source actually used, rather than another technical term the reader must hold without a purpose.

### F03 — Give the post-Syriac age paragraph one forward movement

**Priority:** optional.  
**Anchor:** “The Greek *aiōn* has its own history…”

### F05 — Introduce Paul's relevant background without assigning him an unestablished role

**Priority:** high; factual clarification.  
**Anchor:** the annotation following “The apostle Paul” in “Paul and changed seeing.”

Paul calls himself a Pharisee in Philippians 3:5. Acts 22:3 describes his education under Gamaliel. These support a connection with Jewish religious learning and commitment, but they do not by themselves establish that he held the role of *grammateus*. Do not use “Pharisee” and “scribe” as interchangeable labels or call him “another scribe” on the evidence presently cited.

Recommended first sentence:

> The apostle Paul, who identifies himself as a Pharisee, gives the possibility of change particular weight.

Then continue with the existing sentence beginning “In his letter to the Galatians…” and the existing quotation. Add Philippians 3:5–6 to n36. This places his background within his own testimony without adding a biographical digression.

The moral significance of the example remains his persecution and changed direction, not his Pharisaic identity. The comparison should not imply that Pharisaic learning itself produces hostility, or that the scribes in Mark were all Pharisees.

**Suggested addition to n36:**

> Paul identifies himself as a Pharisee in Philippians 3:5–6. Acts 22:3 separately portrays his training under Gamaliel. Neither identification is used here to establish that he held the occupational or social role of a scribe.

**Dating:** a precise year adds little to the body. If a temporal orientation is wanted, give it in the note as approximate, not a guess presented as fact:

> Chronologies commonly place Paul's encounter in the 30s CE, although the exact year is disputed.

For a documented version of that note, Nicholas J. Frederick's proposed chronology places it in 34, while Mato Zovkić's reconstruction adopts 36. Those are examples of scholarly reconstruction, not two independently dated records. The broad decade is sufficient; no year needs to be placed beside the change in the main prose.

## 5. Overall flow after these edits

The major sections now have distinct work to do. Section 2 gives the warning its human setting. Section 3 preserves the wording and the difficulty of hearing it. Section 4 brings the inquiry into attachment, change, and the consequences of interpretation. Section 5 joins humanity, continuing responsibility, and the practice of speech. Section 6 returns these concerns to the person reading.

The cross-tradition sequence is no longer merely a collection of parallels. The Talmud contributes the discipline of hearing another position; al-Ghazālī contributes the difference between knowing and living; Rūmī makes the correcting teacher answerable to what correction does; Mark's disciples bring that question back within the Gospel. Keep those different contributions. Reducing them to one generalized claim about openness would make the essay shorter and less intelligible.

The transition from person/description to time, and from time to speech, also holds. Speech becomes part of what continues in a life. The conclusion's current bridge—“The practice of forgiveness asks for this same attention to a particular life”—successfully connects interreligious hearing to forgiveness and accountability. I would leave the conclusion unchanged in this pass.

The useful restraint now is to stop treating every occurrence of a recurring word as a defect. “Hearing,” “understanding,” “care,” and “encounter” are the threads of the argument. Remove an occurrence when the adjacent sentence performs the same work, not merely because the vocabulary appears elsewhere. Avoid substituting an abstract synonym solely for variety.
