# All Will Be Forgiven?
## Compassion, a Warning, and Our Shared Humanity

> *“Truly I tell you, people can be forgiven all their sins and every slander they utter, but whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit will never be forgiven; they are guilty of an eternal sin.”*
>
> — Mark 3:28–29, New International Version (2011). [^n01]

## 1. Hearing the warning

In the span of a single breath, this remarkable saying opens forgiveness across the range of human sin and slander, then names a stark exception. Read as two detached statements, the words seem to pull us in opposite directions. Heard within an encounter, they may do something more: the breadth of the first makes the expression that follows all the more startling. Against the possibility of forgiveness for so much, Jesus places the gravity of what his hearers are saying. That contrast may be part of the force with which he is trying to reach them. [^n02]

The words can be heard as a sharp warning, an effort to awaken those who have called the work of healing evil, or to disrupt the harmful judgment itself. The warning may also reach others witnessing the exchange. Jesus uses the language of judgment to challenge a judgment that turns the source of healing into an enemy. The severity draws attention to what that reversal means. A warning may carry compassion without sounding reassuring, and its care may extend beyond the people it directly addresses to those who could be affected by their words.

What the words declare, what speaking them seeks to accomplish, and what eventually happens to those addressed are related but distinct. A warning can seek to prevent a loss that the speaker regards as final; it can also confront a refusal that might yet change. Mark gives us the declaration and the encounter, not the hearers’ final history.

We bring our own histories to these words. Forgiveness may suggest pardon, release, or reconciliation; judgment may suggest discernment or condemnation. Jesus and those who heard him spoke from their own inherited language and religious life. As their exchange is remembered, written, translated, and taught, it enters other lives and other circumstances. What changes when words spoken to particular people become a judgment we pronounce about someone else—or an entire community?

Teachings from other traditions can make familiar assumptions visible. The Vietnamese Buddhist monk, teacher, and peace activist Thích Nhất Hạnh (1926–2022) joined contemplative practice with the work of responding to suffering. He founded the Order of Interbeing and helped establish the Plum Village community in France. His tradition brings a particular question to the encounter: what happens when we become attached not only to an opinion but to our sense of possessing the truth? The Order’s commitment to nonattachment extends to its own teachings. Such a comparison can deepen understanding while still honoring differences between traditions. [^n03]

Jewish teachings also ask how firmly we should hold our own understanding. The Mishnah preserves rejected opinions of Hillel and Shammai, Jewish teachers associated with the period around the beginning of the Common Era, to teach that a person should not insist on their own position (Eduyot 1:4). Much later, the British rabbi and public thinker Jonathan Sacks (1948–2020) describes the ideal exemplified by Hillel’s school: “They sought truth, not victory.” The willingness to be corrected belongs here within a committed religious life. Buddhist nonattachment and rabbinic argument arise within different accounts of truth and practice, yet each can question the moment when fidelity to a teaching becomes the need to defend ourselves. Jewish interpretation enters both as attention to Jesus’s own inheritance and as a continuing voice with questions of its own. [^n60]

The Gospels also challenge the assumption that faith and mercy will appear only within our own community. Jesus responds to a centurion’s trust with astonishment: “I have not found anyone in Israel with such great faith” (Matthew 8:10, NIV). In Luke’s parable of the Good Samaritan, the compassionate action comes from a Samaritan, a member of a related but contested Israelite tradition: he sees the wounded man and “took pity on him” (Luke 10:33, NIV). Jesus directs attention toward faith and compassionate action where his hearers might not expect to find them. [^n04]

## 2. A teacher among people

### The story before the saying

In Mark’s opening chapter, the Spirit descends upon Jesus, who later proclaims that “The kingdom of God has come near” (1:15, NIV). Teaching, healing, and release from impure spirits give that proclamation a visible form. People gather around him; he also withdraws to pray. From the beginning, the Gospel presents divine activity through a life moving among other lives, not through a list of propositions delivered outside any human setting. [^n05]

Questions develop around what this activity means. When Jesus forgives a paralyzed man, some *grammateis* (γραμματεῖς)—scribes, learned interpreters of Torah—think, “He’s blaspheming!” (2:7, NIV). His meals with people identified as sinners, and his disciples’ conduct on the Sabbath, bring further challenges. Jesus responds through questions and images, including the physician who attends the sick and new wine that cannot be contained in old wineskins (2:17–22). Jesus and those questioning him share a Jewish scriptural world in which mercy, Torah, interpretation, and faithfulness already matter deeply. Their disagreements concern how that inheritance is being lived. [^n06]

Sabbath observance belongs to that inheritance. In the Torah it is holy rest, extending to servants, resident foreigners, and animals; Deuteronomy also recalls liberation from slavery. Torah gives form to religious and communal life, and living by its commandments requires interpretation. When chapter 3 opens with a man whose hand needs healing, some of those present watch to see whether Jesus will heal on the Sabbath so that they can accuse him. Here the question concerns what faithfulness to Sabbath rest asks when a person before them needs healing. Jesus asks about doing good and saving life; the response is silence. Mark describes him as angry and “deeply distressed at their stubborn hearts” (3:5, NIV), then shows him restoring the man’s hand. [^n07]

Later, so many people gather that Jesus and those with him cannot eat, and his family comes to take charge of him (3:20–21). Even those closest to him struggle to understand what is happening. The difficulty of receiving him reaches into family and community as well as religious debate. The saying about forgiveness emerges from this crowded, unsettled human world.

### Mercy and warning within the Jewish inheritance

The scriptures shared by Jesus and those questioning him hold mercy and the consequences of wrongdoing in close relation. In Exodus’s proclamation of the divine name, God is “compassionate and gracious,” “abounding in love and faithfulness,” and “forgiving wickedness, rebellion and sin” (34:6–7, NIV). The same passage refuses to treat wrongdoing as inconsequential and speaks in difficult language about guilt and consequences across generations. Compassion here is not indifference to harm; mercy and accountability are held together within the same sacred language. [^n09]

Ezekiel gives severe warning an explicitly life-seeking purpose. God declares, “I take no pleasure in the death of the wicked,” then calls, “Turn! Turn from your evil ways!” (33:11, NIV). The following verses connect turning with restitution and changed conduct. The warning names a real danger, and the call to change is equally real. This does not decide the meaning of Mark’s exception. It locates concern, judgment, and the possibility of turning within a Jewish scriptural inheritance that Jesus and his hearers share. [^n10]

### From accusation to warning

The scribes who arrive from Jerusalem give Jesus’s work a particularly damaging interpretation: “By the prince of demons he is driving out demons” (3:22, NIV). They associate his power with Beelzebul, the ruler of demons. They are not simply denying that something has happened. They name its source as evil. [^n11]

Jesus calls them over and asks, “How can Satan drive out Satan?” (3:23, NIV). A divided kingdom or household cannot stand; taking a strong man’s possessions requires first overcoming him. These images challenge the accusation at its center. In Mark’s telling, Jesus is breaking the hold of destructive power, not serving it. He addresses those making the accusation with reasons and images through which they might reconsider. The summons keeps the exchange an act of address, even amid sharp disagreement. Mark does not report how they receive this argument; the next recorded words sharpen the address.

The address then turns to the warning with which we began. Mark connects it directly with their accusation: “He has an impure spirit” (3:30, NIV). The source of the work he presents as holy is being named evil. This is not a general disagreement about religious language; it concerns whether the healing taking place before them is to be welcomed or opposed.

The breadth of the promise gives the warning its force. The movement from familiar images to words of such severity may be heard as an urgent attempt to awaken the hearers or to disrupt the harmful judgment taking shape around Jesus’s work. The rising intensity belongs to one continuing address.

There is a further reversal across these chapters. Earlier, Jesus is accused of blasphemy because he forgives. Now the language of judgment turns back upon the judgment directed against his work. His response can be heard as a clear and powerful call, using words his hearers take seriously to confront what their accusation rejects and to challenge its authority. To oppose the Spirit of holiness is to turn against the very source of healing and forgiveness. So understood, the warning does more than classify a wrong: it confronts the rejection of what could bring release. [^n13]

The scribes are the immediate addressees: Mark says that Jesus calls them over. Yet the exchange is framed by a crowd gathering around him (3:20) and a crowd seated around him afterward (3:32). His words may work within that wider circle as well. An accusation about the source of healing can shape more than the convictions of those who make it; it can influence what others fear, whom they trust, and whether they approach the one offering help. On this reading, interrupting the accusation also serves those whose possibilities it might close. The narrative makes that wider effect plausible without telling us that Jesus addresses every person present or how anyone responds. [^n58]

### Disruption without retaliation

Walter Wink, a twentieth- and early-twenty-first-century American biblical scholar, approaches Jesus’s teaching on nonviolence as an alternative to both submission and violent retaliation. In his reading of Matthew’s turning-the-cheek, cloak, and second-mile sayings, people resist a degrading relationship without accepting violence as their only means of resistance. He writes, “Loving confrontation can free both the oppressed from docility and the oppressor from sin.” Yet he also warns that nonviolent action can be used for revenge and humiliation. Refusing physical violence does not, by itself, establish a loving purpose. [^n59]

Wink’s discussion concerns Jesus’s teaching on retaliation in Matthew 5:38–42, within the Sermon on the Mount, not this passage in Mark. It nevertheless opens a way of attending to the encounter. Jesus refuses the interpretation imposed on his work and brings its consequences into question. He does not need to leave the accusation unanswered in order to refrain from retaliation. A sharp disruption may protect the possibility of healing for others as well as challenge those speaking against it. Care for a person and resistance to what the person is doing can belong to one action.

The severity makes this a demanding possibility, not an easy description. A threat of unforgivable sin can itself be used to silence dissent or secure obedience. Calling it nonviolent would explain nothing if the result were simply to sanctify intimidation. The question is whether the warning exposes a real harm and interrupts its power without making the people responsible disposable. We can hear a possible act of compassion here while continuing to ask what distinguishes truthful, protective confrontation from the religious use of fear.

### A saying that unsettles its hearers

Seen together, the summons, the parables, and the warning suggest a form of teaching that aims not only to rebut an accusation but to interrupt a way of seeing. The words work within relationship: they warn, rebuke, invite, unsettle, and ask the hearer to recognize something the present judgment has obscured. A teacher may speak within familiar categories in order to expose what those categories have prevented someone from noticing. That possibility does not place Jesus outside his own culture. It asks how he works within it, addressing particular people whose response matters.

Koan practice, a form of contemplative inquiry in Zen, offers a related way of encountering challenging speech. A saying can interrupt the understanding with which we approach it, rather than supply an answer that leaves us unchanged. Thích Nhất Hạnh describes this involvement in *Bat Nha: a Koan*, where the question becomes one’s “own problem.” Applied here, the question is no longer only which other person falls under the warning. It includes the judgments through which we ourselves hear it. A conclusion about the scribes in the story may leave those judgments untouched; entering the question can make them visible. Jesus’s response begins with parables, and the severe saying belongs to the same address. [^n14]

This hearing also has a Christian precedent. John Chrysostom, a late-fourth-century preacher in Antioch, in Roman Syria, describes Jesus’s purpose as “to do good to them that were sinning” (*Homily 41 on Matthew*, §1). Commenting on Matthew’s parallel, he hears correction within confrontation. His interpretation retains severe judgment, but concern is not confined to reassuring speech. [^n15]

The human danger coming into view is not simply mistaken perception. It is clinging to a judgment so closely that we can no longer receive what might correct it. If liberation is named evil, liberation itself becomes something to resist. What could then be welcomed as healing? Mark does not explain the inner motives of those speaking, but his account makes this reversal of recognition visible. The possibility that such resistance can change remains a further question; its present seriousness does not by itself answer it.

## 3. What the words keep open—and what they do not

Jesus is responding to the judgment that his liberating work is evil. Careful study of the language keeps that setting in view. The Gospel of Mark is commonly dated around 70 CE and understood to have been composed in Greek, several decades after Jesus’s ministry. That is a date for composition, not for the surviving copies, and a Greek composition is not a transcript of an earlier spoken encounter.

Mark preserves Aramaic words elsewhere, including “Talitha koum” in the raising of Jairus’s daughter (5:41). First-century Palestine was multilingual: Aramaic was widely used, Hebrew remained in use, Greek was also present, and scholars differ over their relative roles. It is therefore plausible that Jesus knew and used more than one language, but the evidence does not identify the language of this exchange or of its earliest oral transmission. Nor has a lost Aramaic Gospel of Mark from which our Greek text was translated been established. The text below follows the *SBL Greek New Testament*, edited by Michael W. Holmes. [^n16]

The human work of translation was recognized within Jewish tradition. In the prologue to Ben Sira, a Jewish wisdom book also called Sirach, the translator observes that Hebrew expression “does not have exactly the same effect when translated into another language” (prologue, lines 21–22, NRSVUE). This is not a reason to abandon the words. It is a reason to study them in relation to the life, language, and practice through which they are transmitted. [^n17]

### The words of the passage

**Mark 3:28**

**Greek:** Ἀμὴν λέγω ὑμῖν ὅτι πάντα ἀφεθήσεται τοῖς υἱοῖς τῶν ἀνθρώπων, τὰ ἁμαρτήματα καὶ αἱ βλασφημίαι ὅσα ἐὰν βλασφημήσωσιν·

**Romanization:** *Amēn legō hymin hoti panta aphethēsetai tois huiois tōn anthrōpōn, ta hamartēmata kai hai blasphēmiai hosa ean blasphēmēsōsin.*

**Literal English:** “Truly I say to you that all [πάντα, *panta*: all] will be forgiven [ἀφεθήσεται, *aphethēsetai*: will be forgiven] to the sons of human beings [τοῖς υἱοῖς τῶν ἀνθρώπων, *tois huiois tōn anthrōpōn*: to human beings / humanity]: the sins and the blasphemies, whatever they may blaspheme.”

The Greek verb is “will be forgiven”; the NIV opening renders the statement “can be forgiven.” The entire movement matters, rather than any isolated phrase. [^n18]

**Mark 3:29**

**Greek:** ὃς δ’ ἂν βλασφημήσῃ εἰς τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον, οὐκ ἔχει ἄφεσιν εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, ἀλλὰ ἔνοχός ἐστιν αἰωνίου ἁμαρτήματος.

**Romanization:** *Hos d’ an blasphēmēsē eis to pneuma to hagion, ouk echei aphesin eis ton aiōna, alla enochos estin aiōniou hamartēmatos.*

**Literal English:** “But whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit does not have forgiveness [ἄφεσιν, *aphesin*: forgiveness / remission] into the age [εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, *eis ton aiōna*: into / unto the age], but is answerable for an eternal sin [αἰωνίου ἁμαρτήματος, *aiōniou hamartēmatos*: of an eternal sin].”

The idiom containing “age” gives an emphatic denial; it does not specify a period after which forgiveness resumes. “Answerable” distinguishes being held accountable for wrongdoing from the inward feeling of guilt. It does not reduce the passage to a financial debt or remove its seriousness. [^n19]

**Mark 3:30**

**Greek:** ὅτι ἔλεγον· Πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον ἔχει.

**Romanization:** *Hoti elegon: pneuma akatharton echei.*

**Literal English:** “Because they were saying, ‘An unclean spirit he has.’”

The NIV uses “impure” for *akatharton*. Mark’s explanation joins the warning to the accusation already being made. [^n20]

The language is forceful. The breadth of forgiveness heightens the exception without becoming empty rhetoric, and the Greek does not remove the severity. The warning concerns what these people are already saying about Jesus’s work. What its denial of forgiveness means for them remains a question inside the translated words.

The final phrase also resists an easy resolution. The critical SBLGNT reads “eternal sin”; the Textus Receptus, the Greek text underlying the King James Version, reads “eternal judgment.” The difference is in the noun, not a softer temporal statement. “Eternal sin” does not itself specify a punishment, but it does speak of being answerable for wrongdoing. Neither a complete theory of punishment nor a purely inward account of blocked healing is contained in those words alone. Their meaning is sought within the story, the wider scriptural witness, and the continuing experience of repentance, mercy, and consequence. [^n21]

The Syriac Peshitta offers another perspective on the words. Syriac is a literary form of Aramaic, and this later Gospel version lets us hear how an Aramaic-speaking Christian community received the saying; it does not recover Jesus’s original utterance. It contrasts *rūḥā d-qudšā*, “Spirit of holiness,” with *rūḥā ṭanpṯā*, “an impure spirit.” The repeated word for spirit makes the opposing descriptions audible. Its ending likewise concerns judgment rather than sin: “judgment that is forever,” in the literal translation supplied for this study. The precise history of that difference remains a question for textual research. [^n22]

Its denial of forgiveness reads *layt leh šubqānā l-ʿālam*, “there is no forgiveness for him forever.” The noun *šubqānā* also names remission, including remission of debt. *L-ʿālam* has the word-for-word shape “for the age,” though its established use here is “forever.” The expression belongs to a word-family that can speak of time, age, and world; it is not literally the plural “for all ages.” These associations do not give the sentence a different ending. They allow us to hear something of the language through which forgiveness and its denial have been understood. [^n62]

The Greek *aiōn* has its own history, with senses including a lifetime, an age, and a world-order. It is the source of English *eon*, through Latin; it is not etymologically the same word as the Aramaic *ʿālam*. Mark’s “into the age” and Matthew’s “this age” and “the age to come” bring a temporal horizon into the saying. The interest of these expressions is not that a root word settles the meaning of eternity. They invite a further question: when we say that something lasts, what are we imagining remains the same? The duration asserted by a saying and the way a life carries its past belong to the inquiry together. [^n23]

### Forgiveness, turning, and responsibility

Our English words bring other associations into the question of what forgiveness means. In the Lord’s Prayer, Matthew has “forgive us our debts” (6:12, NIV), while Luke begins “Forgive us our sins” and then uses language of indebtedness, as the NIV’s Greek-text footnote explains (11:4). The noun for forgiveness in Mark 3:29, *aphesis*, likewise belongs to the language of release and remission. Pardon, freedom from a burden, and the restoration of a relationship can illuminate one another without being the same thing. Divine forgiveness concerns a person’s relation to God; reconciliation between people also requires attention to what has happened between them, and restored trust cannot simply be pronounced into being. [^n24]

Jewish reflection joins seeking mercy to how we treat another person. Ben Sira, a Jewish teacher writing in Jerusalem in the early second century BCE, says, “Forgive your neighbor the wrong he has done” (Sirach 28:2, NRSVUE), then connects that act with asking forgiveness in prayer. Later rabbinic teaching distinguishes wrongs against God from wrongs against another person: the latter are not atoned for by the Day of Atonement alone, “until one has appeased one’s fellow” (Mishnah Yoma 8:9; translation supplied from Hebrew). A religious act does not simply discharge the responsibility to the person harmed. The same passage ends with Rabbi Akiva, a teacher of Roman-era Judea in the late first and early second centuries CE, addressing Israel: “Who purifies you? Your Father in heaven.” Divine care and the obligation to the injured person sound together. The hope of purification does not make the work of repair unnecessary, and the demand for repair does not exhaust what the tradition says about mercy. [^n25]

The possibility of turning remains a searching question within that inheritance: wrongdoing can become deeply established, and mercy cannot be understood without considering what a life has become. Later Jewish reflection includes severe accounts of hardening as well as invitations to return. [^n26]

### Interpretations over time

The possibility of changed understanding brings us back to the people in Mark. Their accusation precedes Jesus’s summons; verse 30 explains why he gives the warning. If they recognize that they have misunderstood what is happening, what becomes possible? Such recognition might open into repentance—a changed direction rather than only a revised opinion. Would that turning show the warning accomplishing its purpose, or how would it stand in relation to the judgment pronounced? Mark does not tell us how they respond. Christian interpreters have returned to these questions through different understandings of repentance, mercy, and judgment.

John Chrysostom asks whether even repentance would be ineffective, then writes, “For even this was forgiven upon repentance” (*Homily 41*, §5). He says some who made the accusation subsequently believed. Yet he also describes grave punishment, and his homily contains anti-Jewish polemic that interprets collective suffering punitively. His hopeful sentence belongs to that troubling larger account. He is a human interpreter whose insights and harmful judgments both require attention. [^n28]

Augustine of Hippo, bishop and theologian in late-fourth- and early-fifth-century North Africa, identifies the decisive condition with final impenitence: refusal of repentance maintained until death. For the living, however, he insists, “For we are not to despair of any” (*Sermon 71*, §21). The thirteenth-century theologian Thomas Aquinas takes up this reading as a warning against reaching that condition. He also distinguishes rejection of the means of pardon from a limit on an “all-powerful and merciful God” (*Summa theologiae*, II–II, q.14, aa.1, 3). Neither removes final judgment, but both leave reason to hesitate before declaring a living person unreachable. [^n29]

John Calvin, sixteenth-century Reformation theologian based in Geneva, draws the boundary around knowing rejection of recognized truth. Those who enter this condition, he says, “never have any desire towards repentance.” His reading is not that God refuses a repentant person; it is that repentance will not arise. [^n30]

The Catholic Catechism also takes refusal seriously, affirming “There are no limits to the mercy of God” (§1864) before describing rejection of repentance and possible final loss. The nineteenth-century Scottish writer and preacher George MacDonald presses further, refusing to regard the condition as “a fixed, a final condition” (*It Shall Not Be Forgiven*). Whether resistance can itself ultimately change is where these readings diverge. [^n31]

Each account brings something consequential to the saying: a distinction between ignorance and knowing rejection, a boundary at death, or trust in a further possibility of transformation. These are efforts to understand the warning within a larger vision of God and human life. A dictionary cannot decide between them, and an argument depends on what we bring to its premises. Interpretation involves attention to the particular saying, the life and teaching to which it belongs, and the experience through which mercy and responsibility become more than concepts.

### The warning and the work of hearing

Matthew’s parallel states that forgiveness will not come “either in this age or in the age to come” (12:32, NIV). This is a substantial reason for taking the warning’s finality seriously. A compassionate purpose can coexist with a conviction that the danger is irreversible. That reading deserves an answer, not a diagnosis of closed-mindedness. Equally, hope for transformation cannot simply insert “only while the refusal continues” into the sentence. The words retain their force even as we continue to ask how they address a human life. [^n32]

Mark 4 takes us further into the question of hearing. After the parable of the sower, the Twelve and others around Jesus ask about the parables. He speaks of the secret of God’s kingdom being given to them and of those outside receiving everything in parables. Then, with the words “so that,” he brings an ancient voice into the exchange:

> “they may be ever seeing but never perceiving,
> and ever hearing but never understanding;
> otherwise they might turn and be forgiven!”
>
> — Mark 4:12, NIV, drawing on Isaiah 6:9–10. [^n33]

Jesus is speaking within a stream of scriptural memory and interpretation. Isaiah’s words belong to a prophetic commission in which the failure to hear and the threat of judgment are grave realities. Now they are spoken again, among people asking about a teaching. What might it mean to hear them there? Does “otherwise” describe an exclusion, expose the condition of hearing without understanding, or press upon the hearers the possibility of turning? What might compassion be doing through such a quotation—not outside its severity, but within it?

The same chapter places a lamp where its light can be seen and speaks of hidden things being brought into the open. It also describes Jesus teaching through parables in relation to what people could receive, while continuing to explain them to his disciples. Those closest to him do not simply possess understanding; they ask, listen, and need help. Parabolic teaching can both reveal and expose an inability to hear. Might the quotation from Isaiah bring this condition before the hearers so that they encounter it in themselves, rather than only recognize it in someone else?

There is a familiar Buddhist image for this difference. The *Dhammapada* compares long association with a wise person without understanding to a spoon that cannot taste the soup; the next verse compares swift understanding to a tongue that can. Closeness to a teaching and realization of it are not the same. The image need not become another division of people into fixed kinds. It can ask what our own familiarity has allowed us to understand—and what we still have not tasted. [^n64]

In Mark’s image of the growing seed, the sower sleeps and rises while growth takes place beyond his understanding (4:26–29). We can attend, question, practice, and help create conditions for understanding, but can we command the moment in which a teaching becomes clear? Willingness matters without being an explanation of everything that opens a heart. If compassion is our ground, perhaps the question becomes how teaching can nourish that opening, including through words that confront us. Mark’s language of concealment does not disappear. It remains within this inquiry into why a teaching reaches one person, seems not to reach another, and may be heard differently at another time.

Reason can carry us into this tension. It can test what follows from the words, expose hidden premises, and keep us from treating preference as interpretation. But it need not force every religious utterance to operate on one level of description or yield an abstract system free of paradox. We bring language study, reason, history, experience, and contemplative insight together in seeking a meaningful, practicable understanding. A seeming contradiction is not permission to make the text mean anything; it is sometimes the place where our first categories prove too narrow.

What changes when we hear the saying as an address is not necessarily its truth, but our position in relation to it. Before it becomes an answer about someone else’s destiny, it can become a question about our own way of seeing. We need not settle everything about eternity before allowing the words to reach the part of our lives that has not yet learned to listen.

## 4. When certainty closes the heart

### Clinging to a view, looking deeply

Clinging to a view is not the same as reasoning carefully or holding a conviction. The danger begins when keeping a judgment becomes more important than discovering what is true. The scribes’ description of Jesus’s work gives that danger a concrete form: liberation is named evil and becomes something to resist rather than receive. Dogmatism gives this attachment a doctrinal form. We judge everything by the teaching as we have understood it, but no longer allow what happens, or what another person tells us, to question that understanding.

The Order of Interbeing places this danger within its First Mindfulness Training, which commits practitioners to transforming dogmatism and the violence it can produce, including attachment to Buddhist doctrines. The Second Training turns toward the practice through which understanding develops: “compassionate listening, deep looking, and letting go of notions.” It says, “Truth is found in life.” This is not an instruction to stop studying. It asks that study remain in contact with what happens within us and around us, rather than become an accumulation that leaves our way of seeing untouched. [^n34]

Deep looking can begin very concretely. What did I hear this person say, and what did I assume they meant? What fear or loyalty makes one explanation easy to accept and another hard to consider? What happens to the people affected when I act on my view? We do not answer by announcing that every view is equally true. We stay with the words, the encounter, and its effects long enough to notice something our first understanding missed. An insight matters because it can change how we listen and what we do.

Some Buddhist scripture works directly with the limits of fixed designation. In Thích Nhất Hạnh’s translation of the *Diamond Sutra*, the bodhisattva’s commitment is “we must lead all these beings to nirvana”; yet the passage also says, “we do not, in truth, think that a single being has been liberated.” The instruction to liberate beings is not withdrawn. What is questioned is the grasping of a separate self, person, or living being. Words that guide compassionate action can also become fixed notions that obstruct understanding. The teaching asks us neither to abandon the work nor to make its language immune to inquiry. [^n63]

Mark 3 is not constructed in this form as a deliberate paradox. Its tension arises from a concrete framing and warning. However, the *Diamond Sutra* helps name a discipline that is important here: reasoning can bring us toward understanding, distinguish the levels at which our words are working, and show where a fixed conceptual frame fails. Insight is not an escape from reasoning; it is what can become possible when reason and contemplation reveal the limits of the notions with which we began.

The Babylonian Talmud gives openness another concrete discipline. Its account of disagreement between the schools of Hillel and Shammai recognizes both as expressing words of the living God, yet directs practice according to Hillel’s school. The explanation concerns how that school taught: its members studied both positions and “put the words of the House of Shammai before their own” (Eruvin 13b; translation supplied here). The other voice was given a place within their own teaching. A ruling could still be made without requiring that voice to disappear. Can we understand an account from within before deciding what to accept or reject? [^n61]

Mark directs the concern about failed recognition toward the disciples too. Jesus asks them, “Are your hearts hardened?” and “[D]on’t you remember?” (8:17–18, NIV), then continues to teach them. Later they try to stop someone driving out demons in Jesus’s name because “he was not one of us”; Jesus answers, “Do not stop him” (9:38–39, NIV). Attachment to belonging threatens to obstruct the work they claim to serve. Difficulty in recognizing what is good belongs within the circle of disciples as well as beyond it. [^n35]

The disciples’ misunderstandings prevent us from assigning failed recognition only to those who challenge Jesus. Those closest to him also struggle to understand. The scribes, the disciples, and the members of his family meet what is happening through different histories and commitments; no single response gives a complete account of any of them.

### Paul and changed seeing

Paul’s life gives the possibility of change particular weight. He recalls, “I persecuted the church of God and tried to destroy it,” before describing God’s revelation of the Son (Galatians 1:13–16, NIV). Acts tells the change through blindness, restored sight, and Ananias’s words: “you may see again and be filled with the Holy Spirit” (9:17, NIV). The person who had worked to destroy the community comes to recognize what he had opposed. [^n36]

First Timothy qualifies its account of mercy: “I acted in ignorance and unbelief” (1:13, NIV). This canonical portrayal has disputed authorship and should not simply be merged with Paul’s own account in Galatians or with Acts’ narrative. The distinction between ignorance and knowing rejection matters, and none of these passages establishes that Paul made the particular accusation in Mark. His life does not cancel the warning. It does prevent destructive religious opposition from becoming, by itself, proof that a person cannot change. Paul’s own severe words against a different gospel also keep him from becoming a simple emblem of openness: transformation does not remove every hard boundary from a person’s convictions. [^n37]

Nor does receptivity have to begin as an achievement a person produces alone. An encounter can interrupt the understanding through which everything else has been interpreted. Paul attributes his change of direction to God’s revelation of the Son; Acts connects restored sight with the gift of the Spirit. “If we refuse to see, we do not see” remains a searching formulation, but refusal need not be the last thing that happens to us. What becomes possible when we encounter what our settled understanding could not receive?

That question concerns transformation, not automatic absolution. Changed perception does not erase harm, establish divine forgiveness, or oblige another person to restore trust. A changing person can bear a lasting consequence. Responsibility and the possibility of becoming different remain together.

### The warning turns toward its interpreters

Clinging to a view can also shape Christian interpretation. We can defend an account of goodness while refusing to hear those harmed by its application. Even forgiveness can be used this way when it requires an injured person to stop speaking before responsibility has been acknowledged. A boundary against further harm is not closed-heartedness, and reluctance to trust a teacher’s claimed healing is not blasphemy.

The Gospel’s own test of profession is demanding. Matthew warns about those who say “Lord, Lord” (7:21, NIV), including people who claim powerful deeds in Jesus’s name. In the judgment scene of chapter 25, the king recalls being fed, welcomed, clothed, and visited in sickness and prison. The people he commends are surprised: when did they see him in such need? His answer brings recognition into the acts of care themselves:

> “whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers and sisters of mine, you did for me.”
>
> — Matthew 25:40, NIV. [^n38]

Those who failed to offer such care also ask when they saw him. The scene ends with eternal punishment and eternal life, not merely approval of kindness. The identity of those called the least has been interpreted in different ways, but the narrative makes one demand unmistakable: profession does not replace a response to the life before us. Recognition may become apparent in what we have done, or failed to do, rather than in the confidence of our religious claims.

Christian anti-Judaism makes the stakes historical as well as personal. In its account of the German churches under Nazism, the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum records that “traditional interpretations of religious scriptures seemed to support these prejudices.” Inherited Christian teaching, Nazi racial ideology, state power, and different forms of complicity and resistance belong to this history. Christian teaching was not its single cause, but its part cannot be omitted. Nor can the suffering of Jewish people be reduced to a lesson about someone else’s spiritual growth. [^n39]

The responsibility includes questioning interpretations that enabled people and institutions to disregard Jewish lives. It also includes correction: the Second Vatican Council’s declaration *Nostra aetate* §4 states that Jews “should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God.” The declaration does not undo earlier injury, but shows that authoritative teaching can restrain harm as well as be used to sustain it. The problem is not simply that there is a doctrine. It is whether that doctrine and its application remain answerable to the truth and human dignity they claim to serve. [^n40]

The antidote to dogmatism is therefore not indifference. It includes study, prayer, listening, concrete care, and willingness to change what we do when harm becomes visible. It also leaves room for honest disagreement. Another person’s refusal to accept our interpretation does not establish that their heart is closed. The same test returns upon a compassionate reading of Mark: if we dismiss every difficult verse or contrary interpretation as a failure of compassion, we have protected our own view from the encounter we ask of others.

## 5. Humanity without distance from the divine

The call to look again does not stop with those whom Jesus addresses. It also concerns how we see Jesus himself. Reverence can bring us close to a life, but it can also place that life at such a distance that we no longer hear a person speaking to other people. Then a warning becomes a timeless classification before we have attended to the act of address. Recovering the human encounter does not weaken the words; it restores the relation in which their force is first felt.

### A human teacher, a human transmission

The teaching reaches us through lives as well as words. Matthew retains the exchange about Jesus’s power; Luke places the warning beside the promise that “the Holy Spirit will teach you at that time what you should say” (12:12, NIV). The different settings guide different acts of understanding. *Dei verbum*, the Second Vatican Council’s constitution on divine revelation, recognizes the evangelists as “selecting some things from the many which had been handed on” (§19), while affirming the truth of the Gospels. Interpretation already participates in how the tradition is given, not simply in what later readers add. [^n41]

Jesus’s humanity belongs within this stream. Luke says that he “grew in wisdom” (2:52, NIV); John shows him “tired as he was from the journey” (4:6, NIV). Mark gives us prayer, anger, grief, questions, and encounters under pressure. These are not incidental concessions to a human appearance. They are part of the life through which his teaching is encountered. A human teacher speaks to particular hearers, with words that may console in one moment and confront in another. [^n42]

To attend to this humanity is not to take something from his divinity. The Catholic Catechism itself holds together “true God and true man” (§464), and, quoting the Second Vatican Council, affirms his human thought and says that “with a human heart he loved” (§470). That tradition makes claims beyond describing a divinely inspired teacher, but it also gives genuine humanity a central place. Human thought, love, and relationship are not what must be removed before the divine can be encountered. Taking incarnation seriously brings us toward the particular person speaking under accusation, not away from him into an account of truth without a human voice. [^n43]

### The only child and the whole world

The language of divine sonship can seem to place Jesus at an unreachable distance. John calls him God’s “one and only Son” (3:16, NIV), yet also speaks of those who receive him becoming “children of God” (1:12, NIV). These statements are not identical, but neither is the second insignificant. The Gospel’s particular claim about Jesus accompanies an invitation into relationship with God. [^n44]

The word *monogenēs*, traditionally translated “only begotten,” identifies the only or unique one in a relationship; in John 3:16 it refers to Jesus. Alongside that particular claim, the image of an only child can open another question about love. Could each person be loved *as if an only child*, with a care that does not treat anyone as expendable? This is a contemplative extension of the image, not an account of where John’s expression historically came from. It asks about the quality of a love that the same verse extends toward the world. An only child is not loved as one interchangeable instance of a category. The comparison invites care that sees a particular life, even when that care must confront what the person is doing.

The Buddhist *Discourse on Love* makes a related movement. In Thích Nhất Hạnh’s translation, the example is “[j]ust as a mother loves and protects her only child at the risk of her own life.” Such wholehearted loving-kindness is to be extended to all beings. Matthew’s call to “love your enemies” (5:44, NIV), grounded in the Father’s care for the evil and the good, offers a Christian point of encounter. Neither passage makes harmful conduct harmless. Both unsettle the division of care according to who belongs, who agrees, or who has become difficult to love. [^n45]

The people addressed in Mark remain within that field of care. Their accusation is serious, and Jesus can confront it without their humanity disappearing. His concern need not depend on their agreement. The question is not whether their words matter, but whether those words become all that can be seen of them.

### A person is more than a description

A statement about what someone has done can become the final account of who they are. This is not a choice between logic and insight. Careful reasoning can expose the hidden step from “this person did harm” to “this is all this person can ever be”; insight asks whether the description we are using is adequate to the living person before us. A truthful account of harmful actions remains necessary without becoming an exhaustive account of a life. [^n46]

Thích Nhất Hạnh approaches historical and ultimate dimensions through the image of wave and water. The wave’s arising and passing are not denied, but neither do they exhaust what the wave is. He cautions that “interbeing does not mean that everything is one or that everything is different.” This invites attention to two forms of closure: identifying someone completely with a past act, or imagining that transformation produces an unrelated person with no responsibility for it. In relation to Mark, this does not establish that an eternal consequence is impossible. It helps us distinguish a person’s act, the person who remains answerable for it, and the possibility of a different response. None should be made to disappear into the others. [^n47]

### What lasts, and what changes?

The question of a different response brings time back into view. What does it mean for something to last? A warning is spoken in the present to people whose earlier judgments are shaping what they can receive. Those judgments may continue through repeated words, habits of fear, or the influence they have on others. If understanding changes, something else can grow from the same history. Continuation need not mean that an action and all its effects remain unchanged.

Plum Village teaching approaches this through the expression “neither the same nor different.” A later response arises through what preceded it without being a repetition of it, or the act of an unrelated person. In this frame, karma concerns action and its consequences, not an indelible substance carried intact through time. The question is how actions continue through changing conditions, and how new understanding enters those conditions. What is preserved, what is transformed, and what remains to be cared for? [^n67]

Jewish Passover practice makes a past deliverance part of present identity and obligation. Mishnah Pesachim requires each person, in every generation, to regard themselves “as though they had come out of Egypt” (10:5; translation supplied from Hebrew). This later rabbinic formulation develops the first-person language of Exodus 13:8. The event is not simply over and absent; remembrance enters how life is lived now. [^n48]

Augustine offers a different but related attention to time in his *Confessions*: “a present of things past, a present of things present, and a present of things future” (XI.20.26). He locates these in memory, present awareness, and expectation. His attention to lived time coexists with his understanding of final impenitence: the same thinker still places a decisive boundary at death. [^n49]

John’s Gospel speaks of someone who already “has eternal life,” while retaining a future resurrection and judgment (5:24–29, NIV). In another image, a grain of wheat bears fruit through losing the form in which it stood alone (12:24). Within John this speaks of Jesus’s death and the life it makes possible. We need not gather these different accounts into a single philosophy to notice their attention to life that is received, shared, and transformed, not only prolonged. [^n50]

The relation to a past action can change as well. Ezekiel says that when a person turns from wrongdoing, none of the former sins “will be remembered against them” (18:22, NIV). The Qur’an describes those who repent, believe, and do good as people “whose evil deeds Allah will change into good deeds” (25:70, translated by Mustafa Khattab). These texts speak of divine mercy, not a Buddhist account of karma. Yet each makes us ask what it means to carry a past without being confined to repeating it. The claim is not that the harm never happened, but that it need not exhaust the relation between the person, God, and the life still to be lived. [^n66]

Sister Annabel Laity (True Virtue), a British-born Buddhist nun and teacher in the Plum Village tradition, connects attention to time with regret and present responsibility: “The three times inter-are.” Past action continues in its consequences; how we respond now helps form what comes next. Her discussion of regret turns toward acting differently in the present, not making the past unhappen. Transformation can mean entering responsibility more fully. [^n51]

The relation among the times matters alongside their duration. A past act is not changed into something that never happened, yet its continuation can involve remorse, repair, renewed harm, or a different way of living. These comparisons do not translate Mark’s “never” away. They bring us closer to the lives about which we are speaking when we ask what lasts, what changes, and what responsibility asks of us now. [^n52]

### Compassion that is not merely reassurance

How we speak to someone becomes part of what happens next. A warning does not enter an empty space; it enters a life already shaped by fear, conviction, suffering, and hope. Its truth matters, and so do the way it is offered and the response it makes possible. The question is now close to the original encounter: can severe speech open a way toward recognition without reducing the hearer to the judgment being challenged?

Freedom of thought need not leave us silent before a harmful view. The Third Mindfulness Training of the Order of Interbeing joins respect for another person’s freedom with helping to transform fanaticism and narrowness through “loving speech and compassionate dialogue.” Its commitment “not to force others” is not a commitment never to challenge them. The distinction concerns whether speech helps understanding become possible or demands submission to the speaker. [^n53]

The Qur’an places a discipline around religious address as well. The invitation is to be offered with “wisdom and kind advice,” and disagreement conducted “in the best manner” (16:125, translated by Mustafa Khattab). The verse ends by locating the fullest knowledge of who is rightly guided with God. Conviction does not relieve the speaker of responsibility for how another person is addressed, nor does it make the speaker’s judgment exhaustive. [^n65]

An authentic warning draws attention to a real danger. It may need to be urgent, and its discomfort does not make it coercive. The Buddhist discourse to Prince Abhaya similarly describes speech that is “factual, true, beneficial, but unendearing & disagreeable to others,” with attention to the proper time for speaking it (*Majjhima Nikāya* 58, translated by Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu). That discourse, too, arises around a severe judgment. Compassion is not identified simply with what sounds reassuring. [^n54]

Heard in this light, Jesus’s warning may belong to compassion in action. Compassion need not mean continuing an exchange on terms that allow harm to go unchallenged. Sometimes it calls for an interruption, a boundary, or a refusal to let another person’s account of events become the only one that can be heard. The care involved includes people affected by the exchange, not only the person whose words are challenged.

Whenever we speak from spiritual, moral, or institutional authority, our intention is only part of what matters. We must also attend to what is happening, to the person being addressed, and to the effects of our words. Dialogue is not always possible or wise. A person facing threats or coercion may need distance, support, or a firm boundary instead. Nonviolence asks how harm may be resisted without being reproduced, including how to protect the space in which others can speak and be heard.

### Remaining in the teaching

Contemplative engagement carries attention into the act of interpretation. The question includes the one asking it. What needs to change may be not only a proposed answer, but how we listen, speak, and respond to someone we had already decided we understood. The warning can touch our own attachment rather than remain a means of classifying other people. [^n55]

It can also ask us, imaginatively and carefully, to stand in more than one place in the scene: among the crowd who may be influenced, with the disciples learning how to recognize what is good, with the scribes whose judgment is being challenged, and with Jesus speaking under accusation. This does not pretend access to anyone’s private mind. It prevents us from remaining safely outside the encounter while deciding what it means for everyone else.

John gives this relation between teaching and lived understanding a direct expression:

> “If you hold to my teaching, you are really my disciples. Then you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free.”
>
> — John 8:31–32, NIV. [^n56]

The verb *menō* means to remain or abide. Knowing is joined to remaining in the word, not simply to possessing a settled explanation of it. In John’s setting, the freedom concerns sin and the liberating work of the Son. The invitation has a practical depth: forgiveness, love, and repentance become known through the lives in which they are practiced. Study and inherited teaching belong to that process; so do the encounters that reveal where our understanding has not yet become action.

John also speaks of “whoever lives by the truth” coming into the light (3:21, NIV). The Greek phrase is ποιῶν τὴν ἀλήθειαν, *poiōn tēn alētheian*: literally, “doing the truth.” Truth is not only something affirmed; it is enacted. An interpretation of mercy must become more than a persuasive account of mercy. Its meaning appears in whether we can tell the truth about harm, care for those affected, and remain willing to learn. [^n57]

## 6. The invitation before us

Returning to Mark 3, we hear a sharp wake-up call. The breadth of forgiveness gives the warning its startling force. Jesus speaks to people who have called the work of liberation evil, and the language of judgment confronts their own judgment. The warning brings the danger into the present encounter: while healing is treated as an enemy, it cannot be welcomed as healing. Its force, on this hearing, lies both in calling people to recognize what they are turning away from and in interrupting a judgment that others might begin to accept. Compassion may take the form of that interruption: care for those speaking, and care for those whose lives their words could affect.

The question of “eternal sin” remains, and its unresolved tension can continue to work on us. The Greek is emphatic, other Gospel passages sustain its severity, and interpreters have not reached a common answer. A compassionate purpose does not settle those differences. Yet uncertainty about the meaning of finality need not suspend the work of seeing, repentance, and responsibility. When understanding changes, the suffering already caused still calls for acknowledgment and whatever repair is possible; a new beginning is not an erased past.

Humanizing Jesus does not require making him ordinary in the sense of insignificant. It means allowing his teaching to arise within a human life, grounded in time, place, culture, relationships, conflicts, grief, courage, and love. His Jewish inheritance belongs to that humanity, as do the people who shared it and understood it differently. The disciples, religious teachers, evangelists, and later interpreters belong to that arc of human experience. Reverence can bring us closer to these lives rather than place them beyond understanding.

An understanding of forgiveness can remain generous in its claims while becoming anemic in practice. We can defend mercy and fail to hear someone asking for accountability; we can defend truth while refusing an encounter that might deepen our understanding of it. The question is not whether we have found a formulation that no longer troubles us. It is whether we can remain with the teaching in a way that changes how we live. Whom have we already decided we understand—perhaps ourselves—and what might become possible if we listened again?
